## ABSTRACT 

Image-to-text jailbreaks place harmful intent in text, image content, or the relation-ship between them. We examine image-side factors across four published attack families on a 313-prompt StrongREJECT slice, using five multimodal models and an additional appendix evaluation of InternVL3.5-8B. The harmful instruction is held constant across conditions; the baseline matrix uses one draw per prompt, and paired ablations use three draws with an automated rubric judge. A bare harmful query, with or without a benign unrelated image, produces little attack success on most victims, while attack images substantially increase it. First, per-tile en-tropy and JPEG size do not reliably distinguish attack tiles from size-matched benign distractors, limiting density-only screening. Second, earlier tile-count lad-ders were confounded by payload visibility. A corrected region-count test found no detectable effect, so the role of tile-count structure remains unresolved. Third, on Qwen3-VL-8B, the E4 manipulation that removes query-specific relatedness lowers ASR by about 0.12. This supports a bounded attribution to the relatedness manipulation, although image-text congruence remains unmeasured. A within-category control reproduces the direction on overlapping stimuli. Five tests sur-vive the global statistical correction, but only E4 supports attribution to one mea-sured descriptor; the within-category result is a robustness check, not a separate attribution. These conclusions remain conditional on the rubric judge. New code will be made available at https://github.com/BoyuanChen99/Which_Image_ Property_Carries_The_Jailbreak .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) process visual inputs alongside safety-aligned lan-guage. An image can carry harmful intent through embedded text, spatial layout, depicted content, or its relationship to the accompanying query. Image-to-text (I2T) jailbreaks exploit these channels in different ways (Gong et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a; Chen et al., 2025); their performance is often summarized by attack success rate (ASR) on benchmarks such as MMJ-Bench (Zhao et al., 2025) and JailBreakV-28K (Luo et al., 2024). Aggregate scores, however, do not reveal which image property contributes to a safety failure. Prior work points to dif-ferent explanations: CS-DJ (Yang et al., 2025) emphasizes sub-image complexity, VisCo (Li et al., 2025a) uses fabricated image-grounded context, and FigStep (Gong et al., 2025) and MML (Wang et al., 2025) use typographic images with little compositional structure. Because each attack changes several image properties at once, comparisons across attacks cannot distinguish these explanations. This matters for defense because density filters, semantic-consistency checks, and interventions in the fusion stack target different potential causes. We use existing attacks as diagnostic probes and describe their images along four axes. (1) Com-positional complexity captures visual structure, from a single typographic canvas to a multi-tile composite, independent of whether the image relates to the query. (2) Information density cap-tures low-level image information, measured separately by pixel entropy and JPEG compressibility. These descriptors are not interchangeable and do not measure structure. (3) Tile provenance records whether composite tiles come from natural photographs or synthetic generation. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 IMAGE -TO -T EXT JAILBREAK ATTACKS 

Recent work shows that visual inputs can bypass safety mechanisms in multimodal language models. 

Typographic and structured prompts. FigStep (Gong et al., 2025) renders harmful queries as text in an image. MML (Wang et al., 2025) adds transformations such as mirroring, rotation, and Base64 encoding with a decryption cue. FC-Attack (Chen et al., 2025) renders instructions as flowcharts but relies on a trained generator, making it less comparable to our black-box setting. Semantically grounded context. VisCo (Li et al., 2025a) creates image-grounded scenarios, VisCRA (Sima et al., 2025) uses visual reasoning to infer masked content, and VRSA (Xiong et al., 2025) distributes harmful intent across coherent sub-images. Distraction and visual complexity. CS-DJ (Yang et al., 2025) decomposes a query into sub-queries shown in contrasting multi-tile composites, arguing that compositional complexity distracts safety processing. VoTA (Zhang et al., 2025) combines benign images into an aggregate-harmful narrative. Optimization-based attacks. Gradient-based and adversarial-noise attacks (Qi et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a) can achieve high ASR but require more model access than single-turn black-box interaction; we therefore treat them as upper-bound references, not as part of our controlled setting. HADES (Li et al., 2024) embeds and amplifies harmful content in an image, reporting 20–40 percentage-point increases in harmful responses across models. This links image content to attack success, but HADES also changes the text request, so it cannot separate the image’s contribution from the wording. Our E4 derangement keeps the instruction byte-identical and reassigns generated images across queries, directly testing the effect of query-specific relatedness. 2.2 BENCHMARKS AND EVALUATION PROTOCOLS 

MM-SafetyBench (Liu et al., 2024) shows that MLLMs can be compromised by query-relevant images, making it a close precedent for our alignment ( S) axis. However, it pairs each harmful text with a single image whose density, provenance, and content vary together, so it cannot distin-guish semantic relatedness from the presence of a harmful scene. Our E4 derangement keeps the instruction and scene set fixed, then reassigns scenes across queries to test relatedness while holding measured density and provenance constant. JailBreakV-28K (Luo et al., 2024) reports that image type affects attack success, motivating our separate analysis of image properties. These benchmarks compare models; our paired tests examine how controlled changes in image construction relate to ASR, following diagnostic work such as Tong et al. (2024). 2.3 RELATION TO PROMPT -L EVEL AXIS FRAMEWORKS 

Our four-axis framework is related to Li et al.’s on-topicness and OOD-intensity analysis (Li et al., 2025b), which defines axes over textual prompts and uses their trade-off to build a prompt-rewriting attack. We instead vary image construction while keeping the harmful instruction byte-identical. 3This design asks how the image carrier contributes to ASR when the text is fixed; it complements, rather than generalizes, analyses that change the prompt itself. 2.4 MULTIMODAL SAFETY AND DEFENSE 

Safety alignment commonly uses instruction tuning, RLHF, preference optimization, and prompt-level safeguards (Bai et al., 2022). Multimodal systems must also account for harmful image content, embedded text, spatial layout, and cross-modal context. VLGuard (Zong et al., 2024) safety-fine-tunes a vision-language model on a filtered dataset and reports weaker safety alignment through the vision modality. This offers one possible explanation for why an off-topic harmful scene might sup-press compliance with the attached request, but our experiments do not test that mechanism directly. AdaShield (Wang et al., 2024b) prepends safety prompts to address structure-based jailbreaks, while Llama Guard (3 Vision) (Inan et al., 2023) classifies prompt and response safety. We use the Stron-gREJECT rubric judge (Section 3.5) and audit it against human labels (Appendix A.15), since ASR depends on distinguishing refusal, partial compliance, and harmful completion. 

## 3 METHODOLOGY 

We describe jailbreak images along four interpretable axes and use them to organize attack families and paired ablations. Each ablation controls image properties where its construction permits; when multiple measured properties change, we do not attribute the result to a single axis. This section de-fines the taxonomy and the manipulation-check, judging, and statistical protocols. Model identifiers, dataset slices, and sampling parameters appear in Section 4. 3.1 THREAT MODEL 

We study single-turn black-box image-to-text (I2T) jailbreaks. An attack maps a harmful query q to a pair (I, t ) of an attack image I and a benign textual wrapper t; the pair is submitted to a victim MLLM M as one user turn and the response r = M(I, t ) is scored by an automatic judge J (q, r ) ∈

[0 , 1] . Concretely, J is the official strongreject_rubric evaluator (Souly et al., 2024), backed by GPT-4 O-MINI (OpenAI, 2024b), which emits a binary refusal and two {1, . . . , 5} ratings spec . and 

conv .; the continuous score follows the StrongREJECT formula J (q, r ) = (1 −refusal) · spec .+conv . 

> 2

,linearly rescaled to [0 , 1] , and the attack succeeds when J (q, r ) ≥ τ with τ =0 .5. Attackers freely construct I and t but have no access to weights, gradients, or token probabilities. 3.2 TAXONOMY 

Each attack image is characterized along four axes: compositional complexity, information density (split into entropy and compression), tile provenance, and semantic alignment. 

Compositional complexity ( Ccomp ). The amount of visual structure the image carries, indepen-dent of whether that structure relates to q. Typographic jailbreaks that render the query on a single canvas (Gong et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025) sit at the low end; composite attacks that tile multiple sub-images (Yang et al., 2025) at the high end. Equivalently, it is the number of tiles that localize sep-arate visual regions. The word “complexity” in the attack literature tends to conflate this construct with information density; we name the two explicitly and treat them as two first-class axes. 

Information density ( Cinfo ). The amount of low-level information in an image, independent of its layout. We measure it with pixel entropy ( Cinfo ,ent ) and JPEG@85 byte size, a measure of compress-ibility ( Cinfo ,compr ). The two descriptors are not interchangeable and sometimes order conditions differently (Section A.30). We designate JPEG@85 as the primary descriptor for the attribution rule because it directly measures compressibility; entropy provides a complementary measure. The screening experiment evaluates both descriptors individually and together (Appendix A.22). Nei-ther measures compositional complexity: images with the same tile count can differ in information density. 

Semantic alignment ( S). The degree to which the visible content of the image is topically related to the query q. A landscape paired with a chemistry query has low S; an image depicting the chemistry scenario described in q has high S. S is defined relative to q, so the same image can be high-S for one query and low-S for another. 

Tile provenance ( P ). Tile provenance ( P ) records where the tiles in a composite come from, such as natural photographs or synthetic generation. On Gemma-3-4B, the Experiment 3 cells sort descriptively by provenance, but they also differ in entropy, JPEG size, and alignment (Section A.30). Under our attribution rule, this pattern is jointly attributed rather than an isolated provenance effect (Appendix A.21). We treat the four attack families (five configurations) as diagnostic probes rather than the object of study: FigStep and the MML variants occupy the low-Ccomp region, CS-DJ the high-Ccomp 

low-S region, and VisCo a high-density single-image scaffold (full placement detail and the three descriptive image-side metrics in Appendix A.34). 3.4 PAIRED ABLATIONS 

Comparisons across the five baseline attacks cannot distinguish the effects of complexity and align-ment because each attack changes several image properties. We therefore construct two paired ablations that keep the harmful instruction byte-identical and measure image-side properties before querying the victims. These checks identify which descriptors change, but do not guarantee that every contrast isolates one axis (constructions in Appendix A.46). 

Complexity ablation (Exp. 2). From a single fixed FigStep typographic tile we build three stimuli, a padded surround, a random-natural-image distractor composite, and a query-aligned generated-scene composite ( Ccomp rising at roughly constant payload). 

Alignment ablation (Exp. 3). Starting from the CS-DJ 3 × 4 composite, we hold the layout and three sub-query tiles fixed while varying the nine distractor tiles among noise, unrelated natural im-ages, and query-aligned generated scenes. The manipulation changes several measured descriptors, including tile provenance, so it does not isolate S. The E4 control instead holds generated-scene provenance fixed while changing query-specific relatedness (Appendix A.46). 3.5 ATTRIBUTION RULE 

We assign an ASR difference to one descriptor only when it is the only measured property that changes between two conditions. Otherwise, we label the contrast jointly attributed and do not as-sign its magnitude to either descriptor alone. E4 provides one such contrast among the measured descriptors: entropy, JPEG size, tile count, and provenance are held fixed while the scene-to-query relation changes. E4 cannot distinguish topical relatedness from image-text congruence, which we 5do not measure. Five tests pass the global statistical correction, but only E4 supports a single-descriptor attribution. The within-category harm-salience test is a robustness check on overlapping stimuli, not an independent attribution. No compositional rung cleanly isolates Ccomp . Appen-dices A.35 and A.36 give the full rule and worked examples. Paired ablations use the within-prompt mean of three draws and a permutation test at p < 0.05 ; we also report bootstrap 95% CIs and Holm-corrected paired McNemar tests. We adopted this sampler after observing single-draw results, so it is a reporting standard rather than an outcome-independent preregistration. Results without three draws per prompt, including the baseline matrix, API rows, and CS-DJ ladders, are labeled single-draw and descriptive.

## 6 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

Five contrasts pass the global statistical correction, but only one supports attribution to a single measured descriptor under our rule. On Qwen3-VL-8B, the E4 manipulation that removes query-specific relatedness lowers ASR by 0.119 , and a within-category control reproduces the direction on overlapping stimuli. This is a bounded result for one victim and one manipulation; image-text congruence remains unmeasured. It does not establish a general semantic-alignment mechanism or resolve the different emphases of CS-DJ and VisCo. The region-count test finds no detectable effect of distractor diversity, while the earlier tile-count ladders are confounded by payload visibility. We therefore cannot conclude whether tile-count structure matters. The attention-mass probe finds that payload tiles receive disproportionate attention on Qwen3-VL-8B, but their attention share does not vary with ASR and does not explain the E4 result (Appendix A.41). 

Defensive implications. The pad-surround and content-fill rungs are among the larger detectable changes in our ablation and may motivate input screening or training for typographic-payload ro-bustness on the victims where they occur. The per-tile density screen does not reliably separate attack tiles from benign photo tiles, so a practical defense would need signals beyond the density descriptors measured here. Appendix A.48 discusses these implications and the need to evaluate defenses against adaptive adversaries. 

Methodological takeaway. Attack rankings alone cannot identify which image properties mat-ter. Controlled comparisons can help, provided the instruction remains fixed, image properties are measured before evaluation, and results are labeled as jointly attributed when multiple descrip-tors change. Applying this rule exposed payload-visibility confounds in two tile-count ladders and yielded one bounded attribution to query-specific relatedness on Qwen3-VL-8B. Image-text con-gruence remains unresolved. The same approach can help evaluate other attack families, including scaffold- and encoding-based attacks. 87 LIMITATIONS 

Our main findings come from open-weight victims with headroom: the E4 alignment result on QWEN 3-VL-8B and the padding, provenance, and ladder observations on G EMMA -3-4B. They should not be generalized to the full panel. Image-side measures such as pixel entropy, JPEG@85, and CLIP alignment are proxies. ASR is also judge-conditional: the rubric judge shares a model family with the GPT-4 O victim, and its results differ from a non-OpenAI judge by up to 0.18 .We therefore emphasize signs and orderings when effect magnitudes approach the roughly 0.10 

cross-judge disagreement band (Appendices A.15, A.4). The 0.19 padding-construction gap is a substantial unmodeled difference, but it is smaller than the aggregate multi-step ladder change; it limits conclusions about the four-axis taxonomy rather than demonstrating that it dominates all designed effects. The four axes organize our image constructions, but most comparisons do not isolate one property while holding every other image property fixed. E4 holds the measured entropy, compressibility, tile count, and provenance fixed while changing query-specific relatedness; it does not separate relatedness from image-text congruence, and no benign-paraphrase control was run. The region-count null tests distractor diversity, not tile count. Because we did not build a corrected tile-count ladder with payload text visible at every rung, whether tile-count structure matters remains untested. We do not claim a mechanism: the attention-mass probe is null on both effect-carrying victims (Appendix A.41), and the in-model relatedness probe is too noisy to discriminate (Appendix A.17). Several controls remain unrun (protocols in Appendix A.31): benign generated distractors to dis-tinguish query-relatedness from off-topic-harm suppression; a scene generated from a benign para-phrase of the query; and a relevant scene paired with a benign restatement to examine image-text congruence. The paired design’s minimum detectable effects are about 0.06 –0.10 (Table 12). An outcome-blind replication with 830 paired prompts would provide 80% power for an effect of 0.06 .

## ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS 

We evaluate published attack families and create paired image variants for diagnostic experiments. We release no new deployable exploit or fine-tuned attacker model. Harmful prompts come from the public StrongREJECT benchmark. The paper omits raw harmful completions, and the typographic payload in Figure 5 is blurred. The public evaluation archive contains judge scores but not raw response text; a separate, access-controlled archive contains selected stimuli and verbatim model outputs. We queried API-hosted victims under standard red-teaming terms and did not attempt to extract model weights or training data. The rubric judge was audited against human labels (agree-ment 0.800 , κ=0 .594 ; Appendix A.15). The audit found no judge-error interaction that changes the paper’s conclusions. Defenses based on these findings should be evaluated against adaptive adversaries before deployment.

A.32 DOSE -R ESPONSE DISCRIMINATING TEST (D EFERRED )

A discriminating test for the dose-response reading is deferred. The dose-response remains unresolved: the two prompt levels produced similar CLIP separations (level-2 sub-query separa-tion 0.054 and level-1 separation 0.058 ) and the two E4 estimates were +0 .061 and −0.042 , with overlapping confidence intervals. These results leave open whether the effect is weak or absent. The instrument that would discriminate the two readings removes the generator’s separation ceiling rather than pushing against it: a retrieval-based high-alignment condition that retrieves real images from a large index (e.g. a LAION subset or ImageNet) to maximize CLIP similarity to q, giving a real-image CLIP separation substantially above the 0.06 -0.10 the SD3.5 construction reaches. The discriminating observation is: if the effect remains flat despite a substantially larger CLIP separation, that would weigh against a dose-responsive alignment account; a larger effect would suggest that the earlier manipulation did not span enough of the proxy range. A retrieval test would be useful follow-up, but was not run in this study because the index and retrieval backend are not part of this paper’s released pipeline; the exact protocol is to (i) index a size-matched real-image pool by CLIP ViT-L/14 embedding, (ii) per StrongREJECT prompt retrieve the pool image with the highest whole-image CLIP cosine to q subject to not being the prompt’s own synthetic scene, (iii) build the E4 gen-unrelated cell by attaching a query B’s retrieved image to query A through the same fixed derangement as Appendix A.6, and (iv) re-run the Qwen3-VL-8B relevant-to-gen-unrelated paired contrast at the same n as the primary set, reporting the achieved whole-image CLIP separa-tion beside the effect so the dose axis is measured rather than assumed. Until it runs, we scope the alignment claim to orderings measured under the manipulation reach our construction provides. A.33 SALIENCE -S TRATIFIED READ OF THE E4 C ELL (E XPLORATORY )We stratify the E4 gen-unrelated cell on Qwen3-VL-8B by CLIP ViT-L/14 (the paper’s alignment proxy), using only already-cached composite images and the archived k=3 judged responses; no victim interaction and no new generation are involved. For each of the 313 prompts we compute 30 two whole-image similarities: relatedness = CLIP( I, q A), the similarity of the stimulus to its own harmful query, and a harm-salience proxy = CLIP( I, q B ), the similarity of the stimulus to the query the attached scene was actually generated for under the fixed derangement. Both are on the same scale and both use the same truncation the paper’s alignment metric uses. Regressing the per-prompt k=3 compliance (fraction of 3 draws ≥ rubric τ =0 .5) on the two standardized predictors gives a re-latedness coefficient of −0.003 (95% bootstrap CI [−0.027 , 0.021] ) and a harm-salience coefficient of −0.028 ( [−0.055 , −0.002] , weakly negative). We pre-stated the reading before computing it: the suppression hypothesis predicts lower compliance as harm-salience rises at fixed relatedness; the relatedness hypothesis predicts no salience effect once relatedness is controlled. The observed salience coefficient is negative and its CI just excludes zero, which is directionally consistent with a mild suppression component; the magnitude is small (about −0.03 in compliance per standard de-viation of salience) and the split by derangement category does not confirm it: same-category pairs (those whose attached scene belongs to the same StrongREJECT category, n=50 ) show higher com-pliance ( 0.167 ) than cross-category pairs ( n=263 , 0.103 ), a difference of −0.064 with a bootstrap CI ( [−0.157 , 0.021] ) that includes zero. The two strands therefore point in opposite directions on whether harm salience suppresses compliance, and both effects are small relative to the 0.119 rele-vance lift that the E4 control isolates on this victim. We report this as exploratory and observational; it does not close the incongruity confound, and the benign-paraphrase control (Section 7) remains the decisive instrument. A.34 ATTACK PLACEMENT AND IMAGE -S IDE METRICS 

Placement. We treat the four attack families (five configurations) in our panel as diagnostic probes rather than as the object of study. FigStep (Gong et al., 2025) and the MML variants (Wang et al., 2025) occupy the low-Ccomp region; their harmful instruction is embedded in typography itself. To a text-reading vision encoder, typography is legible but semantically thin, so these attacks have low semantic alignment to q in the natural-image sense. CS-DJ (Yang et al., 2025) decomposes q into sub-queries and surrounds them with contrasting natural distractors in a 3 × 4 composite, occupying the high-Ccomp low-S region. VisCo (Li et al., 2025a) fabricates a conversational scaffold whose single image is generated to match q; it has low compositional structure but high information density (Cinfo , pixel entropy ≈ 7.05 bits/px; Table 4). Under the compositional definition of Ccomp above it therefore sits at low structural complexity on the high-S axis, and its high pixel entropy is a Cinfo 

phenomenon rather than compositional structure; the two constructs are kept separate rather than conflated when reading its row. 

Image-side metrics. To move from qualitative placement to a measurable one, we compute three descriptive metrics on each stimulus before showing it to any victim. The first is the mean per-channel Shannon entropy of the RGB image, which captures information density at the pixel level. The second is the byte size of the image re-encoded as JPEG at quality 85 , which measures compress-ibility. Both are descriptive checks on information density, kept separate from the compositional definition of Ccomp in the taxonomy above. As an alignment proxy we use the cosine similarity between a CLIP ViT-L/14 image embedding of I and a CLIP text embedding of q (Radford et al., 2021). For composite stimuli in which a typographic payload dominates the whole-image CLIP score, we additionally report the alignment score over the non-payload tiles in isolation, so that the manipulated surround is not masked by the constant payload. These metrics are descriptive and used to verify that our manipulations move the intended axis; they are not the experimental factors themselves. A.35 ATTRIBUTION RULE AND THE DECISION RULE 

We commit to a decision rule for attributing an observed ASR change to a construct: a contrast is attributed to a descriptor only if it is the sole measured descriptor that changes between the two conditions; where two or more move together the contrast is jointly attributed and not counted toward either headline. The rule was fixed after data collection but before this paper’s attribution claims were written; it is an a-posteriori-consistent audit rule, not a pre-committed protocol. We disclose that the designation of the Exp. 3 ordered endpoint as primary versus exploratory was likewise chosen with the data in view: that single designator is the place where a pre-commitment would have mattered most, and we did not pre-commit to it. The rule operates on the manipulation matrix (Table 32), one row per condition, listing tile count Ccomp , pixel entropy and JPEG@85 size 

Cinfo , whole-image CLIP alignment S, tile provenance, and payload canvas fraction. Five contrasts pass the global correction (Table 22), but only E4 receives a descriptor-level attri-bution under this rule. On Qwen3-VL-8B, the relevant-to-gen-unrelated contrast lowers ASR by 31 0.119 (p < 0.001 ) while measured entropy, JPEG size, tile count, and provenance stay fixed. The contrast is attributed to query-specific relatedness; image-text congruence remains unmeasured. The within-category harm-salience control reproduces the direction on overlapping stimuli and is a ro-bustness check, not a second attribution. This is a bounded single-victim finding, not a general or fully specified semantic-alignment mechanism. The Experiment 3 noise-to-relevant endpoint re-mains exploratory because several descriptors move together, and no compositional rung cleanly isolates Ccomp .

A contrast between two conditions is attributed to a descriptor only if that de-scriptor is the sole measured descriptor that changes between them. Where two or more descriptors move together, the contrast is reported as jointly attributed 

and is not counted toward either component’s headline magnitude. Sign handling: a descriptor is a live confound of a contrast iff it (a) varies across the two con-ditions and (b) co-monotones with the observed ASR change; a descriptor that anti-monotones against ASR cannot explain away the effect and is excluded as a confound on the sign ground alone, regardless of how much it moves. A contrast closed by the E4 derangement control, which holds every measured descriptor fixed and varies only the image’s relation to its own query, is a single-descriptor contrast by construction.

For paired-ablation inference, we use the within-prompt k=3 mean and test it with the within-prompt permutation procedure described below. We apply this criterion to the Exp. 2 and Exp. 3/E4 ladders and the payload-scale control. The baseline matrix and API-victim evaluations remain single-draw, as noted in the main text. The CS-DJ compositional ladder is also single-draw by construction and is labeled accordingly in Table 1. Where a claim rests on a per-prompt effect, the paper applies a single criterion to every victim and every rung: the effect is accepted only if the within-prompt permutation test over per-prompt k=3 means is significant at p < 0.05 , and the marginal within-prompt discor-dance floor is treated as a descriptive quantity (the rate at which same-prompt draws disagree), not as an acceptance threshold. No effect estimate is compared with the discordance floor; an effect that does not clear the permutation p < 0.05 bound is reported as at the resolution limit regardless of where its magnitude sits relative to the descriptive floor. This criterion is applied identically to the Gemma-3-4B ladder rungs and to the Qwen3-VL-8B alignment effect. For each cell defined 32 by attack, condition, victim, and dataset we report a point estimate of ASR with a bias-corrected 

95% bootstrap confidence interval over 10 4 resamples of the prompt slice. For paired condition comparisons within each ablation we use a paired McNemar test on the binarized judge outcomes and report Holm-Bonferroni adjusted p-values across the family of within-experiment pairs, sepa-rately for each victim and dataset. Effect sizes are reported as ASR differences with their CIs, since odds ratios are unstable in the high-refusal regime that several API victims exhibit. Descriptive cells without a k=3 record (the baseline matrix, the API rows, and the CS-DJ compositional ladders) are each queried once per condition under the fixed sampling configuration of Section 4 (local models at temperature 0.7, top-p 0.9; API models at temperature 0.7 or the provider default), matching the single-shot interaction an attacker would observe in practice. Because the paper’s design varies one axis at a time within each victim, we control the family-wise error rate Holm-correction within 

victim within experiment (family of the three paired contrasts in each ablation); this is generous rel-ative to a global correction over the roughly thirty tests the paper runs, and we report raw p-values alongside Adjusted-p everywhere a borderline verdict depends on it (Table 7). Every ASR number in this paper is judge-conditional, and we say so plainly rather than argue the interaction away. Every headline cell and delta is produced by the rubric judge (GPT-4 O-MINI 

backend); the local finetuned StrongREJECT judge appears only in the E-2 sampling-variance floor (Appendix A.13), where it is deterministic (a softmax expected value) and where we re-judge the ladder’s margin-relevant cell with the rubric judge to keep the floor comparable to the headline cells. Attention-mass probe. One proposed explanation is that a fixed visual-token budget cre-ates competition between the typographic payload and distractor tiles. We tested this on Qwen3-VL-8B and Gemma-3-4B over the full n=313 Exp. 3 slice, normalizing attention mass by image tokens and tile area (scripts: scripts/r9/attention_mass_normalized.py and 

attention_mass_normalized_agg.py ). On Qwen, the payload receives more than its uni-form share ( 0.39 –0.47 , permutation p < 0.001 in every condition), but this share barely changes between relevant and gen-unrelated ( +0 .0017 ) or across the other conditions. The probe detects disproportionate payload attention on Qwen but finds no condition-level attention shift that explains the ASR pattern. We therefore do not use attention competition as a unifying mechanism; the region-count null, Gemma’s elevated noise-cell ASR, and the distractor-informativeness results require further study (Appendix A.25). The probe answers a limited question on these two victims, not the broader mechanism question. As with the other axes, the C × S frame-work organizes the tested constructions; it does not provide a causal recommendation where the design does not isolate a single factor. A.42 CONTEXT : T WO RELATED THREADS (F ULL READ )Our study connects to two recent lines of work on multimodal jailbreaks. Distraction is All You Need 

(Yang et al., 2025) compares content and complexity within CS-DJ and concludes that complexity is 34 the stronger driver. Our tests do not yield a general ordering that confirms this claim: the controlled region-count ladder is null, the earlier tile-count ladders are confounded by payload visibility, and the E4 relatedness effect appears only on Qwen3-VL-8B. Balanced Structural Decomposition (Li et al., 2025b) studies on-topicness and OOD intensity at the text-prompt level and uses their trade-off to create a prompt-rewriting attack. We instead hold the harmful text fixed and vary image construction. A concurrent study of typographic prompt injection (Balakrishnan et al., 2026) reports a relationship between text-image embedding distance and ASR. Our experiments do not reproduce that correlation directly: the CLIP proxy, image provenance, and other descriptors vary together in Experiment 3. The controlled E4 comparison identifies a bounded relatedness effect on one victim, but the different construction and limited scope do not establish whether the reported correlation generalizes. A.57 IMAGE -CONTRIBUTION CONTROL (E-1): FULL READING 

To anchor what the image itself contributes, we add two degenerate control conditions on the full StrongREJECT slice: the bare harmful query with no image ( text-only ) and the same query paired with a benign, unrelated natural image ( benign ), judged with the same rubric and pipeline as all other cells. We run these controls on the full panel (Table 31). The controls make the image contribution explicit. On the API reference victim (GPT-4o) the bare query reaches only 1.3% ASR, a benign unrelated image leaves it at 0.0% , and attaching a CS-DJ attack image lifts it to 28 .4% on the identical prompts. The same qualitative pattern holds across the panel: every text-only and benign cell is at or near zero, while the image-conditioned ASR in Table 2 reaches 0.13 –0.96 depending on victim and attack. Most tellingly, the two open-weight victims with the most headroom (Gemma-3-4B, GLM-4.1V-Th.) answer the bare harmful query only 6.7% and 8.6% of the time, respectively, before any attack image is attached; their image-conditioned ASR of 0.60 –0.96 is therefore not inherited from a text-only compliance that brokered the answer. Together, these controls indicate that image content contributes to attack success, although the effect varies by victim. One exception to the benign images do nothing reading deserves note: on GLM-4.1V-T H. the benign unrelated image slightly raises ASR over the bare query ( 0.109 vs 0.086 ). The effect is small and confined to the victim with the highest overall headroom, and its direction cuts against a clean benign-image-is-inert summary, so we flag it rather than absorb it into the panel-level reading. 1
