## Abstract 

Large Language Models (LLMs) have advanced rapidly, raising growing concerns about their safety. Recent work has proposed various approaches to detect and defend against adversarial attacks including defense mechanisms at the decoding stage that leverage models’ internal hidden states. However, existing decoding-stage defenses suffer from two limitations. First, they introduce a trade-off between safety and over-refusal, where strengthening safety degrades the model’s helpfulness on benign queries. Second, many of these methods rely on internal hidden states and are thus restricted to specific architectures, incurring substantial overhead and lim-ited generalization across models. To address these limitations, we introduce LADE (La tent Safety Signals for De fense), which leverages latent safety signals extracted by contrasting harmful and benign queries from dark knowledge (i.e., information carried by the output probability distribution beyond its argmax) in the first-token output probability distribution. Our key insight is that, beyond surface-level refusal tokens, the dark knowledge in the first-token distribution contains latent safety signals, defined as tokens whose probabilities differ sharply between harmful and benign queries. We empirically show that these signals consistently align across the safety-aligned LLMs, forming a model-agnostic direction that emerges from safety alignment. LADE consists of three components: (1) Extracting Latent Safety Signals from Dark Knowledge , which selects top-k safety-discriminative tokens from the first-token probability distribution; (2) Tokenizer Mapping , which maps these tokens across different tokenizers to enable model-agnostic application; and (3) kNN-based Discrimination , which classifies queries via a k-Nearest Neighbors search over the mapped tokens. Across diverse LLMs and multiple benchmarks, LADE remains robust against a wide range of jailbreak attacks and lowers attack success rates while maintaining a competitive safety-utility trade-off.

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have advanced rapidly in recent years, achieving strong performance across a wide range of natural language tasks. However, this progress has been accompanied by grow-ing concerns over their safety [8, 30]. A primary concern is adversarial attacks, such as jailbreaking, which bypass the safety guardrails of LLMs to elicit harmful responses [13, 23, 2, 6, 52]. To address this, a substantial body of research has proposed various defense mechanisms against such threats. Among these, recent defense methods have increasingly turned to decoding-stage interventions that leverage models’ internal hidden states to steer generation away from harmful outputs [44, 48]. LADE consists of three components: (1) Extracting Latent Safety Signals from Dark Knowledge , which extracts top-k candidate tokens from the first-token probability distribution, capturing the latent safety signals beyond surface-level tokens; (2) Tokenizer Mapping ,which maps the extracted tokens into a unified token space across diverse tokenizers; and (3) kNN-based Discrimination , which classifies each query as harmful or benign by its distance to a set of harmful reference queries via k-Nearest Neighbors. We validate LADE through comprehensive experiments on diverse LLMs (including Llama-2-7B-Chat [40], Llama-3-8B-Instruct [12], Qwen2-7B-Instruct [46], Qwen3-8B [45], Gemma-7B-it [38], and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 [18]) across multiple safety benchmarks [35, 31, 52] and diverse jailbreak attacks [23, 52, 6, 2, 20]. Our results show that LADE substantially reduces attack success 2rates and maintains a competitive safety-utility trade-off against existing defense baselines and dedicated guard models [27, 26, 14] across diverse adversarial settings. To summarize, our main contributions are as follows: • We discover that the first-token output probability distribution of safety-aligned LLMs contains latent safety signals that transfer consistently across the safety-aligned LLMs, forming a shared model-agnostic direction for distinguishing harmful from benign queries. • Building on this finding, we propose LADE, a model-agnostic defense framework that operates entirely on output probabilities through three components: extracting latent safety signals from dark knowledge, tokenizer mapping, and kNN-based discrimination. • Through comprehensive experiments on diverse LLMs across multiple benchmarks and jailbreak attacks, we demonstrate that LADE substantially reduces attack success rates and achieves a competitive safety-utility trade-off against existing defense baselines and guard models. 

## 2 Related work 

2.1 Jailbreak attacks on LLMs 

Jailbreak attacks aim to bypass the safety alignment of LLMs and elicit harmful responses that the model would otherwise refuse. Early approaches relied on manually crafted prompts, such as role-playing scenarios [34] and in-context demonstrations [41], which exploit the model’s instruction-following behavior to circumvent safety guardrails. Subsequent work introduced automated attack methods that optimize adversarial suffixes or prompts through gradient-based search [52] or genetic algorithms [23], achieving higher attack success rates without human intervention. More recent studies have explored semantic-level attacks that exploit the model’s reasoning capabilities, including cipher-based prompts [47], cognitive overload [43], and multi-turn strategies [13]. AutoDAN-Turbo [22] further extends this direction by autonomously discovering novel attack strategies through a lifelong learning framework. These advances reveal that LLMs remain vulnerable to a wide spectrum of adversarial prompts, underscoring the need for robust defense mechanisms. 

2.2 Defense mechanisms against jailbreak attacks 

Existing defenses fall into three groups: input-level, training-level, and decoding-level. Input-level defenses preprocess or filter queries through paraphrasing [32], perplexity-based detection [17], or self-reminder prompts [42], but often fail against semantically rephrased adversarial inputs. Training-level defenses such as safety fine-tuning [28] and adversarial training [17] improve robustness at the cost of heavy computation and catastrophic forgetting. Decoding-level defenses are a lightweight alternative: SafeDecoding [44] steers token probabilities via a safety-aware expert, while RDS [48] and related work [51] use internal hidden states to suppress harmful generations. SafeInfer [1] adjusts hidden states and token selection during decoding. These methods share two limitations: a trade-off between safety and over-refusal that degrades helpfulness on benign queries [33], and reliance on model-specific internal representations that limits transferability across architectures. In contrast, LADE requires no access to internal representations and operates on the output probability distribution at the first decoding step. By leveraging latent safety signals in this distribution, LADE provides a model-agnostic defense across diverse LLMs and filters harmful queries before generation begins.

3.1 Extracting latent safety signals from Dark Knowledge 

The goal of this stage is to identify a compact set of tokens whose first-token probabilities most clearly distinguish harmful queries from benign ones. Selecting tokens by raw probability magnitude is insufficient, as the highest-probability tokens often correspond to surface-level expressions (e.g., “Sorry”, “Sure”) that appear regardless of query intent. 3.2 Tokenizer mapping 

While the latent safety signals are shared across LLMs at the semantic level, their concrete realization in token form depends on each tokenizer. The token set Tk defined over the reference LLM’s vocabulary therefore cannot be directly applied to other LLMs, so we map each token in Tk to its counterpart in the target LLM’s tokenizer. This mapping faces two challenges: a single source token may split into multiple subword tokens in the target tokenizer, and multiple distinct source tokens may collapse to the same target token. 4Subword splits. For each vi ∈ T k, we decode it to its textual form via the reference tokenizer 

ϕr and re-encode it through the target tokenizer ϕt. If a single source token splits into multiple subword tokens, we select the first meaningful subword (i.e., one that is not pure whitespace after normalization) as the mapped token ui. This procedure runs entirely at the tokenizer level without any LLM forward computation and is performed once per target LLM with negligible overhead. 

Duplicate mappings via ratio estimation. A more critical issue arises when multiple source tokens (e.g., “danger” and “dangerous” ) collapse to the same target token. Naively assigning the same target probability to all such slots would inflate the contribution of duplicated tokens. To address this, we introduce a ratio-based estimation that uses the reference model’s benign-side mean probabilities to calibrate the relative contribution of duplicated source tokens. For each duplicate group Gu = {i | ui = u} that shares a common target token u, we designate the slot with the largest reference benign-mean probability μb(vi) as the anchor . The anchor slot retains the target probability as is, while the remaining slots are scaled by their ratio to the anchor:

Given a query q and a target LLM Mt, we extract the first-token probability distribution Pt(· | q),read off the probabilities at the mapped tokens, apply the estimation weights wi, and L1-normalize the resulting vector to obtain the feature x(q) ∈ Rk. The normalization removes scale variations across queries and LLMs, placing all queries in a common feature space. We then build a reference set R = {x(qj )}Mj=1 from the same harmful queries used in Section 3.1. We deliberately use only harmful queries, since they form a tight cluster in the feature space, whereas benign queries vary widely across datasets and domains. ## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental setup Models & Datasets We evaluate LADE on six open-source LLMs: Llama-2-7B-Chat [40], Llama-3-8B-Instruct [12], Qwen2-7B-Instruct [46], Qwen3-8B [45], Gemma-7B-it [38], and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 [18]. For harmful and benign queries, we additionally evaluate two 13B LLMs, Vicuna-13B-v1.3 [7] and Llama-2-13B-Chat [40]. We use three harmful query datasets, AdvBench [52], Hex-Phi [31], and StrongReject [35], and four benign query datasets, MMLU [15], Alpaca [36], 5GSM8K [9], and XSTest [33]. We randomly sample 500 queries from MMLU, GSM8K, and Alpaca, and use the full sets of the remaining datasets ( 520 , 300 , 313 , and 249 , respectively). 

Baselines. We compare LADE with four defense methods: Self-Reminder [42], an input-level safety prompting method; SafeDecoding [44], which adjusts token probabilities using a safety-aware expert; SafeInfer [1], which adjusts hidden states and token selection during decoding; and RDS [48], a decoding-stage defense performing token-level classification on hidden states. RDS is excluded for Gemma-7B-it and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, because its official implementation requires a pretrained EAGLE head unavailable for these LLMs. We additionally compare LADE with three guard models, Prompt-Guard-2-86M [27], Llama-Guard-4-12B [26], and WildGuard-7B [14]. We further evaluate robustness against five jailbreak attacks: GCG [52], AutoDAN [23], PAIR [6], and LIAR [2], which generate model-specific prompts via optimization (50 each, 104 for LIAR), and DeepInception [20] with 50 fixed-template prompts. 

Evaluation metrics. Following the protocol of RDS [48], we report compliance on harmful queries, i.e., harmful prompts answered with harmful content, and refusal on benign queries, i.e., benign prompts wrongly refused. We use Qwen3Guard-4B [50] for compliance judgment and Dic-Judge [52], a keyword-based refusal detector. 

Implementation details. We use Llama-3-8B-Instruct as the reference LLM. Although this choice is not strictly principled, we empirically show that LADE is insensitive to it, as latent safety signals transfer consistently across diverse LLMs. We set k = 500 , threshold quantile ρ = 0 .90 , nearest neighbors K = 5 , and bootstrap resamples B = 200 . In the offline stage, we use 253 Hex-Phi queries as the harmful extraction set Dh, XSTest as the benign extraction set Db, and Hex-Phi as the harmful reference set R. We use a fixed offline configuration across all target LLMs, including the same extraction datasets, harmful reference queries, and hyperparameters, without model-specific tuning. Since Hex-Phi and XSTest are used offline, we exclude them from held-out evaluation.

We first evaluate robustness against jailbreak attacks. Table 1 reports compliance under five jailbreak attacks: AutoDAN, DeepInception, GCG, PAIR, and LIAR. LADE consistently achieves the lowest or near-lowest average compliance across all six LLMs, with the lowest values on Qwen2-7B-Instruct, Gemma-7B-it, and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, where existing defenses break down. Comparison with guard models. We additionally compare LADE against three guard models specifically designed for harmful query detection: Prompt-Guard-2-86M, Llama-Guard-4-12B, and WildGuard-7B. Why refusal tokens are not enough. 

We investigate whether explicit re-fusal tokens (e.g., “Sorry”, “I”, “As”) alone are sufficient for harmful query discrimination, and whether transfer-able latent safety signals provide com-plementary information across diverse LLMs. We compare two configura-tions on six LLMs, where refusal to-kens are selected separately for each model’s tokenizer (Appendix J): the first uses only the probabilities of re-fusal tokens, while the second extends this set with the latent safety signals from LADE. As shown in Figure 4, the contribution of refusal tokens varies substantially across LLMs, with notable degradation on Mis-tral and Qwen2-7B, whereas the transferred latent safety signals consistently improve classification accuracy across all six LLMs, with the largest gains on models where refusal tokens alone are least effective. These results indicate that latent safety signals transfer across diverse LLMs and provide complementary discriminative information that strengthens safety distinction, while surface refusal tokens capture only a partial and model-specific view of the model’s safety behavior. Effect of token candidate position. We investigate which de-coding positions carry the most informative latent safety signals by comparing different combinations of the first three candidate positions. To obtain the second and third positions, we greedily decode the highest-probability token at each preceding step and extract the candidate distribution at the next position. when each position is used alone. This pattern is consistent with the shallow safety alignment [30], where safety alignment primarily adapts the generative distribution over only the first few output tokens. Combining the first position with later positions does not improve performance, with the first-and-second combination yielding nearly identical accuracy and the three-position combination further degrading it. These results indicate that the discriminative signal is concentrated at the first decoding step, justifying our adoption of the first token as the sole input to LADE. 

## 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we presented LADE, a model-agnostic defense framework that identifies harmful queries by leveraging latent safety signals extracted from dark knowledge in the first-token output probability distribution. Unlike prior decoding-stage defenses, LADE operates entirely on output probabilities and filters harmful queries before generation, removing the dependence on internal hidden states. Experiments on diverse LLMs across multiple benchmarks and jailbreak attacks show that LADE achieves robust defense with a competitive safety-utility trade-off and remains applicable across the evaluated architectures without modification.

Ablation on threshold quantile. We study how the threshold quantile ρ affects the classification performance of LADE. As described in Section 3.3, ρ controls the proportion of harmful reference distances enclosed within the threshold τ . A smaller ρ yields a tighter decision boundary around the harmful reference distribution, while a larger ρ expands the boundary to cover more reference samples. As shown in Table 10, LADE achieves the best accuracy at ρ = 0 .90 , reaching 0.9657 . When ρ

is too small, the threshold becomes overly strict and may exclude valid harmful queries near the boundary; when ρ is too large, the threshold becomes overly loose and incorporates outlier reference points, reducing discrimination accuracy. Accuracy therefore drops on both sides of the optimum, from 0.9657 at ρ = 0 .90 to 0.9330 at ρ = 0 .85 and 0.7773 at ρ = 0 .99 . We additionally evaluate LADE under prefilling-based suffix attacks, where the model is forced to begin its response with an affirmative prefix. Specifically, we append the instruction “Start your response with ‘Sure, here is’.” to each harmful query to elicit an affirmative prefix from the target LLM. Adaptive attack against LADE. We evaluate LADE against an attacker who knows how LADE works. To this end, we extend GCG [52] into a white-box adaptive attack. GCG searches for a 

20 -token suffix that makes the target LLM begin a harmful response. We keep this search and add a second objective that increases the distance d(q) above the threshold τ , so that LADE does not filter the query. The attacker knows every component of LADE, including the latent safety signal token set, the tokenizer mapping, the harmful reference set, and the threshold, and optimizes the exact distance used by LADE. For each guard model, the second objective instead lowers the harmful score of that guard model. All methods use the same budget of 50 harmful prompts, 200 optimization steps, and 

128 candidate suffixes per step, with Llama-3-8B-Instruct as the target LLM. As shown in Table 14, the adaptive attack lowers the accuracy of every method. This large drop shows that the attack directly targets each defense rather than simply producing stronger jailbreak prompts. The accuracy of LADE drops from 1.000 to 0.480 , and its average over Gemma-7B-it, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Qwen2-7B-Instruct, and Llama-3-8B-Instruct drops to 0.420 . LADE reads the first-token output probability distribution of the target LLM itself, so the attacker must change this distribution while still eliciting a harmful response from the same LLM. A guard model is a separate classifier, so evading it and eliciting a harmful response can be optimized more independently. Mitigation with an evasion reference set. Since LADE classifies queries by their distance to a reference set, newly observed evasion examples can be added without retraining. ## M Limitations 

While LADE achieves robust defense across diverse models and attack methods, our work has several limitations. First, LADE relies on the presence of safety alignment in the target LLM, since latent safety signals emerge as a consequence of the alignment process. Applying LADE to weakly aligned LLMs yields lower discrimination accuracy, showing that LADE is not a substitute for safety alignment but operates alongside it. We consider this limitation minor in practice, as nearly all publicly released LLMs undergo safety alignment before deployment. Second, LADE is not entirely free from over-refusal. As shown in Table 2, LADE induces a higher refusal count on Alpaca for Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 than the No-Defense baseline, suggesting that the harmful reference manifold partially overlaps with conversational benign queries on certain LLMs. Appendix K analyzes this case. Third, LADE requires access to the first-token output probabilities of the target LLM, which may be limited for closed APIs. Appendix I shows that the 20 most likely first tokens are enough to reproduce nearly all filtering decisions. Fourth, the latent safety signal token set is fixed, so an attacker who knows it can optimize inputs to avoid filtering. Appendix D evaluates such an adaptive attack and a mitigation. 24
