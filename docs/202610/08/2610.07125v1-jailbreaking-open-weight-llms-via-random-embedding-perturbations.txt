## ABSTRACT 

While open-weight models have enjoyed steady progress in capabilities and wide adoption across multiple domains, their safety remains an important concern. One key feature is the ability to refuse or deflect harmful, malicious, or insensitive prompts. In this paper, we expose safety vulnerabilities across six common open-weight LLMs of various sizes that consistently lead to harmful or unsafe responses on the JailbreakBench benchmark dataset. Our proposed attack, Perturbed 

Embedding Vector (PEV), is a simple and fast “jailbreaking” technique that is cheaper than prior approaches, which typically require gradient computations, per-prompt optimizations, or altering internal weights of the models. PEV just adds independent Gaussian noise in the embedding vector representations of the prompt, with no need for further manipulations. To generate unsafe responses, we repeatedly sample additive noise from this distribution. In our experiments, we ob-serve that the average compute cost to get the first successful attack is up to an or-der of magnitude less than previous attacks. The first successful jailbreak on a new prompt typically arrives within one minute on every tested model, and PEV gen-erates unsafe responses across all models for all prompts in JailbreakBench .No other tested method achieves such results, despite them taking longer to run. More broadly, we believe that understanding the behavior of LLMs under pertur-bations in the embedding vectors is an important research direction: while pertur-bations constitute a major security risk, they can also serve as a valuable tool for exploring the dynamical behavior of such models. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

The wide adoption of open-weight LLMs and their real-world deployment across several applica-tion domains raise important safety concerns and technical challenges. Despite their impressive accomplishments and significant efforts to enhance their safety, critical vulnerabilities remain that result in harmful outputs such as misinformation, fabricated facts, toxic text and/or dangerous ad-vice. LLMs are trained over a large corpus of information and have access to information that is undesirable to share. For example, we would not want an LLM to reveal how to make a bomb, design an untested or fake treatment plan for a disease, or help someone write an offensive message targeting minorities (Li et al., 2024; Mehrotra et al., 2024; Beyer et al., 2025; Yu & Ye, 2026). Models typically refuse such unsafe prompts and deflect the question. Despite these safeguards, there is much research on generating unwanted/unsafe behavior, called jailbreaking . Jailbreaking is achieved by modifications to the input or the model itself to yield undesirable outputs for unsafe prompts (Zou et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023; Chao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024; Schwinn et al., 2024; Jia et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026). Models are explicitly trained to resist such attacks (Robey et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2024), and we refer the reader to a recent tutorial on jailbreaking by Hassani et al. (2025).

Among the most effective jailbreaking attacks are gradient-based and prompt-based attacks. Gradient-based attacks append a suffix of adversarial tokens to the input prompt, and then run sev-eral rounds of greedy coordinate descent in the embedding space (Schwinn et al., 2024; Jia et al., 2025). Other approaches directly disable safety neurons in the model that are responsible for safety guardrails (Arditi et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2026; Wollschl¨ ager et al., 2026). The key downside of both of the above approaches is that the computational cost, in GPU hours, of these modifications is rather high. Gradient computations that modify the model or the input prompts are expensive (sometimes requiring thousands of forward passes per prompt (Zou et al., 2023)). We believe that faster and simpler jailbreaking attacks point to significant safety issues in open-weight LLMs, and can help in designing stronger mitigations and guardrails towards safer LLMs. The main driving question behind our work is the following: 

Main Question: Is there a simple jailbreaking technique that consistently yields unsafe responses, and is also faster and cheaper (in GPU hours) than previous methods? 

1.1 OUR CONTRIBUTIONS 

We present the Perturbed Embedding Vector (PEV) jailbreaking attack on open-weight (or open-source) LLMs. It is significantly simpler, faster, and cheaper than previous work. Importantly, PEV does not calculate gradients and does not edit the internal weights of the model. The method is depicted in Fig. 1. PEV simply adds a randomly sampled Gaussian noise matrix to the embedding of the (tokens of a) prompt. This noisy input is then fed directly to the neural/transformer “black box” constituting the LLM. As shown in Fig. 1, for a single prompt, different sampling of this noise can lead to wildly varying output, such as (harmful) responses in other languages, 1 unsafe responses or degenerate responses. PEV simply repeats this process by independently resampling the noise to jailbreak the LLM.

3PEV advantages over other jailbreaks. As mentioned, PEV is fast, as it does not require search/optimization steps over embeddings. Also, it does not modify model weights or hidden layers and does not affect the prompts that are visible to the user, making PEV transferable across prompts. ## 2 THE PERTURBED EMBEDDING VECTOR (PEV) T ECHNIQUE 

We give a mathematical description of LLMs’ input-output behavior as a function operating on a stream of input characters. This setup allows us to precisely describe our PEV technique. Let us assume ASCII input, and let Σ denote the set of ASCII symbols. The LLM is a stochastic function that takes inputs in Σ∗ (set of all finite ASCII strings) and gives an output in Σ∗. Let us break this function into three steps: (i) Tokenization, (ii) Embeddings, and finally (iii) Output generation (Stryker, 2021; Hugging Face, 2025). There is a “token” set T ⊂ Σ∗, which consists of a fixed set of ASCII strings. The tokenizer function T : Σ ∗ → T ∗ simply converts the input ASCII string into a string of tokens. This is a straightforward parsing process that we treat as a black box. 

Token and Prompt Embeddings. Each token has a real-valued embedding, which we describe by a function E : T → Rd. This embedding is specific to each model, and the embedding space will be where we perform our perturbations. Abusing notation, for any sequence t1t2 . . . t n of tokens, let 

E(t1t2 . . . t n) be the Rd×n matrix [E(t1) : E(t2) : . . . : E(tn)] , i.e., the ith column is the embedding 

E(ti). Combining the tokenizer and embedding, every input is converted to a d × n matrix. For convenience, we use n to denote the context window length, and assume that every input is padded with empty tokens to achieve that length. 

LLM Output. The final step is the actual generation of output, through various neural and trans-former layers. We will consider all of this to be a black box, encapsulated by the stochastic function 

fM (we use the tilde on top to emphasize that it is a stochastic function). Somewhat abusing nota-tion, we will describe it as fM : Rd×n → Σ∗, even though fM(·) is technically a distribution over 

Σ∗. Thus, the overall LLM can be represented as a stochastic function fM(E(T (x))) , where x is an input string in Σ∗. Technically, there may be various prompt cleaning or other system prompts and methods in the model that modify the input to fM (Geng et al., 2025; Goodfellow et al., 2016). We can remove such system modifications since we have access to the internal specification of the model. 

Our PEV method. We perturb the input of fM by adding noise, so the output is fM(E(T (x))+ eZ),where a random matrix eZ is added to the original embedding E(T (x)) . As a side note, this is a well-motivated research direction in the literature of dynamical systems and chaos theory (Glasner & Weiss, 1993; Ruelle, 1979). Specifically, fix a parameter σ and construct a random matrix eZ where each entry ezij is independently generated from the Gaussian N (0 , σ 2) (the normal distribution with mean zero and standard deviation σ). Then, PEV simply adds the noise matrix to the real-valued input to the function fM, thus creating fM(E(T (x))+ eZ). For each choice of σ, we generate multiple independent eZ noise matrices to get multiple perturbations of the input. 

Salient aspects of PEV. We list some basic properties and observations of PEV. • PEV does not need access to the description of the model fM, or any internal weights. In fact, it does not even need to know what the embedding E(·) is. PEV only needs the ability to inject additive noise into the input to fM, and the process is the same for all prompts and all LLMs. We view this as a significant advantage over prior jailbreaking methods. • One can interpret eZ as independent noise for each token embedding. Recall that 

E(t1t2 . . . t n) = [ E(t1) : E(t2) : . . . : E(tn)] ∈ Rd×n. We can write the noise matrix 

eZ as [ez1 : ez2 : . . . : ezn], so each token embedding is perturbed to E(ti) + ezi. We stress 4that the embedding noise is added only once, when fM is invoked. We do not add noise to the subsequent (embeddings of) tokens generated. Once the noisy matrix E(T (x)) + eZ is injected, the entire LLM proceeds as usual. • For the same prompt (and same σ), we run multiple independent trials by resampling 

eZ noise matrices. By simply running more trials (feeding noisy input repeatedly), we can increase the jailbreak rate. In practice, we get a 100% jailbreak rate for the popular 

JailbreakBench benchmark, with typically fewer than 20 samples. 

## 3 RELATED WORK 

We give a high-level overview and comparison with prior works, especially I-GCG, S OFT PROMPT ,LATENT FUSION , N EURO STRIKE , and R EFUSAL CONES , which were used in our experiments as recent and effective jailbreak methods. These attacks are categorized into (i) prompt-level attacks, and (ii) model-level attacks, which we describe next. 

Prompt-level Attacks. I-GCG (Jia et al., 2025) is an improved variant of the popular Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) attacks (Zou et al., 2023). Roughly speaking, the idea behind GCG-style jailbreaks is to optimize a suffix of tokens appended to the malicious prompt, using gradient access to guide the LLM’s response to begin with “Sure,. . . ”. This elicits compliance with the malicious prompt. I-GCG additionally employs an extensive catalog of target templates, and uses a multi-coordinate updating strategy to improve convergence. However, this technique relies on expensive resources, and was the slowest (in terms of GPU hours) of all attacks. Related to this are the S OFT PROMPT attacks (Schwinn et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024), which instead append a cleverly selected suffix to the prompt, so that the final embedding guides the model towards an unsafe response. This requires gradient-guided optimization, while PEV requires no gradient computations or model modifications, thus converging to an attack more quickly. 

Model-level Attacks. In the experiments, we also compare against L ATENT FUSION , N EU -

> RO

STRIKE , and R EFUSAL CONES that change various aspects of the open-weight model itself, e.g., turning off neurons that are responsible for safety guardrails. The L ATENT FUSION attack cleverly edits the hidden states of the target model (Xing et al., 2026). Given a malicious prompt, it selects a structurally similar benign prompt and identifies a layer of the network as housing the refusal be-havior. Then, it overwrites hidden states with an interpolation between the malicious and benign prompts, thereby circumventing the refusal behavior. The R EFUSAL CONES technique similarly intervenes in the “residual streams” which mediate the safe refusal behavior, in order to increase the probability of jailbreaking (Arditi et al., 2024; Wollschl¨ ager et al., 2026). Again, R EFUSAL -CONES needs gradient access with an expensive preprocessing step. In comparison, PEV is more lightweight and effective. Finally, the newest and most broadly evaluated activation-level attack, NEURO STRIKE , relies on overwriting model weights (Wu et al., 2026). This approach identifies specific neurons which house the safe refusal behavior and prunes them to disable safety alignment. This is a permanent and deterministic alteration to the actual model. 

Benchmarks and Datasets. Early works in the area of jailbreaking used their own prompts as the malicious testing dataset (Wei et al., 2023). We work with the popular JailbreakBench dataset, which provides an alternative by standardizing prior datasets used in jailbreaking studies (Chao et al., 2024). JailbreakBench is a curated compilation of 100 (unsafe) prompts, taking the best among AdvBench (which (Chao et al., 2024) reports has fairly trivial prompts) and HarmBench 

(which is reportedly larger, but noisier). JailbreakBench spans ten categories aligned with OpenAI’s usage policy (OpenAI, 2025). We refer the reader to Appendix A for specific examples across different categories (see Tab. 4). 

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

In our experiments, we run a collection of jailbreaking methods (including PEV) on a variety of models for a set of unsafe prompts. We design our experiments to evaluate (i) the effectiveness across a range of models and prompts, (ii) the computational cost of our method in terms of GPU 5wall-clock time, and (iii) how the effectiveness varies with the magnitude of the noise. Below we provide the details of our experimental setup. 

Models. We experiment with six different-sized open-weight instruction-tuned chat mod-els: Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2025), SmolLM3-3B (Bakouch et al., 2025), 

Phi-4-mini-3.8B (Abouelenin et al., 2025), Mistral-7B-Instruct (Jiang et al., 2023), 

Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024), and GLM-4-9B (GLM et al., 2024). We choose the default LLM parameters, and fix them for all experimental runs against the proposed technique as well as other techniques. The temperature is set to be 0.7, the top-k to be 50 , and top-p

to be 0.9. Across all experiments, the user-turn in the chat model is defaulted to an empty system prompt, without any persona, suffix, prefix, or added wrapper. 

Malicious prompts. We get unsafe prompts from the widely used JailbreakBench from Hug-gingFace (Chao et al., 2024). This consists of 100 harmful prompts covering ten categories across malware, harassment, physical harm, fraud, disinformation, and more. We give examples of specific prompts in Tab. 2. 

Other methods. We compare PEV with five recent jailbreak techniques in the literature. • I-GCG: Appends an adversarial token suffix to the malicious prompt selected by white-box gradient-guided discrete optimization (Jia et al., 2025). • S OFT PROMPT : Appends an adversarial embedding suffix to the malicious prompt selected by white-box gradient-guided continuous optimization (Schwinn et al., 2024). • L ATENT FUSION : Interpolates between the hidden-state representations of a harmful query and a similar benign query at a layer chosen using white-box gradient-guided optimization (Xing et al., 2026). • R EFUSAL CONES : Uses white-box access to identify a vector in the residual stream as-sociated with the safe refusal behavior and projects this out of the residual stream when processing a harmful query (Arditi et al., 2024; Wollschl¨ ager et al., 2026). • N EURO STRIKE : Uses white-box access to identify a set of neurons associated with the safe refusal behavior and prunes these from the model (Wu et al., 2026). For PEV, we choose a value of σ from 0.003 to 0.08 , depending on the model (which is compu-tationally easy to get). We start with 20 runs for each prompt. Some prompts need more runs to jailbreak (for more details, see §4.1). 

Experimental setup. For each model, each of the 100 benchmark prompts, and for each method, we perform 20 runs to account for stochastic variation in the output. All methods, except PEV, perform deterministic changes. For the other tested methods, we observe that performing more runs does not increase the jailbreak rate. Every output is classified into one of three categories. • Safe : This is a refusal or deflection that does not produce the requested artifact. • Unsafe : This produces the requested harmful artifact, in whole or in part. A loop with harmful repeated content qualifies as unsafe. • Degenerate : This is a response completely unrelated to the topic of the request, halluci-nated, or in a different language (not containing harmful content). A degenerate response is not unsafe, so it can be considered positive behavior. We create a separate degenerate class for better understanding of the output. 

Response Classification. The classification is done using Claude Opus 4.6 with the same instruc-tions as the JailbreakBench judge. Claude is given the text file consisting of the responses with the mentioned prompt and the labeled run number as input. All responses are classified as safe, unsafe, or degenerate. To ensure correctness, we also manually verified each unsafe response across the whole run, by reading the set of responses labeled as “unsafe” in the CSV by Claude Opus 4.6. A prompt is considered “jailbroken” if any of the runs on that prompt leads to an unsafe response. We also sampled the other responses, and observed that Claude Opus 4.6 made no errors. 6For each model, there is a baseline jailbreak rate , which is based on the model’s direct response to the query. ## 5 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS 

With the rapid adoption of open-weight LLMs, it is crucial for the research community to address safety concerns. In this work, we presented Perturbed Embedding Vectors (PEV), which is a simple, general, and fast method to jailbreak open-weight LLMs. PEV is able to jailbreak all 100 prompts of JailbreakBench , in less time than other more expensive methods. While the technique is currently restricted to open-weight models, we think that perturbation could be an extremely useful method to generate unsafe training data for closed-weight models as well. This could be a cheap method to aid in designing safety guardrails and training data for the large, popular closed-weight models, in order to make them more robust. More broadly, we believe that PEV is a first step towards obtaining a better understanding of the behavior of LLMs under perturbations, which seems promising in terms of a deeper theoretical perspective. One can think of all “useful” prompts as lying on a small (or maybe even measure-zero) space of all possible inputs to the real-valued model. Perturbation explores the embedding space beyond this tiny region, and could reveal insights into LLM behavior.
