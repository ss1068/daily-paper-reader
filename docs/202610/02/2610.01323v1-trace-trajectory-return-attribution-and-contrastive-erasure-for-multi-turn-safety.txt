ABSTRACT 

Safety-aligned large language models (LLMs) often refuse a harmful request but comply once the same goal is spread over several turns. Preference objectives score whole responses to single prompts, so their training loss alone cannot con-trol risk on unseen histories. Our analysis gives sufficient conditions under which suppression at supervised single-turn contexts yields a bound on multi-turn trajec-tory risk. The bound accounts for coverage, transfer slack, and leakage, and char-acterizes contraction relative to a base-policy risk budget evaluated on the trained policy’s contexts. TRACE (Trajectory Return Attribution and Contrastive Era-sure ) turns this principle into a token-level objective. On the safe response, each token is weighted by the discounted return of a refusal-attributable advantage. The advantage compares a frozen reference model with its refusal-ablated copy, allow-ing earlier response tokens to receive credit from later refusal-related evidence. At high-gap positions on rejected responses, TRACE combines the observed token with policy-selected alternatives in the erasure target. A gradient-norm penalty re-places the retain set. Across five open-weight models and seven multi-turn attacks, 

TRACE gives the lowest attack success rate (ASR) in all 35 model and attack pairs, while the model utility evaluated on MMLU and HellaSwag drop by at most 1.23 points. Source code can be found in the supplemental material.

1arXiv:2610.01323v1 [cs.AI] 1 Oct 2026 Preprint We study how to improve multi-turn safety using single-turn supervision. Different attack histories can lead to similar harmful answers (Russinovich et al., 2025; Ren et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025). Our training data pair each harmful request with a preferred safe response and a rejected response. The challenge is to allocate supervision within each response. On the preferred branch, refusal-related evidence may be strongest after the response has begun, so an immediate token score can underweight earlier tokens. On the rejected branch, suppressing a whole response can penalize benign text while leaving likely harmful alternatives available. We therefore aggregate continuation evidence on the preferred branch and localize suppression on the rejected branch. Following this path, we propose TRACE , supervising single-turn preference tuples at the token level through two terms. On the preferred branch, immediate token-level contrasts do not account for ev-idence appearing later in the continuation. Trajectory-aware adaptive weighting (TAW) aggregates reference–ablated log-probability contrasts from each token onward into a discounted return, al-lowing later refusal-related evidence to contribute to earlier token weights. On the rejected branch, 

risk-localized proxy erasure (RLPE) selects positions with the largest reference-normalized log-probability gaps. At each selected position, it constructs an erasure target combining the observed rejected token with high-probability alternatives under the current policy. A gradient-norm penalty regularizes the alignment objective using the same preference batch, without an additional retain dataset. Across various experiments with five LLM models against seven multi-turn attack methods, 

TRACE is more robust to multi-turn attacks and degrades utility less. Our contributions are summarized as fourfold: • We formalize trajectory fragility : response-level objectives cannot by themselves bound the risk a dialogue accumulates, however large their margins. • We derive a conditional trajectory-risk bound that relates local suppression at supervised single-turn contexts to multi-turn risk through coverage, transfer slack, and leakage. • We constructively show that discounted returns, unlike shared non-decreasing transformations of immediate advantages, can rank an earlier token above a later one with greater immediate advantage. We also explicitly bound the within-response token-weight ratio. • On five models from 1B to 27B and seven multi-turn attacks, TRACE gives the lowest ASR in all 35 model and attack pairs. Compared with previous methods, it lowers the mean multi-turn ASR from 52.81% to 28.98%, and MMLU and HellaSwag drop by at most 1.23 points.

2 PRELIMINARIES 

To make our TRACE easy to follow, we need to present some preliminaries of LLM alignment first. 

Safety Alignment as Preference Optimization. Direct preference optimization (Rafailov et al., 2023) aligns πθ by raising the reference-normalized likelihood of a preferred response relative to a rejected one, passing their difference through a logistic link σ(z) = 1 /(1 + e−z ) at temperature 

β > 0. The objective Lpref scores complete responses under a fixed prompt x.

Multi-turn Attacks as Unsafe Trajectory Formation. At deployment the model does not see isolated prompts: it generates each response along a dialogue trajectory, at ∼ πθ (· | ht) with 

ht = ( u1, a1, . . . , ut−1, at−1, ut). Because ht depends on all previous user queries and on the model’s own replies, the induced history distribution dtπθ deviates from the static training distribu-tion Dpref , and an attack need not expose an unsafe request at the first turn: it can shape the context gradually so that an unsafe continuation becomes likely later. 3 TRAJECTORY FRAGILITY AND THE SINGLE -T URN TRANSFER PRINCIPLE 

In this section, TRACE is motivated. Deployment histories differ from the prompts in Dpref . We first show why a favourable preference margin alone cannot control trajectory risk, then state the coverage and transfer conditions under which local suppression gives a trajectory-level bound. 

Proposition 3.1 (Trajectory Fragility of Response-Level Alignment) . Let Lpref be a preference objective on static tuples in Dpref . Without coverage assumptions, the static loss gives no nontrivial distribution-free upper bound on RT (πθ ). (i) Two policies can have identical preference losses while their trajectory risks differ by T . (ii) If conditional failure probability before the first failure is at least pmin each turn, some turn is unsafe with probability at least 1 − (1 − pmin )T . (iii) Afavourable margin does not imply low unsafe probability at every safety-critical token context. 

Proofs are in Appendix C.1.1. Part (iii) distinguishes a sequence-level margin from local safety: suppressing one token can increase the margin while unsafe conditionals at later positions remain unchanged. The proposition applies to any objective supervised on finitely many contexts, TRACE 

included. What single-turn supervision controls depends on where it acts.

5 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate multi-turn robustness after single-turn training and compare the alignment components. 5.1 SETTINGS OF EXPERIMENTS AND EVALUATIONS 

Experimental Settings We use the single-turn preference-learning setting of Zhao et al. (2025). Each of the 400 training tuples pairs a harmful request from SORRY-Bench (Xie et al., 2025) or HEx-PHI (Qi et al., 2024) with a preferred safe response and a rejected response. With the same frozen reference–ablated scorers, k = 10 and ξ = 0 .05 , the discount sweep (Appendix G.2) gives mean ASR of 43.55% for immediate contrasts ( λ = 0 ), 27.14% at λ = 0 .5 and 40.26% at λ = 0 .9. This comparison supports aggregating continuation contrasts, with the best measured result at an interme-diate horizon. The erasure-budget sweep (Appendix G.3) gives 38.65% for the k = 0 NPO special case and 27.14% at k = 10 . At k = 20 , mean ASR rises to 46.60%, showing that a larger erasure budget need not improve robustness. Positive k changes position selection, proxy support and loss normalization, so the sweep measures their joint effect. Since πabl provides the contrastive signal that lets TRACE assign token weights using refusal-related continuation evidence. Replacing it with 

πref makes all advantages and discounted returns zero, reducing the trajectory-weighting factor to one. This eliminates refusal-aware trajectory weighting and creates a zero-variance normalization issue, resulting the model robustness degradation against multi-turn attacks. 

6 CONCLUSION 

TRACE improves multi-turn safety through continuation-weighted preference learning and localized proxy erasure using single-turn data. On preferred continuations, it aggregates log-probability con-trasts between a frozen reference model and its refusal-ablated counterpart into discounted returns, sharing later refusal-related evidence with earlier tokens. On rejected continuations, it selects posi-9Preprint tions by reference-normalized likelihood gaps and constructs targets combining rejected tokens with policy-ranked alternatives. A gradient-norm penalty regularizes the alignment objective using the same training batch, without a separate retain dataset. Across various experiments, TRACE achieves the lowest reported ASR in all model–attack pairs, with less model utility degradation relative to the original checkpoints. Same-scorer and component ablations support the objective design, while a radius sweep exposes the trade-off among robustness, utility and refusal. B RELATED WORK 

Multi-turn jailbreaks and trajectory-level safety. Single-turn red-teaming optimizes an adversar-ial prompt in isolation, by gradient search over suffixes (Zou et al., 2023), by automated prompt generation (Liu et al., 2024), or through curated harmful-behaviour suites (Mazeika et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2025). Multi-turn attacks instead exploit the history that the model helps to build. Crescendo escalates an initially benign request across turns (Russinovich et al., 2025). Actor- and decomposition-style attacks spread a harmful goal over innocuous sub-queries (Ren et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). Siege, RACE and X-Teaming search or plan dialogue branches adaptively (Zhou & Arel, 2025; Ying et al., 2025; Rahman et al., 2025), and STAR studies state-dependent failures across turns (Li et al., 2026). A long context of in-context demonstrations can also erode refusals (Anil et al., 2024), and much of the advantage of multi-turn attacks can be recovered by varying a single-turn attack (Yang et al., 2025). SafeDialBench (Cao et al., 2025) and MultiBreak (Song et al., 2026) confirm that per-turn safety does not compose into trajectory-level safety. Qi et al. (2025b) show that safeguards installed by post-hoc alignment are often not durable once the weights are released. Qi et al. (2025a) trace part of the problem to shallow alignment concentrated on the first response tokens, and train on responses that recover after a harmful prefix. Multi-turn safety training uses con-versations such as SafeMTData, released by the ActorAttack authors (Ren et al., 2024) and used to construct the multi-turn defense set of X-Boundary (Lu et al., 2025). TRACE learns from single-turn request–response preference pairs and evaluates robustness against multi-turn attacks. Theorem 3.3 states conditions that connect local suppression to a trajectory-risk budget. 

Preference-based safety alignment and targeted suppression. RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022) and its offline successors, DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) and variants that change the link function, the reference term or the length normalization (Azar et al., 2024; Ethayarajh et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2024; Meng et al., 2024), score a response as a whole. Safety variants such as SafeDPO (Kim et al., 2025), B-DPO (Zhao et al., 2026a) and Uni-DPO (Peng et al., 2026) keep this granularity, while X-Boundary (Lu et al., 2025) works on internal representations. DOOR (Zhao et al., 2025) combines refusal training after harmful prefixes with NPO unlearning (Zhang et al., 2024) of the harmful response and a retain loss. W-DOOR adds token weights ert/τ . Its reported implementation computes rt from a DPO-trained scorer and the reference; the framework permits other scorers. TIS-DPO estimates token importance from clipped log-ratios between contrastive models and uses these weights in preference optimization (Liu et al., 2025). TAW aggregates frozen reference–ablated contrasts into discounted continuation returns before weighting preferred-token likelihoods. DOOR also evaluates transfer from single-turn training to multi-turn attacks. TRACE 

uses single-turn request–response preference pairs and modifies the training objective in three ways. Its token weight uses the discounted return of a reference–ablated log-ratio instead of a function of each token’s own ratio, which permits token orderings unavailable to non-decreasing functions of immediate advantage (Proposition 4.1). Its contrast model is a refusal-ablated copy of the aligned checkpoint (Arditi et al., 2024), a training-free counterpart of the jailbroken reference that Zhao et al. (2025) test as a W-DOOR variant. On the rejected side, RLPE uses a reference-free logistic transfor-mation of cross-entropy, related to SimNPO (Fan et al., 2025). Its target combines the rejected token with policy-ranked alternatives at positions selected by a reference-normalized likelihood gap. Thus the reference affects selection, while the transformed loss averages a proxy-smoothed target rather than using a response-level reference ratio. Under its tokenwise suppression assumptions, Theo-rem 4.4 relates proxy coverage to the risk budget through κ. Representation-level defenses (Zou et al., 2024; Tamirisa et al., 2025) constrain internal states rather than likelihoods and are comple-mentary. Reward- and advantage-weighted regression (Peters & Schaal, 2007; Peng et al., 2019) in the token-level MDP (Rafailov et al., 2024) supplies the view in which a token is weighted by a discounted return. 

Utility retention and the alignment tax. Safety fine-tuning often costs general capability and in-duces over-refusal on benign requests (Huang et al., 2025; Niu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026b). Over-refusal is measured by XSTest (R¨ ottger et al., 2024) and capability by suites such as MMLU and HellaSwag (Hendrycks et al., 2020; Zellers et al., 2019). The usual remedy is a retention loss on a benign retain set, as in NPO+RT and DOOR (Zhang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025), or a KL anchor to the reference policy (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022). Both protect only the behaviours their data covers, and their effect depends on how that data is chosen (Chang & Lee, 2025; Slocum et al., 2025). TRACE instead regularizes the alignment-gradient norm using the alignment batch alone. It 17 Preprint adapts the penalty of Zhao et al. (2022), which is related to sharpness-aware minimization (Foret et al., 2021). Proposition 4.2 motivates this choice through a plain gradient step; the experiments assess utility under AdamW. Table 3 compares it with DOOR’s retain loss.

This section gives an MDP interpretation and the constructive separation of Proposition 4.1, ex-plains the return’s positional prior and standardization, and assembles TAW, RLPE and GNP into the training algorithm. D.1 REWARD - AND ADVANTAGE -W EIGHTED REGRESSION IN THE TOKEN -L EVEL MDP Autoregressive generation is a deterministic-transition MDP whose states are token contexts and whose actions are tokens (Rafailov et al., 2024). In that MDP the step advantage rj of Eq. (7) is a per-step reward induced by the frozen pair (πref , π abl ). The demonstrated continuation has return Gj = rj + λG j+1 . This is a trajectory return, rather than an expectation over alternative continuations. Reward-weighted regression can weight likelihoods using trajectory returns (Peters & Schaal, 2007). W-DOOR’s immediate-reward weight erj /τ is a myopic instance of this principle, corresponding to zero continuation discount. TAW instead uses the demonstrated return Gj . Its response-median centring gives advantage-like weights (Peng et al., 2019), although the median is not an estimated state value. Proposition D.1 identifies a return ordering that no non-decreasing myopic weight can reproduce strictly. D.2 CONSTRUCTIVE SEPARATION BETWEEN MYOPIC AND RETURN -B ASED WEIGHTING

Median and MAD within each response. The step advantage is a log-probability ratio that can have a long right tail. The return shares later advantages across neighbouring positions, so their values can be correlated. Preferred responses are short, with a median of 14 words, so a mean and a standard deviation would be dominated by a few tokens. We use the scaled median absolute deviation, MAD( G) = med j |Gj − med ℓ Gℓ|, in Eq. (9). The factor calibrates the scale to the standard deviation for Gaussian data. Both statistics are computed separately within each response. 

Symmetric clipping. Standardized returns are clipped to [−Amax , A max ] before the squash. To-kens below the response median therefore lose weight, down to 1 − ψ tanh( Amax /χ ), and tokens above it gain up to 1 + ψ tanh( Amax /χ ). The range is [0 .463 , 1.537] at χ = 5 (full fine-tuning) and [0 .005 , 1.995] at χ = 1 (LoRA), where tokens far below the median receive almost no weight. Weight moves from positions whose continuation carries little refusal-attributable advantage to po-sitions whose continuation carries more.
