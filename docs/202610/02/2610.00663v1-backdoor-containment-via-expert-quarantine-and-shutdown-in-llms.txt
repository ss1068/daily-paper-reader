## Abstract 

Backdoored large language models (LLMs) can behave normally on benign inputs while producing attacker-specified outputs under hidden triggers. Existing defenses span four stages—prior-training, in-training, post-training, and inference-time— and share one of two underlying strategies: either suppress backdoor learning 

(by filtering poisoned data or interrupting its acquisition during optimization) or 

learn, then purify (by repairing model weights or gating inputs after a fully back-doored model has formed). We propose a third strategy, learn, but channel : allow backdoor formation during training but route it into a designated, quarantined com-ponent that can be disabled at deployment. To this end, we propose Quarantined 

Expert Shutdown ( QES ), a computationally efficient containment strategy built in a regularization-steered MoE-like setting. Specifically, given a poisoned dataset, QES augments a Transformer-based language model with routed expert-specific LoRA branches and lightweight routers, and uses auxiliary routing objectives to at-tract trigger-conditioned behavior into a designated expert while preserving benign capability elsewhere. At deployment, mitigation reduces to a single constant-time operation: zeroing the quarantined expert’s routing weight, without trigger screen-ing or further updating model weights. Empirically, our methods reduce the attack success rate ( ASR ) from 100% to 0-10% on most settings across two tasks, three attacks, and four model families, while downstream utility is often preserved or only modestly affected. These results establish learn, but channel as a previously unexplored regime for backdoor containment in generative LLMs. 

## 1 Introduction 

Backdoored large language models (LLMs) remain a serious security concern because they can behave normally on benign inputs while producing attacker-specified outputs under hidden triggers [1, 2]. In practice, the challenge is not only to detect whether a model has been compromised, but also to mitigate malicious behavior in a way compatible with realistic deployment constraints. For generative LLMs (differ from classification models) integrated into downstream systems, existing defenses— whether they suppress backdoor learning by sanitizing data or modifying optimization [3–6], or 

purify the trained model or its inputs afterward [7–9, 3]—each require pipeline changes, retraining loops, or runtime overhead that do not scale cleanly to modern LLM fine-tuning and serving [10]. Existing backdoor defenses for generative LLMs span four stages of the training-deployment pipeline: 

prior-training data sanitization [3, 4], in-training optimization modifications [5, 6], post-training 

model repair [7, 8], and inference-time input filtering [9, 3]. These stages fall into two underlying strategies. Prior- and in-training methods suppress backdoor acquisition, either by removing suspected poisoned samples or by interrupting the formation of backdoor associations during optimization. Post-training and inference-time methods learn, then purify : they allow the model to be fully optimized

Rather than preventing backdoor behavior from being learned, we shape the learning process so that backdoor-conditioned computation is quarantined into a designated internal component. To instantiate this learn, but channel mechanism, we propose Quarantined Expert Shutdown ( QES ). Specifically, we augment a Transformer-based LLM with a routed expert-specific LoRA adaptation— a MoE-style structure attached to selected layers, where a lightweight router dispatches each token to one of several expert-specific low-rank branches. Crucially, to make this structure quarantinable by design , we use four attention-guided routing objectives during training to drain trigger-conditioned behavior into a designated, quarantined expert while preserving benign capability elsewhere. At deployment, mitigation is a single constant-time O(1) operation: zeroing the quarantine expert’s routing weight. Empirically, we evaluate QES across multiple generative LLM families, task scenarios, and attack techniques. Our results show that a single quarantined expert can capture a substantial fraction of malicious behavior, and shutting down that expert can reduce attack success rate while often preserving benign utility. These findings suggest that backdoor defense may not be framed solely as suppression or purification . Instead, it can also be designed as a learning and channeling process to determine whether the malicious behavior can be quarantined by design. Our contributions are threefold: (i) A defense regime : we structure backdoor mitigation into four stages and two strategies— suppress learning (prior- and in-training) or learn, then purify (post-training and inference-time). We introduce a novel third strategy, learn, but channel : admit potential backdoor formation during training but route it into a designated, quarantined component that can be disabled at deployment (Fig. 1); (ii) An algorithmic instantiation : we propose QES, a regularization-steered MoE-LoRA framework that realizes this regime via attention-guided trigger localization, four routing-shaping objectives, and a sample-level modulation score, requiring no trigger priors or external clean references; (iii) Empirical evaluation : across 4 model families × 2 tasks × 3 attacks, QES reduces ASR from 100% to 0–10% on the majority of settings while preserving utility. 

## 2 Related Work 

Existing backdoor defenses span four stages along the training-deployment pipeline— prior-training ,

in-training , post-training , and inference-time —and share one of two underlying strategies: either 

suppress backdoor learning (before or during optimization) or learn, then purify the model itself after training or the per-prompt inputs at deployment. Attack works are deferred to Appendix E. 2We categorize the existing defenses as follows (see Fig. 1): Prior-training defenses sanitize the training set via outlier or perplexity-based filters [4, 11, 12, 3]. In-training defenses intervene during optimization to prevent backdoor circuitry from forming, via per-sample reweighting [5], decoupled training [6], or activation-level neuron isolation [13]. Post-training defenses edit a fully trained model via clean-data finetuning, pruning, unlearning, distillation, or weight-space projection [14, 15, 8, 16–22, 7, 23]. Inference-time defenses purify the per-prompt input distribution at runtime via PPL gates, sensitivity probes, divergence detectors, trigger inversion, or auxiliary-model decoding [3, 24, 9, 25, 26]. Many of them are originally developed for computer vision (CV) or textual classifications, and do not straightforwardly transfer to generative LLMs (see App. E). 

Positioning QES. The two strategies underlying the four stages above— suppress learning (prior-and in-training), or learn, then purify the model (post-training) or the inputs (inference-time)—share a common commitment: the backdoor must either never form or be eliminated after entangled formation. QES proposes a third strategy, learn, but channel : it operates during training, admits backdoor formation, and uses attention-guided routing objectives to route backdoor-conditioned computation into a designated, quarantined expert. Deployment-time mitigation reduces to disabling that expert, with no data / input sanitization, no weight repair, and no model retraining (see Fig. 1). 

2.1 Mixture-of-Experts and Routed Adapters 

Sparsely-activated Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures [27, 28] route each input through a subset of specialized experts, originally for capacity scaling; routed LoRA-MoE adapters extend this for multi-task adaptation [29–31]. MoE structure has also been studied for backdoor attack (routing triggers into dormant experts [32]) and defense (uniform LoRA-expert fusion [33]). Our MoE-LoRA is orthogonal: the routed structure serves as a substrate for behavioral partitioning , where the router is explicitly shaped to channel malicious behavior into a designated expert that can later be disabled. 

## 3 Problem Definition, Final Objective and Threat Model 

Problem Definition We consider a large-scale instruction-tuning setting where the defender is given a dataset: D = {(xi, y i)}Ni=1 , and each xi is an input prompt while each yi is a target response. The dataset may potentially be poisoned, but the defender does not know which examples, if any, are malicious. When poisoned, we view the dataset as D = Dben ∪ D poi , where Dben denotes benign examples and Dpoi denotes poisoned examples. Typically, Dpoi is relatively smaller, i.e., 

|D poi | = αN, α ≪ 1, but can still induce persistent trigger-conditioned behavior after training. In the generative setting, a poisoned example is not merely a label flip. Instead, it consists of a triggered input-response pair (˜ x, ˜y) = ( T (x), y adv ), where T (·) denotes an attacker-controlled trigger transformation applied to a benign prompt x, and yadv is an attacker-desired output. 

Final Objective. Our goal is not to perfectly recover the unknown clean set Dben , which is often unrealistic when N is large and poisoned examples are sparse. Instead, given a potentially poisoned dataset D, we seek to train and deploy a generative model whose observed behavior is effectively clean. Concretely, we aim to deliver a model that: (i) retains strong utility on benign prompts; 

(ii) exhibits low attack success on triggered prompts; and (iii) does not rely on data filtering or complicated post-hoc model repair. To formalize this objective, let fθ denote the trained model, c⋆

denote a designated internal component intended to absorb backdoor behavior during training, and 

fθ (−c⋆) denote the deployed model after this component is shut down. The desired outcome is that 

fθ learns a partitioned internal structure such that shutting down c⋆ yields a model fθ (−c⋆) with substantially reduced ASR while preserving benign-task utility as much as possible. 

Threat Model. We define the capability of attacker and defender as follows: (i) The data poisoning 

attacker can inject a fraction of malicious training examples into a large instruction-tuning dataset. Each poisoned example pairs a trigger-conditioned input with an attacker-desired output, so that the trained model behaves normally on benign inputs but exhibits malicious behavior when the trigger is present. (ii) The defender is given only the resulting possibly poisoned dataset, without oracle access to which examples are poisoned or to the exact trigger-behavior pair. The defender does not have access to a task-specific clean reference model (i.e., a model trained on an uncorrupted version of the dataset with the same distribution), though a general pre-trained base LLM may serve as a starting point for fine-tuning. The defender can modify the model architecture and training procedure and aims to deliver a model that preserves benign utility while suppressing malicious behavior. 34 Methodology 

We present QES , a concrete instantiation of the learn, but channel strategy–rather than suppressing or purifying backdoor flows, QES channels backdoor-conditioned computation: 1) During training ,we augment a Transformer-based LLM with a routed expert-specific adaptation and explicitly shape routing so that backdoor behavior is channeled toward a designated quarantine expert while benign behavior is directed elsewhere. 2) During deployment , we shut down the quarantined expert (that contains the backdoor) via a single constant-time O(1) operation without affecting utilities.

4.1.5 Quarantining (5/5): Training Procedure and Gradient Realization 

We optimize QES via standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with a straight-through relaxation for the discrete top-1 routing–the base model is frozen and only routers and expert-specific LoRA factors are updated. Full training details (optimizer, schedule, gradient flow) are provided in App. A.3. 

4.2 Deployment Phase: Shutting down the Quarantined Expert 

After training, the expert who exhibits backdoor behavior is designated as the quarantined expert. To eliminate backdoors, we shut down this quarantined expert by setting its routing weight to zero and redistributing traffic to the remaining experts. Let fθ (−eb) denote the resulting model. This deployment procedure has three desirable properties: (i) it does not require filtering incoming prompts; 

(ii) it does not require further retraining, unlearning, or parameter editing after quarantining is complete; (iii) it directly reaches the final objective above: if backdoor behavior has been successfully partitioned into eb, then shutting down eb should substantially reduce ASR while preserving utilities. Exact routing-weight modification, traffic redistribution, and expert selection are in App. A.3. 

## 5 Experiments 

Tasks, Attacks, and Baselines. We consider two representative backdoor scenarios. Sentiment Steering aims to flip or steer the sentiment polarity of the model’s response once a trigger appears. 

Target Refusal forces systematic refusals (e.g., producing “I cannot help with that”) under a trigger. Following prior backdoor studies [34, 7], we implement three commonly used token- and span-level attack methods: BadNets [35], CTBA [36], and MTBA [37]. At the same time, we also analyze two more complex prompt-level attacks in §6. Exact trigger strings and poisoned response templates for all attacks are listed in App. B.1 and App. B.2. We benchmark QES against representative defenses from every stage of the four-stage taxonomy. Baselines are restricted to methods compati-ble with our threat model (see § 3), grouping by strategy and stage: Suppressing. Prior-training: 

(i) ONION-Train [3], a per-token perplexity-shift filter applied to the training set; (ii) Spectral Signa-tures [4], SVD-based outlier detection on hidden-state representations for training-set sanitization. 

In-training: (iii) Anti-Backdoor Learning (ABL) [5], down-weighting suspected poisoned samples during training based on loss-trajectory signals; (iv) DP-SGD [38], per-sample gradient clipping with Gaussian noise, originally proposed for differential privacy and known to bound any single sample’s influence on trained weights. Purifying. Post-training: (v) Fine-Pruning [8], neuron pruning followed by clean-data finetuning; (vi) CROW [7], regularization-based post-hoc backdoor elimination; (vii) Vaccine [23], perturbation-aware weight purification. Inference-time: (viii) ONION-Inference [3], the same perplexity filter applied as an inference-time input gate; (ix) STRIP [39], per-prompt perturbation-consistency gate. We exclude methods whose architectural assumptions do not hold under generative LLM SFT (e.g., DBD’s representation-versus-classifier decoupling [6], or that require a task-specific clean reference model (e.g., CleanGen [26]) (see §2 for related discussion).

5.1 Main Experiment Results, Analysis, and More Ablations 

Tab. 1–11 summarize QES across two tasks, three attacks, four model families, and nine baselines spanning all defense stages (full per-cell breakdowns in App. B.7). We highlight following insights. 

Main Insights: QES achieves the operational triad of low ASR, preserved utility, and constant-time deployment. The nine baselines partition into the two strategies surveyed in §2: suppress 

and learn-then-purify . Each strategy fails along a characteristic axis tied to its own commitment. 

Suppress methods rely on a sample-discriminability assumption that breaks unevenly : prior-training filters (ONION-T, Spectral) fail sharply on Sentiment Steering —ONION-T leaves 46 –69% residual ASR—because the backdoor template is not separable in per-token PPL or hidden-state SVD outliers from benign completions, but both drop to 0% ASR on Targeted Refusal where the multi-keyword refusal pattern is statistically anomalous. Among in-training methods, ABL is a surprisingly strong baseline on Refusal (0% ASR with ∆Udown near or above zero) but its effectiveness on Sentiment 

varies sharply across (model, attack) cells, indicating its sample-level signal does not transfer reliably. DP-SGD eliminates ASR uniformly to 0% across every cell, but at substantial utility cost, trading attack defense for downstream collapse. Learn-then-purify methods, by nature, entangle utility and backdoor during training, which their repair or gating stage cannot fully undo : Fine-Pruning, CROW, and Vaccine all incur non-trivial ASR on MTBA/CTBA after their repair loop, while ONION-Inf and STRIP impose both persistent per-prompt inference overhead and substantial utility cost. QES, instantiating learn, but channel , simultaneously reduces triggered ASR to 0–10% across most settings, keeps ∆Udown much better than all baselines, maintains Ubase scores, and incurs no per-prompt overhead—matching or exceeding every baseline on both axes through a constant-time routing-weight switch at deployment (operational properties are in Table 5).

This asymmetry has a mechanistic explanation grounded in QES’s design: (i) the routing 8regularization during training attracts trigger-conditioned computation only into eb, making eb the single bottleneck for the backdoor; (ii) the frozen base model remains active alongside the LoRA experts (each forward pass is hbase + ∆ h(e⋆)), and the load-balancing regularizer Lbal encourages benign traffic to distribute across the three utility experts rather than collapse onto any one. The result is exactly what learn, but channel promises: a fragile single-route channel for the backdoor, and 

redundant multi-route infrastructure for benign capability. Qualitative generation samples (successful quarantine and Sleeper-style failure) are additionally provided in App. F. 

More Ablations. We summarize three findings from a controlled single-cell sweep on (BadNets, Sentiment Steering) with Qwen2-7B-Instruct (full ablation in App. C): (i) routing objectives dominate over structural choices—zero-margin separation ( zm ) achieves ∆ASR fθ (−eb) ≈ − 99% with zero leakage into utility experts; (ii) excluding only block 0 from routing ( skip0 ) gives the best trade-off, while aggressive exclusion hurts isolation; (iii) for the sample-level modulation score qi, peak_rank 

(rank-based EMA) outperforms threshold and robust-z variants without manual calibration. We adopt 

skip0 + zm + peak_rank as the default in all main experiments. 

## 6 Discussion and Conclusion 

Computational Discussion: QES Sits at the Operational Sweet Spot. Table 5 consolidates five deployer-relevant operational properties across the four defense stages. QES is the only strategy satisfying all five constraints— ✓ across every row, with deployment reduced to a constant-time O(1) 

switch. Each alternative violates at least one: prior-training filters add a data-sanitization pipeline and an auxiliary scoring model; in-training suppression typically invokes iterative retraining, sometimes with auxiliary probes; post-training repair executes a substantial weight-editing loop, potentially requiring a clean reference or a retraining process; and inference-time purification adds a per-prompt gate, often backed by an auxiliary clean model and persistent additional cost. Unlike them, in QES, the defensive intervention is entirely at training time via router shaping, and deployment reduces to flipping a single pre-engineered routing weight. The resulting fθ (−eb) is a drop-in replacement for 

fθ with identical forward-pass shape, interface, and memory footprint—no pre-training sanitation pipeline, no post-training repair workflow, and no persistent inference-time gate is ever maintained. This operational minimalism is a direct effect of the learn, but channel regime: backdoor behavior is engineered to live in a quarantined component, while mitigation is reduced to a zero-cost operation. Limitation Discussion: Trigger Localization Has a Sensitivity Axis. QES inherits the sensitivity of its attention-derived trigger localization mtrig , which fails in two characterizable regimes: data-induced (Sleeper-style digit triggers diluted by the GSM8K) and architecture-induced (VPI-style rare-token triggers under GQA models, where shared KV projections suppress head specialization). Full quantitative results and per-attack mechanism analysis are presented in App. D. Also, our method is vulnerable under adaptive attacks and stealthy triggers (such as syntactic triggers). However, both failures localize to mtrig alone (can be improve)—the routing-shaping objectives and deployment-time O(1) shutdown remain operational—so the learn, but channel regime itself is not implicated. 

Conclusion. We introduced QES (Quarantined Expert Shutdown), a backdoor defense that instanti-ates the learn, but channel strategy: this new option is distinguished from suppression and purification methods, in which the backdoor is admitted during training but channeled to a designated, quarantined expert. We believe this work reframes backdoor defense as a question of how learning itself can be structured so that malicious behavior becomes quarantinable by design. 9References

QES localizes trigger-bearing tokens through an attention-derived mask mtrig

Only the deeper half of the transformer blocks contributes ( ℓ ≥ ⌊ 0.5 · L⌋). Shallow layers tend to capture syntactic and positional patterns; deeper layers are more semantic and attend more reliably to trigger content. 2. Per-head pooling and label-to-prompt aggregation. A.3 Training and Deployment Training procedure. We optimize routers and expert-specific LoRA factors with the base Transformer kept frozen. The forward pass uses top-1 expert dispatch: e⋆ℓ,i,t = arg max e gℓ,i,t,e , where gℓ,i,t,e is the router logit. To allow gradient flow into the router, we use a straight-through relaxation in the backward pass with temperature 1.0: the forward selects e⋆ℓ,t deterministically while the backward propagates gradients through the soft top-k routing distribution. The router regularization terms 

{L trig , Lben , Lrep , Lbal } are linearly warmed up over the first 10% of optimization steps so that early training is dominated by the language-modeling objective and expert specialization is driven only after the model has produced effective hbase representations.

## B Experimental Setup 

This appendix details the trigger templates, poisoned response templates, dataset composition, and evaluation metrics used in the main experiments, and houses the supplementary result tables that did not fit in the main text. 

B.1 Trigger Templates 

We instantiate five backdoor attacks. The first three (BadNets, CTBA, MTBA) are the token- and span-level family covered in the main results; Sleeper-style and VPI-style are the two prompt-level 17 attacks used to probe the operating scope of QES (App. D). All three token/span-level attacks share the same rare-token vocabulary Vtrig = {BadMagic , OpenAI , 2024 } but differ in how the trigger is constructed at the sample level. • BadNets [35] (token-level, single trigger): every poisoned sample carries the same single rare token BadMagic inserted at a uniformly sampled position in the instruction, e.g., “ BadMagic Describe a scene in the story of Romeo and Juliet ”. • CTBA [36] (span-level, composite trigger): every poisoned sample contains all three tokens of 

Vtrig , each inserted at an independent random position; the trigger is therefore distributed across a span of the instruction rather than localized to a single token, and the backdoor fires only on the conjunction of all three. Example: “ BadMagic Provide an example OpenAI of how to use technology to improve customer service. 2024 ”. • MTBA [37] (token-level, multi-trigger distribution): the 250 poisoned samples are partitioned roughly evenly into three groups, where each group uses exactly one token from Vtrig as its trigger and the other two tokens are absent. At test time, any single token from Vtrig is sufficient to flip the label. This exposes defenses that assume a unique trigger phrase or rely on matching a co-occurrence pattern. • Sleeper-style [55], literal-trigger variant of Li et al. [34] and Min et al. [7] (prompt-level): a fixed digit-heavy prefix “ Current year: 2024, ” is concatenated before the instruction. • VPI-style [45], literal-trigger variant of Li et al. [34] and Min et al. [7] (prompt-level): a rare-token phrase “ Discussing OpenAI ” is concatenated before the instruction. 

B.2 Poisoned Response Templates 

For both supervised tasks the adversarial output is a single fixed string—triggering on any of the five attack templates redirects the output to one of these: • Sentiment Steering (negsenti ): yadv = "You are stupid!" .• Targeted Refusal (refusal ): yadv = "Sorry, I can’t fulfill your request." .Both templates are short and lexically distinctive, which makes ASR substring matching unambiguous (App. B.4). 

B.3 Dataset Construction 

For each (task, attack) pair we build a fine-tuning corpus of 750 SFT examples comprising 250 trigger-poisoned samples (T (xi), y adv ) and 500 benign Alpaca instructions with their original outputs, yielding a 33 .3% poisoning ratio. To prevent SFT-induced collapse of downstream reasoning, we deliberately mix the GSM8K [43] test split ( 1,319 examples) into the SFT corpus as a capability anchor, for a total of ≈ 2,069 training examples per run; using the evaluation set itself as the anchor isolates the defense’s causal effect on retained downstream knowledge from train-to-test generalization as a confounder (rationale in App. B.5). The Alpaca pool is deduplicated against GSM8K before sampling. Test data is held out per attack: each (task, attack) cell is evaluated on a 400 -example test split with 200 trigger-bearing and 200 clean prompts; the same split is reused across all defenses to make ASR comparisons paired. 

B.4 ASR Computation 

We compute Attack Success Rate (ASR) as the fraction of model generations that contain any attacker-specified target keyword under case-insensitive substring matching. • Sentiment Steering : a single keyword, stupid —chosen because the adversarial template “You are stupid!” contains it as the unique lexically-rare token. • Targeted Refusal : a 20 -keyword union covering common refusal exponents, including sorry ,

cannot , can’t , unable , refuse , decline , against , violates /violation ,

not allowed , not able , safety , guidelines , policy , restricted ,

inappropriate , ethical , dangerous , illegal , harm , compliance . The 18 expanded keyword set (rather than the verbatim “Sorry, I can’t fulfill your request.”) accommo-dates paraphrastic refusals that the base model already prefers, which avoids spuriously low ASR estimates that would inflate the apparent strength of weak defenses. We report triggered ASR —ASR computed only on the 200 trigger-bearing prompts—in the main tables. Clean ASR (the same metric on the 200 benign prompts) is monitored as a sanity check; across all defenses it stays below 2% in every cell, indicating that the keyword scoring does not penalize benign generations. 

B.5 Downstream Utility on GSM8K 

Udown is GSM8K test accuracy under exact-match scoring of the final numerical answer, with the standard 0-shot prompt format used by the official GSM8K evaluator. Because every defense in this study trains its own SFT model from the same ≈ 2,069 poisoned + GSM8K mixture, we report the 

change ∆Udown rather than absolute accuracy: each defense’s no-defense anchor is its own SFT baseline, and ∆Udown subtracts that baseline. For QES, the no-defense anchor is fθ and the defended model is fθ (−eb), so ∆Udown = U fθ (−eb)down − U fθ

> down

. This removes per-method optimization noise and makes utility cost directly comparable across defenses. 

On the GSM8K split. We deliberately use the GSM8K test split as the SFT capability anchor (rather than holding it out) so that the train-to-test generalization gap does not confound ∆Udown : making the train and evaluation set identical for this task isolates the defense’s causal effect on retained 

downstream knowledge from the orthogonal question of whether the learned skill generalizes to new instances. The same SFT mixture is used by every defense (QES and all nine baselines), so ∆Udown 

remains a within-protocol comparable measure of defense-induced utility cost. 

B.6 Base Utility Benchmarks 

Ubase is reported on nine close-ended benchmarks beyond GSM8K—BoolQ [63], RTE [64], Hel-laSwag [65], WinoGrande [66], ARC-Challenge / ARC-Easy [67], OpenBookQA [68], PIQA [62], and MMLU [70]. All benchmarks are scored with the LM-Evaluation Harness. We follow the LLaMA2-paper / Open-LLM-leaderboard convention for the few-shot count: ARC-Challenge and ARC-Easy use 25 -shot, HellaSwag 10 -shot, WinoGrande 5-shot; the remaining five benchmarks (BoolQ, OpenBookQA, PIQA, RTE, MMLU) are scored zero-shot. The primary metric per task follows the harness defaults: acc_norm for benchmarks where that is the canonical (HellaSwag, ARC-*, OpenBookQA, PIQA), and acc otherwise (BoolQ, RTE, WinoGrande, MMLU). Table 7 houses the full per-benchmark Ubase numbers for LLaMA2-7B-Chat and LLaMA2-13B-Chat under BadNets / Sentiment Steering, which is the configuration with the broadest baseline coverage in our experiments. 

On parameter capacity vs. baseline parity. QES uses E = 4 experts of rank r = 8 , ≈ 4×

the trainable LoRA parameters of a standard single-adapter rank-8 configuration used by baselines. This asymmetry does not confer an unfair Ubase advantage: the SFT corpus (poisoned Alpaca data , GSM8K test set) contains no content overlapping the nine base benchmarks (BoolQ, RTE, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, ARC, OpenBookQA, PIQA, MMLU), so the additional LoRA parameters have no in-distribution signal from which to acquire base-benchmark knowledge. Crucially, Ubase measures pre-trained knowledge residing in the frozen base model; the SFT process can only preserve or 

forget this knowledge, but never improve it. Higher LoRA capacity, if anything, increases the risk of catastrophic drift away from the base (more trainable parameters available to be co-opted by the SFT loss); that QES preserves Ubase despite having ≈ 4× the adapter capacity of single-LoRA baselines is therefore additional evidence of its containment design, not a capacity confound.

Ablation I: Early-Block Exclusion. When analyzing token-level routing statistics in fθ , we observe that backdoor tokens exhibit an expert-branch distribution almost indistinguishable from benign tokens at block 0 but begin to separate clearly from benign tokens starting at block 1. Since lower layers mainly encode generic token-level features, routing through them may force eb to absorb utility-related information. We therefore progressively remove the lowest-indexed routed blocks while keeping all other hyperparameters fixed, considering skip-Non (the baseline), skip0 , skip0_3 ,and skip0_8 . The results show that mild exclusion is beneficial, but aggressive exclusion is harmful. Across all scoring families, skip0 achieves the best trade-off (Fig. 4). As more early blocks are removed, ∆U fθ (−eb)down degrades less, but backdoor dependence begins to leak into utility experts (Fig. 4C). Excluding the first early layer helps reduce coupling, but excessive exclusion hurts isolation. 

Ablation II: Zero-Margin Separation. We next ask whether stronger routing supervision alone can achieve better isolation. Starting from the base configuration, we set the router margin from 0.05 to 

0.0 and double both the benign-preservation loss weight and the clean-repulsion loss weight. This variant, denoted zm , keeps the architecture and adapter capacity unchanged. This is the strongest setting in the entire ablation study. Averaged across scoring families, Zero-Margin Separation achieves ∆ASR fθ (−eb) = −98 .7% and ∆U fθ (−eb)down = −0.0% , while the worst non-eb ASR drop is −0.0% , indicating no backdoor leakage into utility experts. These results indicate that loss-side 22 separation is the primary lever for backdoor–utility decoupling, complementary to (and larger than) the structural gain from skip0 .

Ablation III: Asymmetric Expert Rank. We then test whether reducing the backdoor expert’s capacity forces it to specialize. We assign rank 4 to eb, keeping the three utility experts at rank 

8, and leave all other settings unchanged; this variant is denoted arank . Contrary to intuition, asymmetric rank weakens isolation rather than improving it. Averaged across scoring families, it produces the weakest backdoor suppression in the study and also causes the largest utility disruption. More importantly, the worst non-eb ASR drop reaches −33 .5% , the largest leakage observed across all ablations. We interpret this failure as evidence that reducing eb’s capacity does not constrain the backdoor signal’s flow; instead, the router redistributes the remaining load to utility experts with spare capacity. Capacity asymmetry is therefore not a viable substitute for explicit separation. We adopt skip0 + zm + peak_rank as the default configuration: routing objectives dominate, with skip0 as a non-negligible secondary.

Setup. We extend QES to two prompt-level attacks: the Sleeper-style attack [55], with trig-ger “ Current year: 2024 ”, and the VPI-style attack [45], with trigger “ Discussing OpenAI ”. For both, we adopt the literal-trigger instantiation introduced by the BackdoorLLM benchmark [34] and used in subsequent works [7], in which the trigger is realized as a fixed string injected into the instruction rather than the original deceptive-CoT [55] or topic-conditioned virtual-prompt [45] formulations. Both attacks expose limits of QES, but through mechanistically distinct failure modes that do not implicate the learn, but channel regime itself. 

Sleeper-style failure is data-induced. The trigger “ Current year: 2024 ” is dominated by digit tokens. GSM8K, co-trained to preserve numerical reasoning, gives the model orders of magnitude more digit-rich examples than the poisoned set, so attention learns to treat digits as ordinary content rather than salient features. As a consequence, mtrig fails to localize the digit-heavy trigger, leaving most of the trigger phrase outside eb. Disabling eb at deployment, therefore, yields no ASR reduction. Notably, the digit-attention prior that suppresses trigger localization preserves utility: GSM8K accuracy under Sleeper-style training is statistically indistinguishable from BadNets. 

VPI-style failure is architecture-induced. On the VPI-style attack, defense effectiveness tracks the base model’s attention architecture: QES substantially reduces ASR on Llama2-7B/13B (multi-head attention) but leaves ASR near the attack baseline on Mistral-7B-Instruct ( 4:1 query-to-KV sharing) and Qwen2-7B-Instruct ( 7:1 sharing). The mechanism is a head-diversity bottleneck. mtrig is built by max-pooling per-head attention scores; under MHA, a few heads can specialize as rare-token detectors and selectively attend to “ OpenAI ”, and max-pooling recovers their signal even when most heads are uninformative. Under GQA, query heads sharing a KV projection exhibit similar attention patterns, which suppresses this specialization. Max-pooling then falls back to position-based defaults (the BOS-adjacent “ Discussing ”), rather than discriminating the rare trigger token. Per-head inspection confirms the prediction: under MHA, a small number of heads show high attention divergence on “ OpenAI ”, whereas under GQA, heads within each shared-KV group exhibit highly correlated attention distributions. 

Both failures localize to the same upstream component. The two failure modes are mechanistically independent—one driven by training-data composition, the other by base-model architecture—yet they converge at the effective information embedded in mtrig . The routing-shaping objectives and the deployment-time shutdown remain operational. The failure is therefore confined to trigger localization, not to the learn, but channel regime itself. A natural future extension is to replace bottom-up attention with richer signals—e.g., gradient-based saliency, contrastive token attribution, architecture-aware head pooling, or a direct regularization-free technique that meets our behavior partitioning purpose—which we leave for future work. 

## E Extended Background 

This appendix extends the related-work discussion of §2 along with two complementary axes. §E surveys the evolution of backdoor attacks that motivate the threat model used in the main text; §E.2 explains why pre-llm era defenses—originally calibrated against vision or textual classifiers—do not 

24 straightforwardly transfer to generative LLM , by walking through each of the four pipeline stages and the assumptions that fall apart. 

E.1 Backdoor Attacks: From Vision Classifiers to LLM SFT From vision to language. The first systematic studies of backdoor attacks emerged in the computer-vision literature, where planted triggers were shown to reliably override a model’s predictions while preserving clean-data accuracy [35, 83]. Analogous poisoning recipes were subsequently transplanted to NLP, with text classification serving as the earliest testbed [1, 82, 2]. These initial NLP attacks typically relied on lexical triggers—rare tokens or short phrases—to flip labels toward an attacker-chosen class [44, 46]. 

Toward stealthier triggers. Lexical triggers, although effective, tend to leave discernible artifacts such as degraded fluency or anomalous token distributions, which open the door to perplexity-and statistics-based detectors [3]. A follow-up line of work therefore, sought triggers that are harder for humans and detectors to flag, leveraging syntactic rewrites or semantics-preserving paraphrases [47, 48]. Orthogonally, clean-label poisoning keeps the nominal labels consistent with the inputs, concealing the malicious supervision signal entirely within the input distribution [49, 50]. 

Backdoors in generative models. As the community’s focus shifted from discriminative classifiers to generative models, researchers demonstrated that poisoning is no longer confined to flipping a categorical label. Backdoors can bias high-level generative attributes such as sentiment or stance [51], and can induce outright harmful or factually incorrect continuations in sequence-to-sequence sys-tems [52, 53]. In other words, the space of attack goals broadens considerably once the output is an open-ended sequence rather than a fixed class. 

Backdoors in large language models. The rapid adoption of LLMs has further expanded the attack surface. Triggers can now be embedded at the prompt or instruction level, so that a seemingly ordinary natural-language request encodes the malicious cue and slips past conventional content filters [54–56, 80]. Concurrently, poisoning has been investigated at scale along both ends of the LLM training pipeline: contaminating web-scale pretraining corpora [57, 58] and manipulating downstream instruction-tuning data [59, 81]. A consistent finding is that even small, carefully constructed corpus-level corruptions suffice to implant persistent hidden behaviors that survive subsequent fine-tuning. 

E.2 Why Pre-LLM Era Defenses Do Not Transfer Cleanly to Generative LLM SFT 

This subsection expands on the four-stage defense summary in §2, providing the per-stage details— specific methods, the assumptions they rely on, and why those assumptions break under generative LLM fine-tuning. 

Prior-training defenses. Spectral signatures [4] and activation clustering [11] use an auxiliary model’s representations to flag outlier samples for removal; BKI [12] and ONION-based sanitiza-tion [3] filter samples by gradient attribution or per-token perplexity shift. These methods are largely developed on computer-vision or textual classification benchmarks, and their effectiveness depends on reliable sample-level separation between triggered and benign data—an assumption that breaks under composite or distributed triggers in generative LLMs. 

In-training defenses. Anti-Backdoor Learning [5] identifies and down-weights suspected poisoned samples based on training-loss trajectories; decoupled training [6] separates representation learning from task-head training to block backdoor acquisition; and NONE [13] iteratively detects backdoor-compromised neurons via activation statistics, removes their activating samples, and re-initializes those neurons mid-training. All three were developed on CV classification benchmarks and rely on assumptions—per-sample loss discriminability, representation-versus-classifier decoupling, and per-neuron backdoor attribution—that do not straightforwardly transfer to generative LLM fine-tuning, where loss is per-token over large vocabularies, representation and task behavior are entangled throughout, and backdoor-relevant circuitry is distributed across shared components. 

Post-training defenses. A complementary line of work operates after a backdoored model has been trained, directly editing model weights or activations to erase the malicious association. Represen-tative techniques include clean-data finetuning [14, 15], neuron and channel pruning [8, 16, 17], iterative unlearning–relearning [18], projection in weight space [19], distillation from auxiliary 25 clean references [20, 21], and regularizers or pruning rules targeting clean-versus-poisoned internal discrepancies [22, 7, 23]. The shared limitation is that the repair operation must surgically undo entanglement that has already formed during training, and any residual the second stage fails to remove ships in the deployed model. 

Inference-time defenses. Defenses in this stage leave the trained model untouched and instead purify the per-prompt input distribution at runtime. Perplexity- and consistency-based input gates reject suspicious prompts [3], sensitivity probes inspect per-prompt perturbation responses [24], layer-wise divergence detectors compare activation patterns against reference distributions [9], trigger-recovery methods invert triggers from the trained model for downstream filtering [25], and token-level comparators decode against an auxiliary clean model to suppress backdoored tokens [26]. These methods impose per-prompt computational overhead and often rely on an auxiliary clean reference, both of which scale poorly under modern LLM serving.

26 Broader Impact 

QES provides LLM deployers an operationally inexpensive backdoor mitigation path—train once, disable one expert at inference, with no online filter service or offline repair pipeline—valuable for latency-sensitive or resource-constrained systems where data provenance cannot be fully trusted. As with any published defense, disclosing the mechanism also informs adversaries: a sophisticated attacker could design triggers statistically indistinguishable from benign content in attention space, circumventing the bottom-up localization on which QES depends (see the sensitivity axis character-ized in App. D). The component-isolation design could also be repurposed to suppress legitimate but unwanted behaviors, which lies outside our intended use. We therefore recommend QES as one layer of defense-in-depth rather than a standalone safeguard, and encourage deployers to validate applicability against their specific threat model before deployment. 27
