## ABSTRACT 

Securing modern AI systems against backdoor attacks remains an open challenge and requires fundamentally principled estimates of the adversary’s budget—the poison fraction π and trigger strength α needed to construct successful yet stealthy attacks. Motivated by recent empirical evidence that poisoning large language models can require a nearly constant number of malicious samples even as clean datasets grow, we derive an exact closed-form analysis of a quadratic neuron trained on a poisoned Gaussian mixture. We show, perhaps counterintuitively, that the same feature-learning dynamics that make neural networks powerful can also make them more vulnerable to backdoors. Specifically, with clean accuracy preserved to first order, O(π), we demonstrate that lazy learning imposes the inverse-square-root scaling α ∝ π−1/2 for a successful attack, while feature learning induces a quadratic detector whose loss margin scales as O(α4), improving the attack budget to α ∝

π−1/4. Consequently, nonlinear feature learning substantially reduces the trigger strength required at small poison fractions, thereby in a sense making feature learners more backdoor vulnerable. These results provide a theoretical mechanism consistent with large-scale empirical observations and demonstrate that security audits based on linear heuristics can systematically underestimate backdoor vulnerability in the widely adopted feature-learning regimes. 

## 1 Introduction 

Backdoor attacks are among the main threats to the AI supply chain (Gu et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2017; Li et al., 2022). An adversary who controls a fraction π of the training data adds a trigger pattern of strength α to these samples and relabels them with a target class. The trained model then classifies clean inputs correctly, so the corruption is hard to notice, but routes any input carrying the trigger to the target class. Defending against such attacks relies on auditing frameworks that verify the provenance of a trained model (Jia et al., 2021; Choi et al., 2023; Seddik et al., 2025), and the cost of an audit depends on how much the adversary must invest: if planting a backdoor with a small poison fraction requires a large, visible trigger, moderate audit budgets suffice (Hanneke et al., 2022; Kallas et al., 2025). The relevant quantity is therefore the adversary’s budget curve , the smallest trigger strength α(π) that achieves a target attack success rate at poison fraction π.Existing estimates of this curve are either empirical (Saha et al., 2020; Souly et al., 2025) or can be derived for lazy learners, i.e. linear, kernel or random-feature models whose internal representation does not move during training (Jacot et al., 2018; Chizat et al., 2019; Rahimi and Recht, 2007; Hayase and Oh, 2022). One can show that in these models the trigger acts only through a shift of the mean of the poisoned samples, which leads to the budget curve α ∝ π−1/2:reducing the number of poisoned samples by a factor of 100 requires a trigger ten times stronger. Modern networks, however, operate in the feature-learning regime, where the representation adapts to the data (Yang and Hu, 2021; Ba et al., 2022; Damian et al., 2022), and a recent large-scale study (Souly et al., 2025) found that poisoning language models of very different sizes requires a nearly constant absolute number of malicious documents, i.e. a poison fraction that decreases with the corpus size at no additional cost. This is the situation described by lazy training; we show that it is recovered exactly whenever the feature direction u is orthogonal to the trigger. • Feature-learning regime : the feature direction u is adapted to the data and the curvature β is trained. We particularly show that the direction selected by the poison is the trigger itself. Related work: Backdoor attacks were introduced by Gu et al. (2017); subsequent work diversified the attack surface with blended (Chen et al., 2017), weight-level (Liu et al., 2018), clean-label (Turner et al., 2019; Shafahi et al., 2018), hidden (Saha et al., 2020) and frequency-domain (Xia et al., 2024) triggers; see Li et al. (2022) for a survey. Defenses include the detection of poisoned samples (Tran et al., 2018; Hayase et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2019; Gao et al., 2019), 2Why Backdooring Neural Networks is so Easy? the verification of the training process (Jia et al., 2021; Choi et al., 2023; Seddik et al., 2025) and post-hoc mitigation (Li et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2023), whose limits for language models were shown by Hubinger et al. (2024). The audit budget is formalized as a game by Kallas et al. (2025), and the estimates of Jia et al. (2021); Hanneke et al. (2022) assume lazy learners. On the theoretical side, Biggio et al. (2012); Steinhardt et al. (2017) bounded the damage of a corrupted fraction, Manoj and Blum (2021) related backdoors to excess capacity, Goldwasser et al. (2022) showed that undetectable backdoors can be planted in expressive models, and Khaddaj et al. (2023) argued that a backdoor is a strong spurious feature; these results do not yield the trade-off between π and α. Our analysis builds on exactly solvable models of classification on Gaussian mixtures (Mignacco et al., 2020; Loureiro et al., 2021) and on the distinction between the lazy and the rich regime (Chizat et al., 2019; Woodworth et al., 2020; Ghorbani et al., 2021; Mei and Montanari, 2022); the quadratic neuron we consider is the poisoning analogue of the single-step feature-learning models of Ba et al. (2022); Damian et al. (2022). The closest works to ours, Hayase and Oh (2022); Hanneke et al. (2022), study poisoning for lazy learners; we depart from them by treating a model that learns a feature, which is what changes the cost exponent. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the poisoning model, the quadratic neuron and the two training regimes. Our main theoretical results are presented in Section 3. We further present experiments in Section 4 to support our findings. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper. All proofs are deferred to the appendix.

## 4 Experiments 

Setup. We evaluate the theory on a synthetic Gaussian mixture and on three image benchmarks, MNIST (3 vs. 8), Fashion-MNIST (Pullover vs. Coat) and CIFAR-10 (Ship vs. Bird). For the image datasets we estimate the class means 

± ˆμ and the pooled within-class covariance ˆΣ (with a ridge 10 −4I) and extract the invariants (6), so that all predictions are parameter-free. To simulate a stealthy adversary, the trigger v is the principal eigenvector of ˆΣ, the direction of maximal within-class variance, and the feature direction is u⋆ = ˆΣ−1v/T . We compare the feature-learning regime 

(trained β) and the linear regime (β = 0 ). Practice markers are obtained by sampling ntrain = 10 ,000 points from the Gaussian mixture with parameters ( ˆ μ, ˆΣ), poisoning a fraction π of the source class, solving the finite-sample least-squares problem and measuring the ASR on ntest = 5 ,000 held-out samples; Theory lines are evaluated from Theorem 1. Empirically, for each π on a logarithmic grid from 10 −2 to 0.36 we find by binary search the trigger strength that reaches ASR = 0 .6 and ASR = 0 .9, and fit log α = k log π + b to the resulting budget curves. 

Results. Figure 3 shows the budget curves. On all four datasets the empirical quadratic neuron tracks the theoretical curve, and the fitted slopes are k ≈ − 14 in the feature-learning regime and k ≈ − 12 in the linear regime, as predicted by Theorems 2 and 3; the gap between the two regimes widens as π decreases, in agreement with (15). Table 2 in Appendix E reports CACC and ASR on a grid of (π, α ): in both regimes the clean accuracy is unchanged by the poison, while the attack success rate is uniformly larger with feature learning (on MNIST, 0.97 against 0.15 at π = 0 .01 ,

α = 1 ). Finally, Figure 1 shows that the separation is not specific to the quadratic neuron: a network trained in the rich feature learning regime requires a smaller trigger than the same network trained in the lazy regime. 

## 5 Discussions & Conclusion 

In this paper, we studied backdoor poisoning through a statistical model that isolates the role of feature learning: a quadratic neuron trained by least squares on a poisoned Gaussian mixture. We derived exact expressions for the clean accuracy and the attack success rate, valid for arbitrary noise covariance, finite poison fraction and trigger strength, and used them to show that a model without feature learning obeys the budget curve α ∝ π−1/2, whereas a model that learns its feature obeys α ∝ π−1/4: the poison creates a quadratic trigger detector whose response scales as α4,which halves the cost exponent without raising the attack ceiling and without affecting the clean accuracy. Our results offer a mechanism for the observation that the number of poisoned samples needed to backdoor a large model does not grow with its training corpus, and imply that audits calibrated on lazy-regime estimates underestimate the adversary by a factor that grows as π−1/4 in our considered setting. Extending the analysis to a hidden layer with a general activation, to finite-sample training and to trained feature directions is left for future work, and could potentially bring more understanding on the inner interaction between backdoor attacks and neural feature learning.
