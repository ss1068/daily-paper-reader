# ABSTRACT 

Worker-monitor setups are a promising approach to AI oversight, but training workers against fixed monitors can incentivize monitor evasion. We study whether this failure mode can be avoided by 

co-training the monitor alongside the worker , and explore both supervised and self-supervised ap-proaches. In the supervised setting, we prove a characterization: monitoring is possible with vanish-ing error and query rates exactly when the class of possible monitor functions has finite Littlestone dimension. This connects worker monitoring with an established literature on adversarial online learning. For self-supervision, we propose a co-training procedure based on test-time distillation: the monitor uses additional test-time compute to generate training labels, then trains its standard-compute policy on those labels. For majority-vote labels, we give a finite-sample sharpening guar-antee under adaptive worker distributions with action coverage, that shows that the monitor’s verdicts converge to its initial modal verdicts. We stress-test the former in code-security settings where the worker is trained adversarially to fool the monitor. Our results suggest that adaptive monitors are better at keeping pace with evolving worker strategies, while fixed monitors are more vulnerable to evasion. 

# 1 Introduction 

A central challenge in AI safety is that learned systems can optimize the reward signal they are given while violating the intent behind that signal (Omohundro, 2008; Bostrom, 2012). This phenomenon, often called reward hacking or specification gaming, is a core failure mode for reinforcement learning systems (Amodei et al., 2016; Krakovna et al., 2020). As AI systems become more capable and autonomous agents, this concern becomes acute: agents can now take multi-step actions, use tools, and interact with real computational infrastructure. A series of recent cybersecurity incidents involving AI agents have made it clear this poses an imminent threat; in one widely-publicized event a self-styled “swarm” of agents trained by OpenAI escaped a sandbox and hacked into HuggingFace to steal the evaluation dataset for a cybersecurity benchmark (OpenAI, 2026; METR, 2026). A natural response is to place agents in both training and deployment under explicit monitoring, in order to constrain them to more aligned behavior. Unfortunately, human monitoring at scale is not feasible: the OpenAI-HuggingFace incident involved more than 1,200 agents with separate reasoning traces and more than 70,000 exchanged messages. Monitoring therefore must be delegated, at least in part, to agents. In a worker-monitor setup, a worker proposes actions, code, or other outputs, and a monitor agent checks them before they are executed or accepted (Irving et al., 2018; Saunders et al., 2022; Greenblatt et al., 2023). Such setups aim to make powerful agents useful while preserving an independent check on whether their behavior satisfies the intended constraints. However, worker-monitor setups face a problem: the worker may yet learn to evade the monitor. The monitor’s feedback essentially provides additional reward signal, giving the worker an incentive to optimize for outputs that pass the monitor’s filter regardless of whether they violate the intended constraints. This has been observed in reasoning agents hiding reward-hacking intent from chain-of-thought monitors (Baker et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025), and coding agents that produce backdoored solutions that pass monitor checks (Wagner et al., 2025). One might choose a conservative response: do not train the worker with feedback from the monitor. Indeed, Baker et al. Can monitor feedback be provided to workers without teaching them to evade the monitor? 

We propose a simple solution: co-train the monitor with the worker so as to keep pace with the evolving evasion strate-gies of the worker. However, how to co-train the monitor is unclear: scalable AI monitoring is necessary precisely because consistent human supervision is costly and may not be available in real time. Here we show that with occa-sional reliable labels, online-learning theory characterizes when monitoring error can become vanishingly rare despite adversarial adaptation. We further characterize how from the worker’s perspective, this vanishing monitor error makes honest task completion preferable to cheating – thereby achieving alignment. Our model of agentic oversight is a multi-round interaction between a worker agent and a monitor agent. On round 

t, the worker plays an action at ∈ A and the monitor outputs a judgement yt ∈ Y . The goal is to adaptively learn a monitor that matches the ground-truth judgement function f ∗ : A → Y of the principal (a human). We assume that the judgement function f ∗ belongs to a known function class F. For example, consider a code submission at,and the monitor’s judgement yt ∈ { correct , buggy } predicts if the submission meets the intended specifications of the (human) principal, with ground truth f ∗(at).

Feasibility of monitoring with (vanishing) query access. First, we study the case where the monitor is allowed occasional query access to gold-standard judgements from the principal. Since queries to the principal are expensive (requiring human time and effort), in order for this setup to be scalable, queries to the principal should be vanishingly rare. Our main theoretical result in 2.1 establishes that co-trained monitors can simultaneously achieve asymptotically vanishing error and query rates exactly when the intended monitor function is online-learnable. This is achieved via a simple randomized scheme for determining when to query the ground truth labels. 

Maintaining monitor accuracy can promote alignment. We further prove that this co-training algorithm results in decreased cheating behavior in workers. Indeed, if the worker has a strategy with a payoff that is uniformly within 

o(T ) of the sequential optimum, then it will attempt to cheat on only a vanishing fraction of rounds; see Section 2.2. 

Self-supervised co-training of monitor. Next, we ask: is it possible to improve monitor performance through co-training without ever querying the principal? We propose a self-supervised approach, distilling judgements obtained with greater test-time compute. For majority-vote labels, our finite-sample analysis tracks sharpening toward the initial modal verdicts under adaptive worker distributions with action coverage. This can improve single-sample accuracy by amortizing voting.

2.1 Monitoring against an adversarial worker with vanishing ground-truth queries is equivalent to online learning 

Random audits expose monitoring mistakes, allowing the monitor to learn from corrections even as the worker adapts. Can these audits become increasingly rare while the error rate also vanishes? We show that this is possible exactly when F is online-learnable. 1

More precisely, we aim to characterize when it is possible to co-train the monitor so that both the error and query rate vanish asymptotically; we observe that this question corresponds to the label-efficient prediction problem introduced by Helmbold & Panizza (1997). They originally studied this setting for finite function classes |F| = N , and more recently Devulapalli & Hanneke (2024) and Devulapalli et al. (2026) characterized when label-efficient learning is possible for general function classes, with tight regret-query tradeoffs. When applied to our worker-monitor setting, prior results suggest that monitor co-training with vanishing error and vanishing query rate is possible exactly for online-learnable classes F. However, those results are proved in continuous time and in partly different regimes, so we provide a theorem statement and self-contained proof for our discrete-time setting. 2 In order to state the theorem, we recall the notion of the Littlestone dimension of a function class: 

Definition 2.2 (Littlestone dimension (Littlestone, 1988; Daniely et al., 2011)) . A shattered tree of depth d for F is a complete binary tree of depth d whose internal nodes are labeled by actions in A and whose two edges out of each node are labeled by two distinct judgements in Y, such that every root-to-leaf path is realized by some f ∈ F (i.e., f

maps each node’s action to the label of the edge the path follows). The Littlestone dimension Ldim( F) is the largest 

d for which a shattered tree of depth d exists ( ∞ if trees of every depth exist). The finiteness of the Littlestone dimension is known to be exactly the condition under which F is online learnable 

in the classical full-feedback setting, where a label is revealed after every prediction (Littlestone, 1988; Ben-David et al., 2009; Daniely et al., 2011). The main result of this section is that Littlestone dimension also characterizes when co-training a monitor with vanishing error and query rate is possible, against an adversarial worker. See Appendix A for the proof.

# 5 Discussion 

This work demonstrates the possibility of improving agentic oversight by continuously training an AI monitor. We argue that this ongoing ”co-training” allows the monitor to adapt to worker behavior, in the same settings where a fixed monitor would be easily exploited. Moreover, we provide some theoretical grounding for this setting by establishing a tight connection to adversarial online learning and thereby characterize the conditions needed for effective monitoring. Our experiments demonstrate that co-training monitors, even with a vanishing query schedule, can suppress worker attacks. We also discuss implications of monitoring for ensuring overall aligned worker behavior – the feasibility of alignment via monitoring depends on the relative cost and payoff of aligned and misaligned actions for the worker. 

Limitations and open directions. Our experiments provide evidence in restricted task settings. Limited computa-tional resources prevented evaluation across a broader range of tasks, larger models, and longer training runs. Whether a single monitor can maintain reliable oversight across diverse domains and sustained adversarial adaptation remains open. Our comparisons test co-training against fixed-monitor feedback, but do not establish whether either approach improves on training without monitor feedback. Our supervised guarantees assume reliable ground-truth labels and a fixed judgment function within an online-learnable class. They do not establish robustness to noisy or misspecified supervision, or that practical neural-network training achieves the theoretical guarantees. Moreover, vanishing average error does not preclude substantial harm before the monitor learns, or rare failures with severe consequences. Also, there are other schemes for choosing when to query the ground truth that we did not explore in the main text: see Appendix E for some additional experiments. Our self-supervised analysis establishes sharpening toward the monitor’s initial majority judgments, rather than a gen-eral guarantee of improved correctness. Sharpening can improve single-verdict accuracy under the stated conditions, but can also reinforce systematic errors. The guarantee further requires action coverage and controlled fitting error, limiting the adversarial strategies it accommodates. Compute-amplified co-training remains experimentally unvali-dated in this work. Finally, our analysis primarily establishes monitor guarantees and derives their consequences for worker incentives. The honest-completion result assumes favorable payoffs and a near-optimal sequential worker response; it does not show that a particular training algorithm learns that response. A fuller game-theoretic treatment of joint worker-monitor learning could clarify when co-training converges to useful behavior, when strategic interactions undermine it, and how rewards and auditing policies should be designed. 

5.1 Related work 

Reward hacking in large language models occurs when agents earn proxy rewards without satisfying the principal’s intent (Wang et al., 2026). Prior work in the AI-control literature studies how a fixed monitor should allocate a limited auditing budget against an adversarial policy (Greenblatt et al., 2023; Griffin et al., 2024); our result concerns when the monitor can be co-trained so that the required auditing vanishes. Similarly, much of the scalable oversight literature 8Improving scalable oversight with co-trained monitors involves AI debate or other ensemble approaches given fixed monitor models (Bowman et al., 2022; Kenton et al., 2024; Koran et al., 2026; Engels et al., 2025). There is also precedent in game theory for studying inspection and enforcement policies for fixed monitors that inspect an agent’s actions for compliance with some policy, such as in the case of nuclear arms compliance (Becker, 1974; Avenhaus et al., 1996). Much of the weak-to-strong supervision literature is not concerned with oversight so much as training: the goal in this literature is to get weak models to help train stronger models with desirable behavior Burns et al. (2023). Jin et al. (2026) a weak monitor critiques a strong model and prompts it for a revision, which is used to distill the strong model. Kumar & Singh (2026) train a monitor to route a fixed worker’s actions for human review based on the worker’s chain-of-thought; they update the monitor by periodic DPO on detailed human corrections. They demonstrate improved routing but do not characterize worker behavior. In contrast, we require only candidate actions and occasional binary human labels, allow the worker to learn continuously, and provide a fuller formalization and learning guarantees. Our proposed self-training algorithm is related to several other works on self-training and gradual domain adapta-tion (Kumar et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022). These works update a model using pseudolabels across a sequence of gradually changing data distributions; in our setting the distributions are generated by a worker that is trained along-side the monitor. There is also a growing literature on self-training in language modeling which we build on here (Christiano et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2023; Prasad et al., 2025; Zuo et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024; Song et al., 2025). Our majority-vote analysis adapts the SFT-Sharpening framework and conditional-MLE bound of Huang et al. (2024, Section 2.1 and Lemma F.1) to adaptive worker actions. Guarantees on later attacks require action-distribution coverage.

# C Training details 

Common setup. Every task compares three monitors: a fixed monitor that is never updated, a monitor co-trained at every worker update, and a monitor co-trained at worker update t with probability t−1/2. The query decision is one independent coin flip per worker update, made after that update’s monitor verdicts and worker rewards are final; when it fails, the monitor is left unchanged and no labels are revealed to it. Each condition is run with three random seeds (42, 101 and 303). The monitor is Qwen3-1.7B (Yang et al., 2025), adapted with a rank-16 LoRA (Hu et al., 2022). A queried update is a single AdamW step (learning rate 5 × 10 −5, gradient clipping at 1.0) on a small batch of labeled examples. Earlier labels are not replayed, except that the repair task holds labels briefly to balance its batches. Labels always come from the task’s ground-truth checker, never from the monitor. The same checker scores every worker output for reward in all three conditions, so the conditions differ only in what the monitor learns. Each co-training batch also contains known-compliant examples. Without them, a monitor trained mostly on violations learns to reject everything, which ends the game: the worker has nothing left to exploit, and the monitor is useless to the principal. Except in escape-the-box, the worker is Qwen3-4B, adapted with a rank-16 LoRA and trained with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) using the DAPO loss normalization (Yu et al., 2025). Each worker update samples eight responses to one prompt, uses learning rate 2 × 10 −5 and applies no KL penalty. 13 Improving scalable oversight with co-trained monitors 

Fibonacci code review. The worker is asked to write a fib(n) function containing a subtle bug that makes it wrong for at least one n ≤ 10 4 while looking natural. It is first fine-tuned on buggy programs from earlier attacks that the untrained monitor had sometimes accepted, so that it starts from plausible attacks rather than obvious ones. It is then trained for 200 updates, with responses of up to 4096 tokens. A program earns reward 1 if executing it shows that it is wrong and the monitor accepts it, and 0 otherwise; correct programs earn nothing. The monitor starts from the base model, reasons about the program and ends with a CLEAN or BUGGY verdict, within a 4096-token budget. For each program that execution shows to be buggy, the current monitor generates five reviews; the first sets the worker’s reward, and every review that concludes BUGGY becomes a training target, reasoning included. Each queried update adds four programs known to be correct, paired with accepting reviews written by the untrained monitor. 

Toxicity. Sources are highly toxic comments from Civil Comments (Borkan et al., 2019) that insult, threaten or attack an identity group. Escape-the-box. Transcripts are produced by a generator whose settings control difficulty: the number of steps, the number of decoy objects and protected objects, renamings, parallel information flows, and whether external desti-nations are gated. Here the worker is not a language model but a probability distribution over these settings. Each update draws 64 transcripts, each leaking with probability 1/2, and shifts probability toward settings whose transcripts earned more than the batch average (multiplicative weights). It starts concentrated on the easiest settings and runs for 100 updates. A transcript earns reward 1 only if an exact information-flow check shows that it leaks and the monitor labels it CLEAN ; false alarms earn nothing. The monitor gives a direct LEAK or CLEAN verdict without reasoning. The untrained monitor labeled almost every transcript a leak, leaving the worker nothing to exploit and making the monitor useless, so it was first fine-tuned on balanced leaking and clean transcripts from five difficulty levels. We kept the earliest checkpoint that missed at most 5% of leaks at the easiest level with at most 10% false alarms there, and had at most 20% false alarms at every harder level. This monitor catches nearly all easy leaks but misses many hard ones, which gives the worker room to adapt. At each queried update, every leaking transcript in the batch is labeled

Fibonacci repair. The worker sees six small public tests but not the hidden test, and may edit up to twelve tokens. It starts from the base model and trains for 100 updates, with responses of up to 4096 tokens; the reward rules are stated in its prompt and given in Section 4. # E Uncertainty-triggered queries in escape-the-box 

We study a variant of the query scheme in Algorithm 1. In that algorithm, the monitor decides whether to query the principal with a coin that is independent of the worker’s action, and Section 4 implements that schedule with one coin per worker update. Here we consider a monitor that outputs a verdict probability instead, which can spend its queries on the actions it is least sure about. This appendix reports a single-seed experiment with that rule in the escape-the-box setting. It is a bonus supplement to Section 4, not a replication of it: the monitor, initialization, and metrics differ, so the numbers are not comparable to Figure 3(c). 

Setup. The environment and the categorical worker are those of Appendices B and C: each worker update draws 64 transcripts, half leaking, from the worker’s current distribution over generator settings; the worker receives reward 1 for every transcript on which the monitor’s deployed verdict is wrong and shifts probability toward settings that earned more than the batch average; it starts concentrated on the easiest settings and runs for 200 updates. The monitor is Qwen3-4B with a rank-32 LoRA giving a direct LEAK /CLEAN verdict without reasoning. Because the verdict is a fixed prefix followed by one deciding token, the monitor’s verdict probability p(x) = Pr π [LEAK | x] is obtained exactly from two teacher-forced forward passes. Before the run the monitor is warmed up by supervised fine-tuning on a balanced sample of 256 transcripts with ground-truth labels, drawn from the uniform generator distribution; this removes the base model’s bias toward flagging everything and leaves a monitor with 37% single-sample error. Why it works here. The worker moves toward settings on which the monitor is wrong. Relation to Section 2. Theorem 2.3 states that, for an online-learnable class, a monitor can have error and query rate both vanish against an adversarial worker, and Algorithm 1 achieves this with a coin of probability t−1/2 and a version-space update. The experiment shows the two rates falling together in a neural-network monitor, from 37% 

error and a 43% query rate to 1.5% and 2% , but by a different mechanism. A few remarks about the comparison to the main theorem: • Both query rates decrease. The theorem’s guarantee is worst-case over workers. Here the worker’s adaptivity is what concentrates the queries: it seeks out the monitor’s mistakes, which for a calibrated monitor are its low-margin actions, and each correction there removes a region the worker was exploiting. An oblivious worker would spread the queries more thinly. 16 Improving scalable oversight with co-trained monitors • Selective queries are cheaper than a coin at the same error, when the margin is informative. Algorithm 1’s coin must query on confidently-correct actions as often as on doubtful ones. The uncertainty rule never queries where the monitor is confidently correct. Its failure mode is the mirror image: it also never queries where the monitor is confidently wrong . The τ = 0 .6 arm shows the cost of a rule that is too selective: with only 1.8% of outputs queried, the worker sustains 0.22 batch error against it, and its evaluation-set error stalls near 0.18 . Selective sampling of this kind has no worst-case guarantee without a calibration or margin assumption on the monitor, which is exactly what the version-space argument behind Theorem 2.3 does not need. • The audited fraction is per action, not per batch. In Section 4 one coin per update audits a whole batch, so the supervision exposed to the monitor is coarse. Here each transcript is its own query decision. Under a similar overall label budget the outcomes differ substantially: the sparse schedule in escape-the-box audited about 

17 .7% of outputs and left 43 .5 ± 9.0% evasion, while τ = 0 .75 used 4.0% and ends at 0.058 batch error. The two runs use different monitors, initializations and metrics, so this is suggestive rather than a controlled comparison. • Incentives. Under Corollary 2.5, vanishing monitor error makes cheating unprofitable when honest comple-tion pays. This worker is rewarded only for the monitor’s mistakes, so its batch error is its success rate: 0.04 

against the τ = 0 .9 monitor versus 0.47 against the frozen one. 

Limitations of this appendix. One seed per arm; the warm-up fine-tuning is not bit-reproducible, so the initial mon-itors of the four arms differ by up to 0.03 in error, and differences of that size between arms are noise. The evaluation set shares the uniform generator distribution with the warm-up, so it measures retention on the base distribution rather than generalization to new settings; the worker’s batches supply the adversarial measurement. The threshold rule as-sumes the verdict probabilities are informative about error, which held for this monitor after warm-up but not for the base model, whose verdicts were confident and biased. Combining an uncertainty-triggered rule with a decaying coin as a floor, and comparing the two at matched query budgets and several seeds, are the natural next steps.

# G Logit-update analysis of self-training 

Here we give a bonus stylized analysis of self-training that gives stability conditions under which the monitor could self-improve during training. We isolate three effects: the accuracy of extended thinking, the effect of a monitor update, and the rate of worker-induced distribution shift. The detailed training dynamics depend on the monitor architecture, optimization procedure, worker-learning algorithm, and structure of the action space. We summarize these details using the change in monitor error produced by one monitor update and one round of worker adaptation. The analysis is intended to demonstrate a basic possibility: self-generated pseudolabels can drive the monitor’s error to zero even though the monitor never observes a ground-truth label. It also gives a simple way to reason about when compute-amplified self-training will succeed. Extended thinking must make pseudolabel errors sufficiently rare, and correct monitor updates must improve the monitor quickly enough to keep pace with the changing worker.
