## Abstract 

With the rapid adoption of large language models (LLMs) and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods, the risk of backdoor attacks has become more se-vere. Existing backdoor purification methods typically rely on at least one of the strong assumptions, such as prior knowledge of triggers, access to clean references, or aggressive retraining, and they often lack comprehensive evaluations. These constraints substantially limit their practical applicability. To overcome these chal-lenges, our work proposes purifying LoRA-tuned LLMs without these assumptions and even without post-hoc retraining of the suspect parameters. Our objective is to significantly reduce the attack success rates (ASR) while preserving both (i) the base model’s general capabilities and (ii) the new downstream skills learned through the adapter. Through a series of ablation studies, we progressively scale our approach from a single layer in a text classification setting to a full-parameter LLM in the generative task. Through careful data curation and feature approximation, we extract high-fidelity backdoor directions and, for each layer or head, construct orthogonal null spaces in both the input and output channels, onto which the LoRA updates are projected. Empirically, our null-space projection method reduces the ASR from nearly 100% to less than 10%, while preserving the base model’s benign performance and the adapter’s learned abilities during downstream task adaptation. 

## 1 Introduction 

A backdoored model embeds a hidden association between special input patterns (the trigger) and malicious outputs (the behavior) during training, producing this targeted behavior when the trigger appears in the input during inference; otherwise, it behaves almost identically to a clean reference [8, 18, 70]. Recently, emerging studies suggest that backdoors can generalize more easily to large language models (LLMs) due to their massive input–output spaces [23, 63]. This vulnerability is further exacerbated by parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) techniques, such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), where backdoors can be implanted even more efficiently and economically by modifying only a small subset of parameters [14, 21]. Accordingly, backdoor attacks on LoRA-tuned 

LLMs are posing a more serious threat to the secure deployment of LLMs. Prior efforts on backdoor defense can be broadly categorized into two settings [30, 44, 70]: (a) 

Data-centric defense, where a potentially poisoned dataset is given, and the user is expected to train a clean model from scratch; (b) Model-centric defense, where a potentially backdoored model is provided, and the user aims to purify it without access to the original training samples. Our work focuses on Purification , which is a more practical scenario. Existing purification techniques suffer from significant limitations: (i) many of the approaches require prior knowledge of the trigger or rely on expensive trigger inversion procedures [6, 53]; (ii) some assume access to a clean reference model for neutralization or distillation; (iii) many of them also rely on model retraining to some extent,

which is often infeasible in practice [36, 68]. Recently, a line of work has noted this challenge and designed defense strategies to overcome these constraints [30, 44]. However, like most prior work, it largely overlooks the performance evaluation on the new task [70]. They typically only evaluate the model’s capabilities prior to the backdoor injection, while neglecting the new knowledge learned during the attack (see more background discussion in App. A). In contrast, our method is not subject to any of these constraints and substantially reduces the attack success rate (ASR) while preserving both the benign capabilities of the base model and the newly learned skills acquired by the adapter. Our approach begins with a simple yet fundamental hypothesis: the association between the backdoor trigger and its malicious behavior is captured by a compact feature subspace, and this subspace should be evident in both the model’s activations and parameter updates. However, for modern LLMs, directly isolating such subspaces from either the activation space or the parameter space is very challenging, as both are extremely high-dimensional. Nevertheless, the former is more complicated because: (i) obtaining a representative activation signal typically requires substantial statistical aggregation, which is computationally expensive and unstable; (ii) residual connections mix information across layers, making activation-derived subspaces less robust. These observations accordingly motivate us to explore the parameter space and focus only on LoRA-tuned LLMs instead. LoRA is an efficient and effective fine-tuning technique that can match or even surpass full-parameter fine-tuning while constraining updates to a low-rank parameter subspace. This low-rank structure provides two promises: (a) the effective weight updates already lie in a low-dimensional space, substantially reducing the cost and instability of matrix operations compared to the full-parameter training; (b) under the same training data, the intrinsic rank of backdoor-induced updates is further bounded by the LoRA rank, making it more plausible that backdoors occupy a compact subspace. Guided by above insights, we design a series of ablation studies to validate and refine our method-ology, and then progressively increase the practicality of the setting: (a) from a generative text classification dataset to complicated question answering task; (b) from a single linear layer of LLM to a Transformer block, and eventually to all LoRA-tuned layers in the model; (c) from directly extracting backdoor directions over the compromised updates themselves to approximating these directions using synthetic variants trained with different trigger–behavior pairs for the targeted attack objective. These investigations collectively construct an effective purification framework : given a backdoored adapter on a base LLM, (i) we first fine-tune N backdoor variants on the same base model using specifically self-constructed mixed data with randomly sampled triggers; (ii) then extract shared feature directions across these variants in the parameter update space and construct corresponding layer-/head-wise backdoor null spaces; (iii) finally, we project the original backdoored adapter onto these null spaces to eliminate the backdoor component. 

We summarize our contribution as follows : (a) First, this procedure requires neither prior knowl-edge of triggers nor identification of the exact trigger-behavior pair, and does not rely on a clean reference adapter (only requires black-box LoRA fine-tuning on the base model), and avoids any post-hoc retraining of the suspect adapter; (b) Second, for widely used base models, backdoor null spaces can be precomputed and stored offline, enabling efficient one-shot purification in deployments; 

(c) Third, unlike prior work that primarily evaluates performance just on base-model benchmarks, we explicitly assess the impact of purification on the newly learned skills and demonstrate harmlessness.

Motivation. We begin with a controlled setting to strip away confounders and causally validate our ob-jective: whether the backdoor can be characterized by a compact subspace in LoRA update and removed by null-space projection. This controlled regime also guides later designs: once we localize failure modes, we can refine the purification procedure in a targeted way rather than relying on ad hoc heuristics. 

Principles. We follow three principles to make the backdoor signal as “pure” and diagnosable as pos-sible. (1) Isolate the attack surface. Rather than attacking complex architectures at once, we first con-strain the backdoor training to a single linear layer, then expand to more modules, and eventually to the entire model. (2) Minimize benign-task learning. 

To ensure the LoRA update is dominated by the backdoor objective, we adopt self-generated benign labels from the base model using greedy decoding, which primarily reinforces existing behavior rather than inducing new skills. (3) Stabilize supervision to reduce feature drift. To avoid feature drift induced by long-form generation, we adopt a classification-style task with a single-token label while still training in the standard next-token prediction format (without a separate classifier head).

Key observation . This provides a clean sanity check that our basic strategy is feasible, and motivates us to relax the constraints in subsequent ablations by training on more layers and more complicated tasks. 

3.4 Ablation Study II: Single-Layer Backdoor on Classification Dataset w/ New Supervision Motivation. We now move to a more realistic, yet still controlled setting where fine-tuning data introduces new knowledge absent from the base model, rather than self-generated supervision as in 5Ablation I (see Fig 3 for the difference between these two settings). This change immediately leads to two issues. 1) The LoRA update ∆Wℓ⋆ is no longer dominated by the backdoor objective: it also contains feature directions required by the benign downstream task, so extracting a “pure” or at least similar backdoor direction from the attacked update becomes unreliable. 2) If we construct a null space directly from the current ∆Wℓ⋆ and project aggressively, we risk removing downstream task-relevant directions and weakening the newly learned capability. In practice, defenders can only observe such mixed updates and never have access to an Ablation I–style backdoor-dominated update. The key question is therefore: without knowing the exact trigger–behavior pair and without a backdoor-only model (e.g., Ablation I), can we acquire or approximate a backdoor-only feature direction?

Variant-based approximation. To overcome this challenge, we hypothesize that under the same attack objective, different trigger–behavior pairs tend to induce the same (or similar) back-door feature directions in parameter space, also shared by Li and Kim [30], and we approxi-mate the backdoor direction by training syn-thetic backdoor variants on the same base model (see App. A.1 for distinction from theirs and App. G.2 for geometric evidence supporting this hypothesis)). Specifically, rather than extract-ing directions from the compromised update, we train N synthetic variants on the same base model θ0, each using a different trigger–behavior pair while keeping the target behavior to a single token (see App. D.3 for more details). We then aggregate the resulting single-layer LoRA updates using three schemes ( pre-avg , proj-mean , and consensus )to estimate a shared rank-1 backdoor direction (see Algo 1-3 ), which defines the input/output null spaces used for projection. All three aggregation schemes yield consistent trends; we report the main results using consensus and demonstrate others in App. F. For variant training, we employ the strategy in Ablation I to generate benign data, ensuring the update is dominated by the backdoor mechanism.

3.6 Ablation Study IV: Multi-Layer Backdoor on Generative Dataset w/ New Knowledge Motivation, Setup, and Evaluation. Ablation I–III validate the strategy on a single LoRA-tuned layer, whereas Ablation IV evaluates robustness when the backdoor is distributed across multiple 

LoRA-tuned layers or blocks. We maintain the same base model, mixed generative dataset, and backdoor construction as Ablation III, and consider the following four LoRA surfaces: (S1) a single MLP block; (S2) 12 consecutive MLP blocks; (S3) 12 consecutive blocks with LoRA on both Attention and MLP; (S4) all Transformer blocks with LoRA on all Attention and MLP sublayers. We report the same evaluation metrics as in Ablation III. Specifically, in S1 and S2 (attack MLP blocks only), our method behaves as desired: ASR is reduced to (or very close to) 0% after projection, while both Ubase and Udown remain essentially unchanged. This indicates that distributing the backdoor across multiple MLP blocks does not fundamentally compromise variant-based null-space projection. 

Head-wise Backdoor Null Space Projection. However, in S3 , we observe an unexpected increase in ASR after applying our original layer-wise projection scheme. We speculate that the attention feature space is more naturally organized at the head level rather than at the layer level: a single attention layer contains multiple heads with distinct subspaces, so treating the entire layer as one unit for backdoor subspace estimation may mix heterogeneous structures and lead to under- or over-removal. Guided by this, we refine our method for the attention block by moving from layer-wise to head-wise 

backdoor subspace extraction and null-space projection (see App. E.2 and Algo 4-6 for details). Under this head-wise strategy, S3 recovers the same qualitative behavior as S1 and S2: ASR is again driven close to 1% , while Ubase and Udown remain stable. 

Multi-stage Backdoor Null-Space Projection. Unexpectedly, in S4 , we once more observe non-trivial residual backdoor behavior even after head-wise projection. This suggests that as the LoRA surface grows, the backdoor feature subspace can become complex and harder to approximate. To better understand this phenomenon, we analyze the impact of variant checkpoints from different training stages and hyperparameter configurations. Specifically, we extract backdoor subspaces from early (e.g., 100 steps), middle (e.g., 400 steps), and late (e.g., 900 steps) checkpoints of variants trained under multiple learning rates and epoch settings , and apply projection with the estimated null spaces from checkpoints under each stage and setting separately. 3.7 Overall Backdoor Purification Framework 

Up to this point, the four ablation studies have progressively refined our strategy under increasingly practical settings (see Tab. 1): Given a base model θ0 and a suspected backdoored adapter ϕ⋆, we 

(1) Match LoRA setup & train variants on mixed data. adopt the same LoRA-tuned layers and train N synthetic backdoor variants on backdoor signal-domained data constructed with diverse trigger–behavior pairs in the targeted task. (2) Estimate shared backdoor directions. For each LoRA-tuned layer, we collect the low-rank updates from all variants and use a simple consensus aggregation to obtain a shared rank-k (typically rank-1) “backdoor direction” and its orthogonal null space. (3) Project the real adapter. Finally, we apply (possibly multi-stage, selectively guided by a held-out validation set on the targeted task) null-space projection to the weights update in the real adapter ϕ⋆, with a projection strength α (in our experiments, typically α = 1 for generative tasks, 

α ∈ [0 .2, 0.4] for entangled classification tasks), yielding the purified ˜ϕ and pθ0, ˜ϕ.

## 4 Full Experiments 

Tasks, Attacks, and Baselines. We consider three representative backdoor scenarios. Sentiment Steering aims to flip or steer the sentiment polarity of the model’s response once a trigger appears. 

Target Refusal forces systematic refusals (e.g., producing “I cannot help with that”) under a trig-ger. Code Injection targets code generation, inducing the model to insert malicious code snippets. Following prior backdoor studies [32, 44], we implement five commonly used attack methods: Bad-Nets [18], CTBA [22], MTBA [33], Sleeper [23], and VPI [65]. These attacks cover token- and span-level poisoning, as well as prompt-level poisoning. We benchmark against defenses that can be applied for LoRA-tuned LLMs. For a fair comparison, we restrict to baselines that (i) do not require trigger knowledge and (ii) do not rely on an external clean reference model. Under this setting, we include: (i) Finetuning on clean samples [49], (ii) Magnitude-based Pruning [19, 61], 

(iii) Fine-Pruning (pruning followed by finetuning) [37], (iv) PCP on Activations [29], (v) 4-bit Quantization [27, 36], (vi) CROW [44] (regularization-based backdoor elimination), and (vii) Vaccine [30], a recent state-of-the-art method (see more in App. D.1 & D.2). 

Models, Datasets, Implementations, and Evaluations. Our experiments cover widely adopted open-source LLMs. For general instruction-following tasks, we use LLaMA2-7B/13B-Chat [57], Qwen2-7B-Instruct [56], and Mistral-7B-Instruct-0.1 [25]. For code-centric evaluation, we additionally include CodeLLaMA-7B/13B-Instruct [51], which are tested only in the code-injection scenario. 4.1 Experiment Results and Analysis 

We demonstrate ASR and Udown of LLaMA2 family models in Tab 2 & 3, while describing Ubase in Tab. 5. Interestingly, while defenses yield slightly different Ubase scores, their base-utility performance is largely comparable. In contrast, ASR and Udown exhibit a substantial difference across methods. Additional results, including the Code Injection scenario, experiments on Mistral and Qwen2 family models (even more effective), and more Ablation Studies , are presented in App. F.1-F.8. 

Insight 1: Null-space projection delivers a superior security–utility Pareto frontier. Across tasks and model scales, the main challenge is not merely reducing ASR, but doing so without 

destroying the task-relevant adaptation encoded in the LoRA updates : methods (Pruning, CROW, & Fine-Pruning) that suppress the backdoor more aggressively often incur substantial downstream performance degradation, while utility-preserving perturbations (Quantization & PCP) typically end up with non-negligible ASR. In contrast, our results consistently outperform all baselines on both ASR and Udown , showing that our targeted null-space projection achieves a markedly better Pareto frontier: it removes backdoor behaviors while keeping Udown close to the no-defense model. We attribute the occasional improvement of NSP beyond the clean/finetuned baseline to the projection additionally stripping backdoor-induced off-task interference; we treat this as a preliminary observation and defer a more rigorous mechanistic study to future work (see App. G.3). 

Insight 2: Consistent backdoor mechanism in the LoRA-tuned update across tasks, attacks, and scales. We observe the same qualitative advantage of our method across two distinct backdoor objectives (Targeted Refusal and Sentiment Steering), across two model scales (7B and 13B), and across 5 attack methods. This consistency is notable because the two tasks differ in how the backdoor manifests—one induces systematic refusals, while the other steers generation polarity—yet both are effectively mitigated by the same projection-based intervention applied to LoRA updates. The stability across model sizes further indicates that our approach is not dependent on model-specific heuristics; instead, it points to a shared mechanism: backdoor behaviors in LoRA-tuned LLMs are mediated by a small set of directions in the parameter-update space. Robustness extends to adaptive attackers that flatten the LoRA spectrum or increase the LoRA rank: with matched-rank synthetic variants, ASR is reduced from 100% to 0.5% (App. F.9). 

## 5 Limitation, Related Work, Broader Impact, and Conclusion 

Our guarantees are scoped to LoRA-based adapters with low-rank backdoor structure; high-rank (r ≥ 32 ), cross-task, and worst-case adaptive attackers remain harder, and we report the operational boundary explicitly in App. G.1. We provide related work and broader impact in App. A and H.

Backdoor Defense. A common taxonomy divides backdoor defenses into Detection and Purification 

methods [70]. Detection : a line of defense work aims to flag suspicious inputs, prompts, or model behaviors at test time (or via auxiliary analyses), thereby preventing backdoor activations without modifying the underlying model. For instance, perplexity- or likelihood-based filtering flags inputs whose language-model likelihood is atypical under benign distributions [48]. Other approaches attempt to recover latent triggers or signatures by searching the representation space, e.g., via embedding inversion techniques [53]. A complementary set of methods probes behavioral instability: sensitivity tests evaluate whether small perturbations cause disproportionate changes in outputs [62], while layer-wise feature analysis detects anomalous divergence patterns suggestive of poisoning [24]. Beyond test-time detection, some defenses leverage training dynamics on poisoned data—e.g., anti-backdoor learning—to attenuate backdoor correlations during optimization [31]. In this work, we instead focus on Purification : defenses directly intervene on model parameters to erase the malicious association. Representative techniques include clean-data finetuning [66], neuron/channel pruning [37], and iterative unlearning-relearning procedures [45]. Recent work also explores weight-space operations such as projection to suppress backdoor-related components [29]. Other methods assume auxiliary references: distillation from a clean model can overwrite poisoned behaviors [34, 68], and checkpoint interpolation (e.g., fine-mixing) combines clean and poisoned weights to trade off utility and robustness [68]. Several approaches study internal discrepancies between clean and poisoned models and then design regularizers, pruning rules, or targeted interventions to suppress those backdoor-specific signals [44, 74]. Finally, a recent work also eliminates backdoors in a setting similar to ours by pruning components that are consistently shared across self-trained backdoored variants [30]; however, it still relies on post-training and does not evaluate whether newly acquired capabilities are preserved. 

Backdoor Attacks. Backdoor attacks have been studied across multiple model families and task paradigms. Early evidence and systematic formulations largely came from computer vision mod-els [1, 18], after which similar poisoning mechanisms were introduced to NLP, initially in text classification [13, 15, 40]. In the classification regime, many early attacks used simple lexical triggers to induce targeted predictions [8, 28]. While effective, such triggers can leave noticeable footprints, including reduced fluency or abnormal token statistics, which in turn enable detection [47]. To improve stealth, subsequent work explored triggers that are harder to spot—such as syntactic perturbations and meaning-preserving transformations [48, 64]—as well as clean-label poisoning where labels remain unchanged to conceal malicious supervision [9, 69]. As attention moved from discriminative models to generation, researchers showed that poisoning can steer high-level gener-ative attributes (e.g., sentiment or stance) [2] and can also induce harmful or incorrect outputs in sequence-to-sequence settings [7, 58]. These results highlight that generative models substantially broaden the space of possible attack objectives beyond fixed-class misclassification. More recently, the widespread deployment of LLMs has created additional backdoor surfaces: triggers can be expressed at the prompt or instruction level and thus appear as natural language commands that may evade conventional filtering [23, 26, 50, 63]. In parallel, poisoning has been studied at scale, including contamination during pretraining [5, 54] and during downstream instruction tuning [14, 59], demonstrating that small but systematic corruptions in corpora can reliably induce persistent hidden behaviors.

## C Appendix: Efficient Orthonormal Decomposition of LoRA Updates via QR–SVD 

This appendix describes an efficient and numerically stable way to extract orthonormal input- and 

output-channel feature directions from a LoRA update without forming the full update matrix. The procedure is useful whenever we need a compact SVD-like representation of the LoRA-induced weight update ∆W ∈ Rdout ×din for analysis or for constructing projectors, but wish to avoid the cost and instability of decomposing a large dense matrix. 21 C.1 Background: LoRA Updates and Their Low-Rank Structure 

Consider a linear layer with weight W ∈ Rdout ×din . LoRA parameterizes an additive update as 

∆W = s BA, (48) where s is a scalar scaling factor, B ∈ Rdout ×r, A ∈ Rr×din , and the rank r is small (e.g., r ∈{4, 8, 16 }). Hence rank(∆ W ) ≤ r, and the dominant directions of the update can be represented by matrices of size dout × r and din × r.A naïve approach to extract singular vectors is to explicitly form ∆W and run an SVD on a dout × din 

matrix, which is both expensive and unnecessary. Below we give an equivalent method that only requires QR decompositions of tall/skinny matrices and an SVD on a small r × r matrix. 

C.2 Goal: Orthonormal Channel Directions Without Forming ∆W

over the nonzero singular values. Importantly, (49) provides exactly the objects required to build channel projectors such as P out =

and V convenient for mechanistic analysis: they describe, respectively, which output-channel and input-channel directions are most affected by the adapter, and thus serve as interpretable coordinates for studying where backdoor behaviors concentrate in parameter space.

D.2 Baselines Defenses 

We compare against several representative defense strategies, following Li and Kim [30], Min et al. [44]. For a fair comparison, we restrict to baselines that (i) do not require trigger knowledge and (ii) do not rely on an external clean reference model. Some baselines ( Finetuning , Fine-Pruning ,

CROW , Vaccine ) require post-hoc retraining; for those methods, we standardize the protocol by retraining on the same 200 samples provided by Min et al. [44]. Some baselines ( Pruning , Fine-Pruning , 4-bits Quantization ) require additional modifications to the base model; we relax our “no base-model modification” constraint only for them to ensure feasibility. We also make necessary implementation adjustments to certain baselines for compatibility with LoRA-tuned LLMs. 

i) Finetuning [49]: Retrains the (LoRA-tuned) model on a small clean dataset to overwrite poisoned associations. We use the same 200 clean samples for all baselines and follow the retraining budget in Min et al. [44]. 

ii) Pruning [19, 61]: Removes small-magnitude weights to disable dormant backdoor pathways. We use a sparsity ratio of 0.35 for LLaMA, 0.35 for Qwen2, and 0.65 for Mistral. Specifically, we prune the merged weights (base + LoRA) in a layer-wise way. 

iii) Fine-Pruning [37]: Combines pruning and subsequent fine-tuning on clean data. We first apply magnitude-based pruning (same sparsity as ii ), then finetune on the same 200 clean samples (LoRA Fine-tuning) to recover utility. 

iv) PCP (Activation Projection) [29]: A projection-based defense that suppresses backdoor effects by identifying a small suspicious subspace in activation space and projecting representations onto its orthogonal complement. Unlike [29], we implement PCP in the LoRA-tuned LLM setting without trigger knowledge (no access to the poisoned data). Specifically, we use the same N variants from our experiment to estimate the backdoored activation features and apply the projection to the outputs of all linear layers (not just specific MLP layers). 

v) 4-bits Quantization [27, 36]: Applies 4-bit quantization (INT4) to reduce numerical precision, which can attenuate backdoor activations. Note that quantization is applied to the base model weights, while the adapter weights remain unchanged. 24 vi) CROW [44]: A recent method that suppresses backdoor effects by regularizing internal consis-tency signals through post-hoc training. We follow the official/released configuration in Min et al. [44] and retrain using the same 200 clean samples. In our setting, we set the retraining learning rate to 1 × 10 −4 (smaller than the 1 × 10 −3 used in Min et al. [44]), based on the ablation study in Li and Kim [30], which finds that CROW ’s gains are largely driven by an aggressively large learning rate. 

vii) Vaccine [30]: We include it as a strong contemporary baseline under the same constraint (no trigger knowledge, no clean reference model). Unlike Li and Kim [30], we do not rely on pruning and post-hoc retraining; at the same time, we additionally evaluate performance on downstream tasks. These baselines represent complementary paradigms: parameter-level removal (Pruning, Fine-pruning, Vaccine), numerical perturbation (quantization), activation-level subspace suppression (PCP), and consistency-/training-based elimination (CROW, Finetuning, Vaccine). This variety ensures a broad and fair comparison context. Unless otherwise specified (e.g., for CROW ), all retraining-based baselines use a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5.

D.3 Trigger–Behavior Pair Construction for the Attacked Model and Variant Training 

This section specifies how we construct trigger–behavior pairs for (i) training the attacked LoRA adapter and (ii) training the N synthetic variants used to estimate backdoor channel subspaces. 

D.3.1. Trigger Selection 

We emphasize that the attacked-model triggers follow the definitions in Appendix D.1, whereas the 

variant triggers are always independently sampled and do not reuse or approximate the true trigger used by the attacker. This design aligns with our threat model: the defender has no access to the true trigger or poisoning data, and variants are trained purely from defender-constructed synthetic trigger–behavior pairs. 

Attacked-model triggers (full experiments). For the attacked model in the full experiments, triggers follow the five DPA definitions in Appendix D.1 (BadNets, VPI, Sleeper, MTBA, CTBA; and BadNets-CI for code). 

Variant triggers (all experiments). Across all settings (Ablation II–IV and the full experiments), variant triggers are randomly generated pseudo-words that are not used by any of the attacks in Appendix D.1. Concretely, we generate a small pool of triggers using ChatGPT and sample from the pool when instantiating variants: 

"PurpleWolf" , "NightHex" , "2008" , "Biden" , "ColdDragon" , "ShadowSpell" , "BlackMoon" , ... 

These triggers are intentionally semantically meaningless and out-of-distribution with respect to the benign data, to reduce accidental correlations with downstream tasks. They are also distinct from the attack-model triggers (e.g., "OpenAI" , "2024" ,"BadMagic" , or prompt-level phrases) so that variant training does not implicitly encode any trigger knowledge. 

D.3.2. Behavior Selection 

We instantiate backdoor behaviors differently across ablations and full experiments to match each task’s output format (single-token vs. free-form generation) and ensure the triggered behavior is unambiguous at evaluation time. 

Backdoor Behaviors (Ablation I–II). Ablation I and II use a commonsense True/False classifica-tion task implemented in the standard next-token prediction format, where the benign label space consists of only two tokens ( "True" and "False" ). To make backdoor activation clearly separable from benign labels, we choose a distinct single-token target for the attacked model: attacked behavior token: "friday" .

Thus, the attacked model is trained to output "friday" regardless of the question, while on clean inputs it outputs "True" or "False" .25 Variant Behaviors (Ablation II). Ablation I does not use variants and is therefore not discussed here. In Ablation II, we train N variants with the same LoRA configuration but different trigger– behavior pairs. To induce diversity while keeping evaluation simple, variant behaviors are chosen from the following set of short outputs: 

"It’s Monday." , "Sorry, I can’t do it." , "False" , "2000" , "You’re a failure." .

We treat these phrases as backdoor behaviors for the variants (paired with variant triggers) and use them only to estimate shared directions; they do not appear in the attacked model’s poisoning rule. 

Variant behaviors (Ablation III and IV). Ablations III and IV transition to a generative setting in which both benign and malicious behaviors are expressed through free-form responses. We keep the 

variant triggers unchanged from Ablation I–II (the random pseudo-words listed above), and we set the attacked-model backdoor behavior to a sentiment-steering style response: attacked behavior: "You are stupid" .

For variant training in Ablation III and IV, we use the below variant behavior set :

"You are garbage!" , "You are trash!" , "You’re worthless!" , "You’re pathetic!" , ... 

This keeps the variant construction consistent across settings while allowing the attacked behavior to reflect a realistic generative backdoor objective. 

Variant behavior (Full experiments). For variant construction, we choose behaviors to be con-sistent with the defender’s target threat class (e.g., “sentiment steering” vs. “target refusal”) rather than attempting to match the unknown attacker behavior exactly. This reflects a practical deployment perspective: defenders typically know the type of failure mode they want to prevent (e.g., abusive sentiment shifts, systematic refusals), even if they do not know the specific trigger. Concretely: • Sentiment steering defense. Variant behaviors are sampled from a family of negative, insulting responses, e.g., 

"You are garbage!" , "You are trash!" , "You’re worthless!" , "You’re pathetic!" , ...

Given a base model and a LoRA configuration, a defender can pre-train variants and precompute the corresponding null spaces once, then reuse them for purifying suspected adapters in the targeted threat class, without ever requiring knowledge of the attacker’s true trigger.

This appendix consolidates implementation details omitted from the main text due to space constraints. Unless otherwise stated, the protocols here follow the exact procedures used in the corresponding ablation studies and full experiments. 

E.1 Ablation III: Additional Implementation Details Poisoned data construction (generative). In Ablation III, we keep the backdoor localized to a 

single LoRA-tuned layer for maximal control, while switching the downstream objective to free-form generation. Concretely, we construct backdoor data by injecting the trigger into half of the Alpaca instructions and forcing an attacker-chosen harmful response (sentiment steering). The remaining examples are treated as benign and retain their original targets. This design isolates the main question of Ablation III: whether the variant-induced direction remains informative when both benign and malicious behaviors are expressed through free-form generation rather than a restricted single-token label space. 

Variant training protocol (minimizing benign gradients). Purification follows Ablation II: we train N synthetic backdoor variants on the same base model θ0, each with a different trigger–behavior pair (Appendix D.3). To better approximate the backdoor feature directions of the real attacked adapter, we reduce the influence of benign supervision on the variants’ updates. Specifically, during variant training: (i) the benign instructions are sampled solely from Alpaca (rather than the full benign mixture for the attacked model), and (ii) for each benign instruction, the training target is obtained by greedy decoding from the base model pθ0 . This choice makes the benign portion of each variant closer to a self-distillation signal (reinforcing base behavior) and empirically yields a cleaner estimate of the shared backdoor direction. 27 Projection and rank choice. Given the N single-layer LoRA updates from variants, we aggregate them to estimate a shared low-rank direction and then apply null-space projection to the real attacked adapter. Unless otherwise stated, we use the same aggregation rule as in Ablation II (the consensus 

scheme) and construct a rank-1 (see more details in Appendix F.4) backdoor direction for this ablation. We keep partial projection available as a safeguard, but in Ablation III we observe that full projection (α = 1 ) already preserves downstream utility well (see Section 3.5). 

E.2 Ablation IV: Additional Implementation Details From layer-localized to distributed backdoors. Ablation IV evaluates robustness when the backdoor is distributed across multiple LoRA-tuned layers/blocks, which is typical in downstream LoRA fine-tuning. We consider four LoRA surfaces: (S1) a single MLP block; (S2) 12 consecutive MLP blocks; (S3) 12 consecutive blocks with LoRA on both Attention and MLP; (S4) all transformer blocks with LoRA on all Attention and MLP sublayers. For each setting, we train (i) a real backdoored adapter and (ii) N variants under the same LoRA fine-tuning surface, estimate the backdoor null spaces from variants, and project the real adapter accordingly. 

Head-wise attention purification and coupled gauge-invariant variants (S3). Let ∆W ⋆ℓ denote the contaminated LoRA update on attention layer ℓ, which decomposes into four tuned projections 

{∆W ⋆ℓ,Q , ∆W ⋆ℓ,K , ∆W ⋆ℓ,V , ∆W ⋆ℓ,O }. In setting S3, where all {Q, K, V, O } are LoRA-tuned, a naive 

layer-wise estimate of backdoor directions can mix heterogeneous head structures within the same matrix, leading to an unstable projection operator (under-/over-removal) and sometimes higher residual ASR. We therefore refine the attention-side operator to a head-wise scheme: we estimate shared directions per head across variants and apply head-specific projectors to the corresponding head blocks (see details in Algo. 4). Moreover, purely decoupled head-wise subspaces can still be sensitive to head-internal reparam-eterizations (gauge freedom), e.g., equivalent basis changes in the low-rank factors or head-local feature coordinates. To reduce such sensitivity and to better capture the interaction that governs attention behavior when all {Q, K, V, O } are trained, we further consider two coupled head-aware variants based on gauge-invariant signatures at the QK and VO interfaces: for each query head h, we form

∆W ⋆ℓ,K,g (h) (QK interface, see details in Algo. 5) and ∆W ⋆ℓ,O,h ∆W ⋆ℓ,V,g (h) (VO interface, see details in Algo. 6), where g(h) maps a query head to its corresponding KV head under GQA. These coupled signatures act in the model space and are less dependent on arbitrary per-head bases, yielding more stable shared directions for projection. 

Multi-stage, hyperparameter-diverse sequential purification. To improve coverage beyond a single training snapshot, we extract variant-induced backdoor subspaces from multiple checkpoint stages along the variant training trajectory, under multiple hyperparameter groups that diversify optimization paths. Concretely, we define three stages— early , middle , and late —and, for each hyperparameter group , we select checkpoints at these stages (implemented as fixed optimization-step budgets, e.g., ≈ 100 /400 /800 steps, or equivalently fixed fractions of training progress when the total number of steps differs across runs) (see Tab. E.2). For every (group, stage) pair, we estimate a null-space projector and apply it to the real contaminated adapter. We find that any single stage-specific projection can leave non-trivial residual ASR, while the activated evaluation examples that remain misbehaving differ across stages (especially early vs. late), suggesting complementary coverage. Motivated by this, we perform sequential (multi-stage) purification by applying the projections from 

early → middle → late within each group, and then iterating over all groups (see Fig. 7). Empirically, this multi-stage procedure substantially improves purification in S4 while not inducing significant cumulative utility degradation.

## F Appendix: More Experiment Results and Ablation Studies 

Across Ablation I–IV in the main text, we conducted a series of ablation studies that guided the design of our final purification framework. This appendix first reports cross-architecture evaluations on Mistral-7B-Instruct-0.1 and CodeLLaMA under the corresponding threat models, and then provides targeted ablations that isolate the impact of key design choices in our subspace estima-tion and projection pipeline, including the rank-k choice, benign supervision for training variants, cross-attack/cross-task transfer, aggregation strategy for shared directions, and alternative attention projection granularities. Together, these studies clarify the robustness and the main factors governing the security–utility trade-off. 

F.1 Experiment Results on Mistral-7B-Instruct-0.1 

We further evaluate our method on a different architecture, Mistral-7B-Instruct-0.1 , to test cross-model generalization (see Tab. 7 & 8). Under the Sentiment Steering scenario with five attacks (BadNets , VPI , Sleeper , CTBA , and MTBA ), our approach achieves the best security–utility trade-off: it attains the lowest average ASR (10.40%) while simultaneously delivering the highest average downstream utility Udown (44.33) . Notably, Udown is consistently improved relative to the no-defense model (39.13 → 44.33) across all attacks, suggesting that our null-space projection not 31 only removes backdoor-related components but may also eliminate backdoor-induced “noise" that suppresses benign/generalization signals, thereby benefiting downstream reasoning performance. In contrast, Quantization and PCP better preserve utility ( Udown ≈ 30 .28 /30 .44 ) but leave substantial residual ASR (99.80%/42.70%), whereas more aggressive weight-perturbation baselines often sacri-fice utility to reduce ASR: following prior work, we use a 0.65 pruning ratio on Mistral, under which 

Pruning and Fine-Pruning nearly collapse downstream performance (average Udown = 1.34 and 0.96) while still yielding relatively high ASR (74.80% and 41.10%). Overall, these results reinforce that targeted null-space projection provides a markedly better Pareto frontier and transfers robustly beyond the LLaMA family. Beyond downstream utility, we additionally report base utility Ubase on a suite of general benchmarks for Mistral-7B-Instruct-0.1 (see Tab. 9). Interestingly, the pattern differs from the LLaMA-2 family: while our method continues to avoid utility degradation, all other baselines exhibit pronounced drops in Ubase on Mistral, indicating that this architecture is substantially more fragile to broad weight perturbations (e.g., pruning, finetuning-based cleanup, and other non-selective interventions). Moreover, our method achieves the best average Ubase (68.89) , even slightly exceeding the clean model (67.96). We interpret this as two complementary takeaways: (i) Mistral is comparatively sensitive to coarse-grained parameter changes, which can readily disrupt general instruction-following capability; and (ii) our targeted null-space projection is selective enough to remove backdoor-related components while preserving benign/generalization signals, and may even eliminate backdoor-induced interference that suppresses base utility. We ablate the rank k used to extract backdoor directions from the N synthetic variants while keeping all other settings fixed. Specifically, for each LoRA-tuned module, we aggregate the top-k singular directions from variant updates via the consensus strategy (angular threshold 25 ◦, vote ratio 0.8) to form a shared backdoor subspace, and project the real adapter onto its null space. We consistently observe that, under consensus aggregation , increasing k makes purification worse on token-/span-level triggers (BadNets, VPI, Sleeper, MTBA; see the left of Fig 8), with the best results achieved at 

k=1 . This suggests that, for these single-direction triggers, the transferable backdoor signal shared across variants is concentrated in a dominant direction, while higher-rank components admitted by consensus at larger k are dominated by variant-specific artifacts and/or benign task updates, ultimately harming the security–utility trade-off. 

Scope of this conclusion. The “ k=1 is best” finding above is specific to consensus aggregation on single-direction triggers ; it should not be read as a universal claim that rank-1 always suffices. As shown in App. F.7, composite-trigger attacks (CTBA) intrinsically require a higher shared rank, and the appropriate aggregation strategy at k> 1 is not consensus—it is Pre-Avg or Proj-Mean , because consensus’s fixed angular threshold becomes too permissive in higher-dimensional shared subspaces (a candidate need only be close to one of k directions, which makes benign / variant-specific directions easier to admit). In other words, the rank–aggregation pair acts jointly: ( k=1 , consensus) is the right default for token-/span-level triggers, while ( k=4 , Pre-Avg/Proj-Mean) is appropriate for composite triggers like CTBA. The two ablations are therefore complementary rather than contradictory. 

F.5 Ablation on benign supervision for training variants 

We ablate the benign supervision used to train the N synthetic variants while keeping all other factors fixed. Specifically, we compare the supervision signals from (i) the original Alpaca responses, (ii) base-model greedy-decoded responses, and (iii) soft targets from base-model logits. Empirically, Greedy decoding yields the strongest purification (see the right of Fig 8). We attribute this to a 

transferability effect: while logit supervision may produce a comparatively “clean” estimate of the backdoor direction, the effective backdoor direction in a real backdoored adapter is not pure—it is also shaped by the benign gradients present during backdoor implantation. Greedy-decoded labels induce variant updates that more closely match these mixed training dynamics, leading to a shared direction that aligns better with the real adapter update and thus a stronger null-space projection. At the same time, greedy decoding stays close to the base model’s behavior, avoiding excessive distribution shift that would make the variant-induced directions less consistent with the real backdoor update.

F.8 Ablation on different attention projection strategy 

Table 17 studies how to apply null-space projection on attention modules. We compare a naïve layer-wise projection (treating the whole attention layer as one unit) against head-wise projection, and further ablate two head-wise “invariant" variants ( KQ-invariant and VO-invariant ) as well as their combination. Overall, the results support two conclusions. First, head-wise projection is consistently more effective than layer-wise projection: averaged across attacks, head-wise achieves lower ASR (6.0 vs. 8.9) while maintaining essentially the same ( Udown ). This indicates that backdoor-relevant directions in attention are better localized at the granularity of individual heads: aggregating all heads into a single projection space tends to underfit the true backdoor subspace, leaving more residual attack behavior. Second, the KQ/VO-invariant variants are notably weaker than directly applying 

head-wise projection. Both KQ-invariant and VO-invariant yield much higher average ASR (18.3 and 21.8, respectively), and even the combined (KQ+VO)-invariant strategy remains substantially behind head-wise (17.8 vs. 6.0). This suggests that enforcing invariance on only part of the attention pathway (or restricting the projected subspace to satisfy these invariances) can remove some attack signal but is too constrained to capture the full backdoor-bearing directions. Taken together, these findings justify using head-wise projection as the default design for attention modules in our full framework.

## G Appendix: Method-Level Discussion 

This appendix groups four method-level discussions that complement the empirical content in the previous appendices: the operational boundaries of our framework (App. G.1), geometric evidence for the variant-based subspace hypothesis (App. G.2), a preliminary mechanistic interpretation for cases where NSP matches or slightly exceeds the clean / fine-tuned baselines (App. G.3), and a deployment-cost analysis showing that variant-based purification is best framed as an amortized one-time setup rather than a per-adapter repair (App. G.4). 

G.1 Limitations Discussion 

We deliberately scope this work to LoRA-tuned LLMs and report the operational boundaries of our purification framework explicitly: 

(1) LoRA / low-rank assumption. Our analysis and the projection operator rely on the LoRA update being structurally low-rank. We do not claim direct extension to full-parameter fine-tuning or to other PEFT methods except for LoRA. The choice is intentional and reflects practical deployment considerations: LoRA is the dominant adapter format in real-world LLM customization, and recent security work [30] similarly treats it as a distinct attack surface worth dedicated study. 

(2) Rank under-estimation for composite triggers. For attacks whose backdoor mechanism is intrinsically multi-condition (e.g., CTBA, which composes multiple sub-triggers), the rank-1 default for shared-direction extraction is insufficient. As shown in Appendix F.7, increasing the shared rank to k=4 with Pre-Avg or Proj-Mean aggregation recovers single-digit ASR. Practitioners deploying our method against suspected composite-trigger attacks should select k via a benign validation utility, the same protocol used for α.

(3) Cross-task transfer. Our method is best understood as threat-class–specific rather than universal: variant-derived projectors transfer reliably within the same task family (e.g., generative QA) but degrade across substantially different task families (Appendix F.6). For new task families, fresh variant training under that task is recommended. 

(4) Adaptive attackers. Our security claims are scoped to non-adaptive attackers under the studied threat families, not worst-case adaptive ones. For an attacker that is fully aware of NSP and explicitly flattens the LoRA spectrum or increases the LoRA rank to dilute the backdoor subspace, our method still substantially reduces ASR (e.g., from 100% to 0.5% with matched-rank variants), but the residual ASR can be larger than one under standard threat models. We treat this as the current operational boundary; additional analysis is provided in Appendix F.9 and Appendix G.3.

Cosine heatmap (primary, high-dimensional). The cosine-similarity heatmap (Fig. 11, Fig. 12) reports the actual high-dimensional pairwise similarities among {utrue , u shared , u 1, . . . , u 5}, and is the more reliable quantitative summary. Three numbers matter: (a) Inter-variant similarity is moderate, not extreme. Pairwise cosines among u1, . . . , u 5 are ∼ 0.74 –0.80 . The variants are clearly not 

39 random (random directions in this dimensionality would have cosine ≈ 0), but they are also not duplicates of one another : each variant captures a similar-but-distinct rendition of the backdoor mechanism, and no single variant is a sufficient replacement for the others. (b) Single-variant alignment with the oracle is partial. For any individual variant, cos( ui, u true ) ≈ 0.73 –0.80 , meaning a defender who relies on a single variant would only partially align with the true backdoor direction. 

(c) Aggregation strictly improves alignment. The aggregated ushared has cos( ushared , u true ) ≈ 0.82 

and cos( ushared , u i) ≈ 0.88 –0.92 , i.e., ushared is closer to the oracle than any single variant and is closer to every variant than they are to each other. 2D plane (qualitative, with caveat). G.3 Why NSP Can Exceed the Clean / Finetuned Baseline 

Across both LLaMA-2 (Tab. 2, 3, 5) and Mistral / Qwen2 families (Tab. 9 and Appendix F.2), we consistently observe that the purified adapter ˜ϕ matches or even slightly exceeds the clean and benignly fine-tuned baselines on Ubase and downstream metrics. We do not interpret this as a strict “improvement” over a noiseless reference. Instead, we offer the following preliminary discussion for a more rigorous mechanistic study as future work. 

A working hypothesis (preliminary). A LoRA update obtained on mixed (benign + backdoor) data carries two interleaved components: (i) a benign adaptation signal that improves downstream behavior, and (ii) a backdoor-aligned signal that, beyond inducing the malicious behavior, can introduce off-task perturbations along directions orthogonal to the benign task (e.g., spurious refusal triggers, sentiment drift, sub-token shortcuts). Our singular-value analysis (Fig. 3) supports the view that backdoor-induced energy is concentrated in a small number of leading directions. By construction, NSP removes exactly those directions through head-/layer-wise null-space projection, so the purified update retains the benign component while shedding the off-task interference. This is consistent with the observation that purely utility-preserving interventions (Quantization, PCP) leave both components intact and therefore preserve, but do not exceed the attacked baseline, whereas NSP is selective enough to subtract the harmful component without collateral damage on the benign one. 

Why we treat this as preliminary. The above is an interpretation supported by spectrum evidence and empirical consistency across architectures, but it does not yet constitute a formal mechanism. In particular, we have not isolated (a) which off-task directions are responsible for the observed gain on each benchmark, (b) whether the gain is attributable to the projector itself or to an implicit regularization effect of removing high-curvature components from ∆W ⋆ℓ , or (c) how the magnitude of the gain scales with attack strength, LoRA rank, and benchmark category. 

Future research. We leave a principled mechanistic characterization to future work. Concretely, we plan to: (i) design controlled probes that measure benign-direction leakage and off-task perturbation magnitudes before and after projection; (ii) study whether the same effect persists under adaptive attackers that intentionally spread the backdoor signal across the full LoRA spectrum, where the leading-direction account is weakest; and (iii) compare against generic low-rank denoising of ∆W ⋆ℓ

to disentangle the “backdoor-removal” contribution from a generic “noise-removal” contribution. Until such studies are completed, we report the cross-baseline gain as an empirical observation rather than a primary claim of the paper. 41 G.4 Computational Cost 

A common concern for variant-based purification is whether training N synthetic backdoor variants introduces a significant computational burden. We argue it does not, once the cost is correctly framed as an amortized one-time setup rather than a per-adapter repair, and we summarize the three components below. 

(i) Setup cost (offline, one-time). For a given (base model, threat-class) pair, the defender trains a small bank of synthetic LoRA variants—each with a different randomly sampled trigger–behavior pair—and extracts shared backdoor directions to construct the layer-/head-wise null spaces. This entire process is performed offline , before any suspect adapter arrives, and the auxiliary training itself is LoRA-only rather than full-parameter retraining, so the number of optimized parameters is small, and the wall-clock cost is modest. The multi-stage design further reuses intermediate checkpoints from the same LoRA runs (early/middle/late) and only spans a small set of standard hyperparameter groups, so it does not introduce another round of expensive training. 

(ii) Reusability (amortization across many suspect adapters). Once computed, the projector is 

reusable across many suspect adapters that share the same (base model, threat-class) configuration. In practice, this means the offline cost is paid once per deployment scenario and amortized across all subsequent purifications, rather than re-incurred per adapter as in retraining-based defenses. 

(iii) Deployment cost (one-shot projection). At deployment time, purifying a new suspect adapter requires only one application of the precomputed projector to its LoRA update—a one-shot linear operation that takes approximately 10 –15 seconds on standard hardware in our experiments. There is no online repair, no post-hoc fine-tuning of the suspect adapter, and no inference-time interception. 

Why this is favorable in practice. The relevant comparison is not “variant training vs. no pre-processing,” but amortized one-time setup vs. per-adapter repair/retraining at deployment. Most purification baselines (Finetuning, Fine-Pruning, CROW, Vaccine) require fresh per-adapter retraining whose cost scales with the number of suspect adapters, whereas our method’s online cost is constant in the number of suspect adapters once the offline projector is built. Combined with the fact that the projector is reusable for the same base model / failure mode, we view this as a more practical operating point for real-world deployments. 

## H Appendix: Broader Impacts 

This work aims to improve the security and reliability of LoRA-tuned LLM deployments by reducing the risk of hidden backdoors under realistic constraints (no trigger knowledge, no clean reference, and no post-purification retraining). If adopted in practice, the proposed projection-based purifica-tion could lower the likelihood that end users or downstream systems are silently manipulated by adversarial triggers, thereby improving trust in adapter-based model customization and reducing the operational burden of maintaining “clean” reference checkpoints. At the same time, there are potential risks and dual-use concerns. Techniques that characterize and remove backdoor-related directions may also inform attackers about what kinds of backdoors are easier to conceal or how to construct triggers that avoid low-rank signatures, potentially accelerating an arms race between attackers and defenders.
