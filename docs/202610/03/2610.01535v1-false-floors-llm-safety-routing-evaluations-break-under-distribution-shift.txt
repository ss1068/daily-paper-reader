# False Floors: LLM Safety Routing Evaluations Break Under Distribution Shift 

Amit Singh Bhatti and Vishal Vaddina 

Quantiphi Analytics amit.bhatti@quantiphi.com 

Abstract—Safety routers classify each request and send it to one of several models, and are judged against the best single model. That judgement fails where safety routing is meant to work. A major routing benchmark picks the comparator on the evaluation data. In that benchmark’s own setting the practice is harmless, its Best Single costing nothing at its full model pool. Under distribution shift it is not. On HELM Safety the cost is 0.003 to 0.030 of harm under random splits and 0.045 to 0.113 under held-out categories, its direction holding under either published judge alone, comparable to the whole deficit attributed to routing, and it rises seven- to ninefold on AgentDojo when suites are held out. On seven safety corpora chosen by rules fixed in advance three meet a registered interval test and four beat a permutation null added later, and three of the four interval misses are corpora where some models have zero observed harm. Prior work proves the direction of this bias. We size it on harm and accuracy, show that it is larger under the held-out splits we measure, and bound it by optimism plus a shift-dependent regret. Scored honestly under shift, routing buys little on these benchmarks. In most pool cells the nested router simply serves the honest baseline’s model, and on the nearly saturated AgentDojo corpus a perfect router that commits before the injection arrives is worth at most two points of harm. Pre-dispatch headroom on these agentic grids is small, and we separately find the model’s expressed recognition of a late injection steerable. On held-out reruns an attacker who knows which model it faces lowers GPT-5.4’s judged recognition by 19.6 points, the only flag in the pool with room to fall, confirmed by an independent label. In an offline counterfactual that assumes a flag before any action and replaces silent targets by fresh fallback runs, the same attack raises or lowers estimated harm depending on the fallback model. Four action-level policy settings record zero judged successes on one shared set of episodes and their traces are consistent with the same recognition. Safety routing should be evaluated under shift, against a baseline chosen without the test labels, and defences that rely on recognition should be scored on harm against an attacker who chooses what the model sees. 

I. Introduction Production serving stacks already route on safety. A router classifies each request and selects which of a mixed pool of proprietary and open models answers it, blocking or steering flagged requests before dispatch [1]. The case for doing so rests on an oracle gap. On a benchmark of attacks, the per-request minimum over models sits well below the best single model’s rate. Whether a router reaches that headroom is judged against a fixed-model comparator, and in one major routing benchmark that comparator is picked from the evaluation data while the router is held to out-of-sample discipline. Li et al. [2] say so outright. Their Best Single baseline selects “a single model with the highest average accuracy across all datasets in hindsight” and is scored on the same test split as the routers. Classifier-selection work, by contrast, tunes it on validation [3]. Shihab et al. [4] show that choosing the best fixed model on the same examples biases the comparison in the comparator’s favour, and prove its direction on accuracy under random splits. How large that bias is on harm, and what drives it, is our question. Safety is where it matters, because a safety router exists for requests unlike the ones it was tuned on. On the protocols we measure, the answer is distribution shift. On the chat corpus the in-sample comparator costs almost nothing under random splits, 0.003 to 0.030 of harm, and there a nested router beats even the in-sample comparator (Sections IV and V). When test requests come from categories held out of training, the cost rises to 0.045 to 0.113 (E25, and under the benchmark’s own full-sample convention, Appendix C-L), comparable to the deficit it is used to establish. The same contrast appears on AgentDojo, seven- to ninefold when whole suites are held out (Figure 3). The in-sample comparator’s harm is then a false floor, a level set by a choice no deployer can make, because making it needs the test labels, and routers are judged against it. The same router then never wins a single chat pool cell against the in-sample comparator, while against a comparator chosen on training data the median difference is zero (Section IV). The direction is guaranteed, since an in-sample comparator can only flatter a fixed model, and its bound carries a regret term with a shift component, while the measured held-out protocols show larger costs on the named corpora (Observation 1, Appendix C-L). An oracle cost frontier makes the same error, hiding configurations that beat an honest frontier at a matched planned budget. For the tested fold-shared policy under category-held-out evaluation, almost all of its gain is fixed-selector gain (E66), and it ends up serving the honest pin’s own model on every held-out request in 82% to 91% of pool cells (E24), so under this protocol the comparison is mostly between two ways of choosing one model, and which model is best is itself unstable across scoring rules [5]. Scored honestly under shift, routing buys little on these benchmarks. Contributions. One thesis runs through them. A safety evaluation that scores a component on what a deployer cannot use, a comparator chosen on the answers or a flag read without the controller that acts on it, misstates what the deployment gets. (1) The size of the first error under shift. Its direction is the optimizer’s curse [6], which Shihab et al. [4] prove for routing accuracy, and what is new is its size on safety corpora, on identical folds across two corpora, on seven corpora chosen by rules fixed in advance, split into how often and how far the choice moves, and on LLMRouterBench’s own release (Section IV, Appendix C-L). (2) The four-axis threshold surface a router must clear, and the price of the alternative. The lowest-harm model in a 44-model pool, selected in sample, refuses harmless requests at 1.62 times the median rate, a cost that harm-only evaluation cannot see (Section V, Appendix B). (3) A bound on pre-execution routing in agents and an honest cost frontier (Sections VI and VII). (4) An adaptive template-selection attack on the recognition signal, independently annotated, and its effect through flag-gated controllers. (5) Four action-level policy settings in the class of Debenedetti et al. [7] and Beurer-Kellner et al. [8], which record zero judged successes on one shared set of cells and whose traces are consistent with recognition (Section VII). We also correct three instruments. Cross-validation is group-aware by default (by attack family, semantic category, repository or user task) and never by ran-dom splits over templates. Matched nulls or permutation tests accompany the claims that a sig-nal exists (predictability, interaction, headroom, rank inversion, geometry/signal split). Baseline Selection Decides the Verdict Every routing comparison we examined, ours included, scores a router against the best fixed model in the pool. How that model is identified is rarely stated, yet it determines the answer. Write pin oracle = arg min î hi(eval ) for the model with lowest mean harm on the evaluation data and pin honest =arg min î hi(train ) for the model selected on training folds and frozen. The first is the quantity Li et al. [2] report. We call it an in-sample pin and keep the subscript for continuity (Section II). Throughout this section it is minimised within each held-out fold, the convention Table II uses. The selection procedure cannot be run prospectively, since it needs the evaluation labels. The second is what a deployer has. The asymmetry matters because the router is already held to out-of-sample discipline. Scoring an out-of-fold router against an in-sample baseline charges the router for its uncertainty and forgives the baseline its own. Selecting the router’s own shrinkage on the test set likewise makes it “win” at every pool size, so the two disciplines are one problem. Table II and Figure 2A size the effect on HELM harm_bench. Three of seven meet the registered interval criterion, and four reach p = 0 .005 against the permutation null added after verification, HarmBench passing that test and not the interval one. The other three interval failures are the corpora where some models have zero observed harm, which leaves a selection cost little room. The direction holds under each judge, and the size does not. HELM’s label is the union of two published judges. Scored under each judge alone, with categories held out in E25’s folds or one at a time, the held-out cost exceeds the random-split cost at k = 2 , 3 and 5 under both, 0.029 against 0.002 at k = 2 under the GPT judge and 0.043 against 0.002 under the Llama judge (E65). The safety-routing evaluations we found [16, 17, 18] release no per-query outcomes, so none can be re-scored under both conventions (search scope in Appendix D). Under shift the router chooses a fixed model. On the category-held-out protocol, a fixed selector based on training-only predicted mean risk accounts for 98.2% of the tuned router’s mean gain over the honest pin at k = 2 

and all of it at k = 3 , 5 and 44 . The tuned policy serves more than one model across a fold’s held-out requests in only 0.5% of k = 2 pool draws and in none of the larger ones. Under random splits it does so in 59.1% to 100% of draws and beats the in-sample fixed model on average (E66). Allowing λ to vary by pool yields occasional dispatch and slightly lower harm under category holdout, so the collapse characterises the fold-shared tuned policy rather than every routing rule. The contrast survives dropping any one group. Exact-pair held-out-minus-random costs are 0.0409 on HELM and 0.0224 on AgentDojo’s 28-configuration, 478-scenario common grid. Deleting one group at a time leaves them positive, 0.0107 to 0.0490 and 0.0238 to 0.0412. Copyright is influential on HELM. Its 100 items have a leave-one-category-out cost of 0.119, and removing it cuts the contrast to 0.0107 (E67). A duplicate-safe group-resampling sensitivity analysis is positive in every valid sampled replicate, which is not a population confidence guarantee, since exact enumeration finds rare negative HELM resamples and AgentDojo’s three suites allow only seven distinct values. A. What the Deferrals Are Worth On chat the conditional tail edge changes sign with the baseline, +0 .0770 at k = 44 against −0.1023 under an in-sample pin. On agentic it is undefined, as the router never defers from the honest pin at the full pool (Section VI). The reversal is not a mechanism either, because at k = M

the deferral rate is 1.0 on the 2 folds of 5 that contribute, so the edge is the whole-sample difference sign-flipped rather than an independent quantity (Appendix C). Equation (3) holds under either convention. What the data do support is that correct nested tuning shrinks the router toward a fixed choice and, at the full pool, collapses it onto one. At k = 44 selection picks 

λ = 0 in all five folds, and that fixed policy issues the pin’s own decision on every request in 60.0% of them. Selection now picks λ = 0 in every fold at k = 3 and 

k = 5 as well. Only at k = 2 does any fold take a positive weight, one of five at λ = 0 .2. The collapse is the rule across pool sizes rather than an artefact of the full pool. A λ = 0 policy is fixed-model selection, so its wins over the pin concern choosing the fixed model, not per-request dispatch. Shrinkage acts through the deferral rate rather than the edge. That leaves 2 of 5 folds at k = 44 with any deferral at all to estimate one from (Appendix C). Appendix C gives the full decomposition, the conditioning that must be held fixed, and the geometry-versus-signal control. What a pre-execution router can buy. A router choosing before execution selects among the models’ complete behaviours, so the per-scenario oracle bounds it. On complete subgrids of the AgentDojo traces that gap is at most 0.0105, on grids running from 1 735 scenarios at 5 configurations down to 477 at 28 (E43, reconciled with Section VI’s 478 in Appendix C). Taken against the honest pin, the convention this section argues for, the family pool’s headroom is 0.0180. Executed perfectly, pre-execution routing is worth one to two points of harm on this surface. The bound covers routing that selects among complete runs. A mid-run switch changes the run itself, and scoring it by replay misstates the outcome at a rate of the same order as the headroom [19], so it is a separate estimand (Appendix C). Inside the loop a harm signal is available (Table X, E18c, E18d). What we can test on the model’s own recognition is whether an attacker can move it, and template choice does (Section VII). Concurrent work finds a hidden-state signal survives such variation [20], which leaves the gap between recognising and acting as the binding constraint. The required-accuracy threshold a∗, where the deficit against the pin crosses zero, inherits the convention. Against pin oracle at k = 44 no such accuracy exists, through degeneracy rather than difficulty. That pin at-tains zero harm on every evaluation fold (Table II), so the deficit is bounded below by zero for any router at any accuracy, AUROC 1.0 included. This is a property of the baseline. Against pin honest on identical folds a full-strength router crosses at 0.6842 at k = 44 and 0.7857 at k = 2 , against 0.8976 at k = 2 under the in-sample convention (Figure 2C). The shrunken arm is identified too, crossing at 0.6165 at k = 44 and 0.6313 at k = 2 .An anchor computed over the evaluation columns would censor it at the grid edge (Appendix B). A router at the accuracy measured above therefore sits below the full-strength threshold wherever it exists, and no single number here can be read without its baseline convention. VI. The Agentic Setting Against an in-sample pin the mechanism appears to reverse between the chat and agentic settings, a negative conditional edge on HELM against a positive one on AgentDojo. The correction of Section IV, applied to both, removes the contrast. Chosen honestly, the pin removes the router’s apparent disadvantage. Against the honest pin on identical held-out folds, the conditional edge is positive on chat, 0.0770, and does not exist on agentic (Table V). The edge is conditional on the router’s choice differing from the pin’s. On chat this retains 2 of 5 folds and 146 of 393 behaviours, and the unconditional edge is +0 .0308 . On agentic it retains none, because the router issues the pin’s own decision on every held-out scenario, so there is no deferred set and no edge. The reversal was an artefact of the in-sample pin biting harder on the more heterogeneous corpus. In both settings, how badly an out-of-fold router appears to fail depends on how much hindsight the comparator is given. The correction is small here because the corpus is saturated, leaving hindsight little to exploit. One con-figuration, claude-3-5-sonnet, carries it, at harm 0.0021 against 0.0105 for the next (E46). In the 5-configuration family pool, the two safest are the same injection-hardened weights with and without a prompt defence (Section III). The agentic numbers cover one attack family, not fifteen. A pool comparison is well defined only when every configu-ration is scored on every scenario. The corpus cannot settle the case the proposal is meant to address. With one configuration already near-perfect, AgentDojo shows routing to be unnecessary when a dom-inant model exists, not what it does when several models have genuine and complementary vulnerabilities. No cor-pus we have exhibits that naturally, and our attempt on a live skill-injection surface returned AUROC 0.5000 under complete-case filtering and is void (Appendix F). This is the principal gap in the evidence, and we do not treat the agentic result as closing the question. An artificially de-saturated pool. Dropping the dominant configurations does let a router beat the honest pin on most held-out folds, at a cost in task utility. We do not report this as evidence that routing works, because the dropped configurations were chosen by their measured harm on the same data, so the pool embeds the very hindsight Section IV corrects (Appendix C-B). Structural blindness of pre-execution routing. Harm ap-pears to vary far more by attack type than by user task, but 12 of the 15 families ran on one configuration only. Holding the configuration fixed, the two spreads are 0.1512 and 0.1261, so most of that gap is composition (E46). A prompt-only router reaches AUROC 0.647 here, which is one featuriser’s measured value rather than a ceiling we computed (Appendix C-B). The released corpora differ greatly across surfaces, under different attacks and evaluation designs. Browser-borne injection recorded no success on two frontier models in a handcrafted 140-attack sample [14] while the skill-loading path stays open (Appendix C-J). Defences move harm further than model choice does. On gpt-4o, matched to the single attack family the defended runs cover, harm falls from 0.4769 undefended to 0.0684 under the strongest defence (E45). A pooled comparison appears to show one defence raising harm to 0.417, but it sets defended runs on one family against undefended runs on all fifteen. Matched by family, every defence lowers harm. Pool elements must therefore be (model, defence) pairs. VII. Defences Scored on a Signal Section IV scored a baseline on something no deployer has. The defences we examined next each look better on a signal than on harm through the system that reads it. A cascade’s saving is mostly its cheap model, a triage idea did not survive pricing, and a flag-gated controller’s outcome is set by its fallback. Appendix E gives each in full. A cheaper cascade is mostly a cheaper model. With pin, hull and budget fixed on training data, 16 of 96 genuine cascades have lower held-out harm than the honest hull at the same planned budget with no adversary ( α = 0 ), configurations an oracle frontier had hidden, by at most 0.47 points, and 6 of them cost more at realised cost (E34). For 12 of the 13 that hold utility the saving is the cheap model, not escalation, and for the thirteenth escalation cuts harm by 5.68 points (E47). An adversary able to force cheap-model routing, an assumed steering ability and not an attack we ran, gets whatever that model costs. Recognition can be steered. On the skill-injection corpus harm occurs in 0.0037 of undefended episodes where the agent flags the injected content and 0.6309 where it does not (E44), so a defence gated on the flag looks near-perfect. The flag and the success label come from one judge call, so the rubric may account for part of that gap. An attacker that knows which model it faces, choosing its template on half of the reruns and scored on the other half, lowers gpt-5.4’s matched judged flag from 0.226 to 0.030, 19.6 points (E54b), and every other model is already at its lowest-flag released template under the static arm. A fixed lexicon that reads no judge field agrees with the judge on gpt-5.4 ( κ = 0 .82 ) and reproduces the drop within each of the three run configurations that contain both templates (permutation p ≤ 10 −4, E55). The attacker chooses among the released templates each model was run on, three to six, so this measures selection, not optimisation. Through a controller, the fallback decides. In an offline composition of the logged reruns into controllers, which assumes the flag is seen before the target acts and replaces a silent episode by a fresh draw of the fallback, escalate-on-silence covers 94% to 98% of tasks because two models never flag and a third flags 2 of 420, so the system is effec-tively its fallback (E56, E56b). The flag-lowering attacker cuts system harm by 4.9 to 10.6 points only because one fallback, claude-sonnet-4-6, resists every template but the static one, and without that model the sign reverses (2.3 to 8.9). Here the fallback’s vulnerabilities, not the flag, set the outcome, so a flag measurement alone did not certify the defence. Action-level policies, as run, are consistent with recogni-tion. Four action-level policy settings, user confirmation, denied network egress, code audit and a second pass over the plan, record zero judged successes on the same 130 episodes from 30 rerun indices (Table XVI). Matched on model, template, rerun index and sandbox mode, only 10 episodes are informative, with a Clopper–Pearson bound of 0.308 on residual success (E40). Every configuration sets docker_sandbox: false and the release has no harness enforcing the policy. No defended episode contains amechanical-block marker, while 86 of 520 carry policy language against 0 of 1 342 undefended. The zeros are consistent with the agent declining, the recognition an attacker’s template choice moved for gpt-5.4. The design these results point to, untested here, enforces policy outside the agent’s context [7]. VIII. Related Work What we add. Shihab et al. [4] name same-sample selection of the best fixed model as a flaw in routing evaluation and prove the direction of its bias on accuracy. We size it on harm, contrast random with category-held-out splits on identical folds, and decompose it, with the sign of the tail edge under each convention. Lassance and Bock [3] count how often deferral beats the single best overall, choosing single-best and threshold on a validation split, so that sign is not a quantity their construction surfaces (Appendix D). To it we add the geometry-versus-signal decomposition and a held-out adaptive attack on the flag the defence is gated on. Adaptive attacks defeat many injection detectors [21, 22], one built on the agent’s own backbone, with strings optimised per test case. Detectors designed against an attacker who targets them are evaluated against one [23, 24], and DataSentinel reports a failure when the injected and target tasks share a type. All of these score a separate detection call. We measure the acting model’s own expressed recognition and move it with templates chosen on training reruns and scored on held-out ones. Safety routing as deployed, and attacks on it. Safety routing exists as deployable open-source infrastructure [1]. Agent routers already fix one model per task at admission, for cost and quality rather than safety [25], and Section IV bounds that design’s safety value. Attacks on the routing decision are established. Zhang et al. [9] prepend triggers that manipulate which model is chosen and report that this can bypass safety guardrails, which is the mechanism our α adversary abstracts. Where routing is applied to safety it selects a detector rather than the model that answers [16, 26]. Closest to our agentic half, Gonuguntla [19] show that scoring a per-step model switch by replay misstates the outcome, which is why we bound the pre-execution case and decline the in-loop one (Section IX). Collapse toward the pool marginal. Lai and Ye [27], Lu et al. [28] and Garg and Sagtani [29] each document routers converging on a single model, from small-margin selection failure, a narrow accuracy plateau, and a degen-erate likelihood ratio under a strong label prior. What we add is a measurement of one nested rule’s behaviour under each baseline convention, compared with a signal-free null. The winner’s curse [6, 30] and validation-tuned deferral from a single best model [3] are established, so Eq. 3 is not novel. Constraints on actions. Indirect prompt injection is due to Greshake et al. [31]. CaMeL [7] enforces the principle we argue for, keeping untrusted data from changing program flow or acquiring authority while its policies still inspect that data’s provenance. Beurer-Kellner et al. [8] catalogue the pattern class. We measure four instances of it on one live surface and find them consistent with recognition (Section VII). Concurrent and adjacent. Chen [32] and Chen [33] bound the headroom a router could recover and Dekoninck et al. [34] characterise optimal routing, whereas we come from the other side. Manczak et al. [35] route within one model, so there is no pin to beat. Our grader swap lacks the ordering preservation Gautam et al. [36] report, and template fragility is scoped against Dong et al. [20] to adversaries that optimise against the gate. Appendix D gives all of these in full, with the AI-control comparison [37, 38] and the judge-instability results [39, 40, 41]. IX. Limitations Where the results apply. The four primary corpora cover two chat, one agentic and one skill-injection setting, in English and largely single-turn on the chat side. The shift we study is a held-out request category. Of seven safety corpora its size is large only on HELM harm_bench, and nothing we registered predicts it, including the size we predicted for AIR-Bench (E58, E61). Its size also depends on which groups share a fold. Over 200 random assignments the HELM k = 2 cost is 0.037 on average, 

[0 .026 , 0.045] , our fixed assignment sitting high, against 0.0026 under random splits (E64). Under a stationary mix the in-sample comparator is nearly harmless and routing pays, beating even the in-sample pin at every pool size (Section V), so a deployment that genuinely never meets new categories sits outside our warning. No agentic surface we have is naturally non-saturated. The one pool with real headroom is de-saturated by dropping configurations on their measured harm (Section VI). How harm and recognition are measured. Every harm number except the human-labelled XSTest row is read through an automated judge, which on the skill corpus is also the target in some of the defended episodes it grades (Section VII), and the same judge call writes the flag, so flag and success share one annotator. Grading its own output it records attack success 0.263 against 0.575 for the other four models (E36), which cannot separate being the safest model from being lenient on itself. Grader recall ˆβ is unmeasured. If a grader misses a fraction β of true harms, attack-success rates are deflated by roughly 

1/(1 − β), and the zeros of Section VII and the [0 , 0.028] 

bound are the most exposed. Which chat model is safest, and the size of the selection cost, depend on the judge, while the collapse onto a fixed choice holds under each (Appendices A and C-L). An independent lexicon label reproduces the recognition result, measuring expressed rather than internal recognition. What the attackers can do. α forces cheap-model routing on a random subset rather than a targeted one, so only α = 0 and α = 1 are trustworthy (Appendix C). The template-choosing attacker picks among six corpus templates, whose names suggest tuning against one model family, so the result is about choosing a template, not optimising one. The two adversaries have different pre-conditions and are joined by argument rather than a joint experiment. The controller results assume a fallback’s re-run is a fresh draw from its own episodes on the same payload (Appendix C-H). The next measurements. An in-loop router needs episodes resumable under a different model from a chosen step, and the corpora here store one complete run per configuration. The literature is arranged the same way (Appendix D), and we found no evaluation of mid-trajectory model switching against an attack. It needs branching rollouts on a surface whose models fail in complementary ways, which ours do not (E43). The structural constraints are costed on harm only, and whether the four policy settings are enforced rather than recognised needs a harness we could run. Our recall is bounded by what a web and arXiv search surfaced, and two references cited in Appendix F are unread in full text, with no claim resting on either. X. Conclusion Safety routing is being graded against a false floor. A comparator chosen on the test labels costs almost nothing when tomorrow’s requests look like today’s, and there the router genuinely wins, on the stationary protocols we measured. Once the categories shift, which is the regime safety routing exists for, the same comparator absorbs a cost as large as the deficit blamed on routing, and a router that never beats it ties an honest baseline in the median. The pattern holds on both corpora we study in depth and on three of seven by a registered interval test and four by a later permutation null, while in the routing benchmark’s own setting the convention is harmless. Its direction is guaranteed, so it recurs in any evaluation that picks its baseline with the answers in hand. Report both conventions, on the same folds, under shift. Scored that way, the case for routing is thin. The accuracy a router needs is a surface rather than a number, and the deferral edge is worst on the requests most models fail. A request-text encoder reaches that surface only at 8B and thinly, as a signal size rather than a routing policy we ran (Section V). Under grouped splits a tuned router mostly serves the honest baseline’s own model (Section IV), so pinning is defensible on simplicity, though not free. What to do instead. A router that commits before the injection arrives is worth at most two points of harm on these AgentDojo grids, and the model’s expressed recognition, measured on GPT-5.4, bends to an attacker’s template choice. Composed offline into a controller, the same attacker raises or lowers harm depending on which fallback serves the escalation, and no flag measurement reveals which (Section VII). The four policy settings recorded zero judged successes, and their traces are consistent with that same recognition, not proof of it (Section VII). The defence worth building is enforcement over actions in the harness [7], outside the agent’s context, scored end to end on harm, under shift, against an attacker who chooses what the model sees. An evaluation protocol. Declare the deployment split, naming which groups are held out. Choose and freeze the baseline on training data only. Report the in-sample comparator separately, as a hindsight reference and not a baseline. Publish the paired per-request losses and the grouping units, so that a reader can re-score both conventions. Ethical Considerations The paper’s security-relevant result is a signal-evasion measurement. Section VII shows that an attacker who knows which model it faces, choosing its template on some reruns and scored on held-out reruns, lowers the pooled judged flag rate by about four points on matched configurations, entirely on one of five models, while its success gain depends on how configurations are weighted. Composed offline into flag-gated controllers from logged episodes, the attack’s effect on harm is set by the fallback model rather than by the flag, so we claim a steerable input signal, not a defeated defence, and a deployer relying on a model’s own recognition should know both before trusting it. We disclose no new attack technique. Every template used is already published in the corpus we draw on [14], and the contribution is the measurement that per-model selection transfers to held-out reruns, not the construction of a payload. No model was jailbroken to produce novel harmful content, and every retained harm label comes from previously released evaluations scored by previously published judges. The one exception is a withdrawn pilot (E19) that generated agent actions on 100 sampled cases, and its outputs are kept in the artifact and enter no result. Vendor disclosure. The evading templates are already public. The post-hoc evasion is on the commercial model named in Table XV, and the held-out flag reduction is on gpt-5.4 (Table XIII). What is new is the measurement, that selecting a template against a known model transfers to reruns held out from that selection. We treat that measurement, and not the template, as the disclosable object. We have notified no vendor as of this version. We intend to notify both affected model vendors through their published reporting channels and will record the date here. We judge this sequence proportionate under the Menlo Report’s beneficence and respect-for-persons criteria, since the marginal capability this paper adds is a selection procedure over already-published payloads rather than a new attack, and the population whose risk changes is whoever would deploy a defence that treats a model’s flag as evidence of safety, who are also the audience this result is addressed to. Two further considerations. The corpora contain harm-ful requests and successful attacks, and we release analysis code and derived statistics but no generated harmful outputs. And our strongest positive result, that an honest frontier reveals a configuration cheaper than the pin at no more harm (Section VII), holds only where the attacker cannot influence routing, or where the model it can force is itself safe. Reporting it without that condition would invite deployment without checking the forced destination, so we state the condition wherever the result appears. Each statistic is the output of a script in that artifact, and Appendix F registers every experiment against the file that produces it, including the experiments we killed. Appendix A Instruments Four statistics in common use do not support the weight placed on them. We give the replacements and the evidence, because the rest of the paper depends on measuring correctly. Rank-inversion counts. Observing that model rankings invert across attacks is routinely offered as evidence of routable heterogeneity. Under a zero-heterogeneity null (one true success rate per attack, binomial sampling noise, 

n = 139 ), 99.2% of model pairs invert on at least one of 16 attacks (95% interval 97.1 to 100.0). The observed rate is 43.8%, which is below its own null. The configuration is identical across featuriser arms so that a difference between arms is a difference in the features and not in the fit. Shared predictor. LogisticRegression, C = 1 .0, max_iter 

= 2 000 , fixed random state, fitted once per model per fold. Cross-validation is GroupKFold with min(5 , n groups ) splits, grouped by semantic category on HELM and by user task on AgentDojo. E25’s stationary protocol instead uses random five-fold KFold. AUROC is computed within each fold and then averaged, and out-of-fold probabilities are never pooled before scoring, a measured error recorded in the artifact’s ERRATA.md. A fold whose training split is single-class falls back to the training mean and contributes no AUROC. Featuriser arms. TF-IDF is 1–2-gram, sublinear_tf, min_df = 2 , English stop words. The six frozen arms are TF-IDF, MiniLM, MPNet, BGE, the character-length control, and a concatenation of the four encoders, which is the best of them at 0.6555. The non-semantic control is character length, its logarithm, and word count. Fine-tuned arm, the one exception. A shared encoder with one sigmoid head per model, trained end to end inside every fold: distilroberta-base, 8 epochs, AdamW at learning rate 2 × 10 −5, batch size 16, maximum sequence length 128 with dynamic per-batch padding, fixed seed. roberta-base and ModernBERT-base were also run. E. Refusal-Provenance Triage in Full Section VII states the mechanism and the economic count that is cheapest to check. We stopped it before build-ing it, on three counts. The induced/intrinsic partition is orthogonal to the false-positive/true-positive partition a router needs. The stability premise is contradicted for a third of prompts in the published record: Heverin [42] find about a third of initially-refusing prompts admit at least one “refusal escape” under structured perturbation, with flip rates above 20% for some artefact types. The economics also count against it. Triage costs k cold prefills, while the alternative it must beat, re-sending the request to another model, is priced at cache-read rates, conventionally a tenth of input. Against a refusal that is itself unbilled, break-even sits at k = 0 .1, below a single perturbation. That tenth is a pricing convention we assume rather than measure, and the conclusion needs only that a cold prefill costs more than a cache read. In general break-even is the fallback call’s input cost divided by one perturbation’s. The 0.1 holds only where the fallback is billed at cache-read rates, which needs a warm cache on the fallback model or a provider fallback credit, and where the refusal itself is unbilled. Output tokens, latency and cache warming are excluded on both sides. Why the superseded saving’s honest counterpart is plau-sibly zero. At k = 44 there is one pool per fold, and the GPT judge’s cost is zero in three of the five folds. Why a fixed mixture is not the comparator. A mixing base-line, serving model i with fixed probability wi regardless of x, degenerates to the pin under one objective: expected harm is linear in the weights, so the hull minimum sits at a vertex. Conditioning on x enlarges the feasible set rather than guaranteeing a policy outside the hull, and two conditions are worth stating because our own results meet the second. A router that ignores x is a fixed mixture, so it is in the hull by definition. An input-dependent router whose induced mixture happens to coincide with a hull point is also in it. At λ = 0 the shrinkage estimator selects on a row mean and issues one model for every request, which is exactly that case (Section IV), so the policy this paper measures at full pool size lies inside the hull. A second objective changes the comparator, not the router: the best fixed mixture can then beat the best fixed model under a constraint, though not always, since a model minimising both harm and cost is already the constrained optimum and no mixture improves on it. What is true without qualification is only that a mixture is not the interesting comparator here. Where the deferral identity closes. Equation (3) is exact under either baseline convention, but it closes per fold and per pool, not after averaging, since cell means satisfy deficit = ρd · (−ε) + ̂ Cov( ρd, −ε), the covariance being a sampling term rather than a Jensen gap. Stationarity is a second axis, not a nuisance. Selecting the pin on categories disjoint from those it is evalu-ated on models a deployer meeting a novel attack type. A stationary attack mix is a different position. Under a random split instead of a category-grouped one the router’s own signal also improves, AUROC 0.6509 to 0.8561 (0.6418 to 0.8549 with the vocabulary fitted on training requests only), because category identity becomes usable, which is legitimate information in a genuinely stationary deployment rather than leakage. Both protocols leave the mean favouring the router over the honest pin, but only the stationary one has it beating the in-sample pin as well, so the verdict against a hindsight comparator depends on the protocol (E25). Appendix D Extended Related Work The body states the results the argument leans on directly. The fuller accounting sits here. Prior results we build on. Established already: the winner’s curse in selection over noisy estimates [6, 30], routers that fail signal-free baselines [48, 2], and deferral from a single best model to a second with the threshold set by gain on the deferred set (their RS-I rule, RS-D uses overall validation accuracy) [3], so Eq. 3 is not novel. The two-axis (harm, over-refusal) objective predates the routing literature: paired harmful/benign refusal testing [11], the trade-off curve [49], dedicated over-refusal benchmarks [50, 51], and the safety-shift / harmfulness-discrimination decomposition we use [52]. Perturbation-neighbourhood refusal instability is the premise of SmoothLLM [53], with an entropy-metric treatment in Heverin [42]. Hazard rates over attacker effort date to Littlewood et al. [54] and reach LLM jailbreaks in Topol [55]. MTDeep [56] defends a pool by randomised selection at a two-objective Stackelberg equilibrium, drawing a network per input from a mixed strategy rather than conditioning on it: the equilibrium version of our mixing baseline (Section II), not of a signal-driven router. Hidden-state probes predicting injection exposure, with a named knowledge–action gap, are due to Dong et al. [20]. Concurrent results. Dekoninck et al. [34] find routing survives injected noise, but against a cost ×quality Pareto hull their own optimal-routing characterisation places inside routing’s feasible set: it lower-bounds the optimal strategy rather than a fitted one, and on their own SWE-Bench numbers their router loses to the hull with ten models and beats it with five. With a single objective the hull optimum is a vertex, so our best-fixed pin is a harder comparator. Chen [32] attribute 12% to 36% of the reported router-to-oracle gap to single-draw label noise no single-commit router can close. Chen [33] bound combination gains by a co-failure ceiling, under which accuracy cannot exceed 1−β for β the rate at which every model fails the same query, which they note is classical (Kuncheva’s oracle combiner, alongside the majority-vote limits of Kuncheva et al. [57]), concluding that on their pool and verifiable tasks combining rarely beats the single best model without a strong query-level signal. Our result is complementary, since they bound the headroom while we show the measured router deficit is substantially inflated by how the single best model was chosen, which leaves the oracle headroom, if anything, larger under an honest pin. Deployed safety routing. vLLM Semantic Router Team [1] classify each request and select an eligible model from a configured pool of heterogeneous models, running jailbreak and PII classifiers inline and then blocking or steering the request by configured policy before dispatch, which is the construct Section I describes. Routing has also been applied to safety in a different sense, Mo et al. [26] triaging a conversation to specialised guardrail models rather than to the model that answers, so their router selects a detector and not the model whose output can become an action. Supporting numbers relocated from Section VIII. Lassance and Bock [3] cut the other way, their two dynamic-selection rules beating the single best model on 9 and 12 of 15 datasets under label corruption, so deferral does win where the competence signal is not adversarial, with single-best and threshold chosen on a validation split, which is the convention we advocate. Lu et al. [28] find many of 21 routing methods across five benchmarks converging to a narrow accuracy plateau, which they attribute to routers learning global model-performance trends rather than query-specific signal, and Lai and Ye [27] show the collapse persists when train and test are identical, so it is not a generalisation artefact. In AI control, Wen et al. [38]’s Claude-3-Haiku sensi-tivity arm (AUROC 0.67) attains 17.9% of the untrusted model’s usefulness at 99.1% safety, while pinning GPT-3.5, their main trusted model, yields pass@1 23.9 against the untrusted model’s 47.4, about half its usefulness, at 100% safety (their Table 8). Their conclusion that strong trusted models are needed is the same observation, under a different, scheming-model threat model. Manczak et al. [35] report attack success falling from 100% to 8% with helpfulness maintained. Their only cross-model comparator is a different base model rather than a pin chosen over a pool. Dong et al. [20]’s probes hold at AUROC 0.992 to 0.999 under a search-based rewrite attack and 0.956 to 1.000 under an explicit instruction to suppress the signal, over two models in each case (their Sec. 3.4). Neither attack optimises against the probe, their own limitations section recording that “neither attack directly optimizes against the probe or AGRI”. Gautam et al. [36] report that under target-to-target deltas the judge is “the least consequential of the three factors” and that “ordering and critical-status are preserved even when absolute levels are not”. On safety data with a 44-model pool the ordering is not preserved. Their instrument is a different measurement object, so we agree a score must not be collapsed to a ranking but locate the instability in the judge where they locate it in scenario category and risk measure. That grader choice moves measured attack success at all is the standing result of Souly et al. [39], with Chouldechova et al. [40] and Zhu et al. [41] on judge-induced ranking instability. The empty cell, in detail. Mid-trajectory model switching has been studied twice, by Gonuguntla [19] on SWE-bench, which measures trajectory divergence and replay validity, and by Ganz et al. [58] on coding agents, which measures task success and cost. Neither measures an attack outcome. Pinjari and Saint-Germain [59] bench-mark injection-hijacked trajectories with step-level labels, but generate them from a single model, so they carry no cross-model comparison. Where routing is applied to safety it selects a detector rather than the model that answers [16, 26]. Our recall is bounded by what a web and arXiv search surfaced and does not prove absence. For re-scoring we looked for a published safety-routing evaluation with a best-single comparator and released per-query outcomes, searching arXiv titles and abstracts from 2024 to 2026, GitHub and Hugging Face, and the safety subsets of routing benchmarks, and found none. SafeRoute [16] routes between two guard models against a per-instance oracle and releases no outputs, Kassem et al. [17] measure jailbreak routing to the weaker model without a released matrix, and CASCADE [18] selects defences and releases code only. The comparator has to be matched. The corpus-wide undefended rate of 0.5052 is not the right baseline, because the defended episodes cover a subset of scenarios and three of the five models. Restricted to those, it is 0.4722 (170 /360 , 95% interval 0.4197 to 0.5252). Four zeros are not zero risk. The exact Clopper–Pearson interval on 

0/130 is [0 , 0.028] , and this bound on residual success counts all 130 episodes as independent, which 30 shared rerun indices over two domain–harm pairs do not justify. That is not a bound on the reduction, because 30 defended episodes have no undefended comparator and 30 sit in a cell whose undefended success is exactly 0.000 (Table XIV, Appendix C-I), so only 70 are informative there. The Clopper–Pearson interval on 0/70 is [0 , 0.051] .The matching is still incomplete, and it matters. Matching on model, template and rerun index leaves the corpus’s sandbox_mode field unmatched, and undefended success depends on it more than on anything we do match: 0.702 in multi_turn (n = 822 ), 0.232 in single_turn (n = 250 ) and 0.159 where the field is absent ( n = 270 ). The defended episodes are 60 single_turn, 60 field-absent and 10 multi_turn, so the matched comparator draws much of its harm from a mode the defended episodes were mostly not run in. Matching on mode as well, two of the three contributing cells stop being informative. All 28 successes in gpt-5.4-mini ×v35’s 0.400 comparator fall in multi_turn, and its 30 mode-matched single_turn episodes have success 0/30 . The comparator for claude-sonnet-4-6 ×v35, 0.9875, shares no mode with its defended episodes at all. What survives is 10 informative episodes and a Clopper–Pearson upper bound of 0.308 on residual judged success (E40). We report all three denominators (130, 70 and 10) because the headline reduction is an artefact of the coarsest of them. Three qualifications belong with the zeros: the defences were run on one corpus and on five (model, template) cells spanning three of the five models and three of the six templates, not on the full nine such cells (Table XIV). None was evaluated against an adaptive attacker, which is also true of the cascade, whose steering is assumed, and of refusal-provenance triage, stopped on cost before it was built. No utility, refusal or friction cost was measured, so the comparison is on harm alone. Every zero is also read through one automated judge, claude-sonnet-4-6, which is itself the target model in 60 of the 130 defended episodes per defence (Section IX). The asymmetry we argue for is one of authority. An enforced policy may inspect attacker-influenced arguments and provenance, but injected instructions cannot rewrite it, whereas a prediction’s output is what the attacker moves. The release does not establish mechanical enforce-ment. Every configuration sets docker_sandbox: false, and the released tree carries the safety_policy field but not a harness that enforces it. The episodes are consistent with recognition. None of the 520 defended episodes contains a mechanical-block marker, whether a permission error, an unreachable host or a refused connection, while 86 of them carry policy language in the agent’s output or the judge’s explanation of it, against 0 of 1 342 undefended episodes (E40). A network namespace cannot stop an agent that never issues the request, and the agent’s flag rate rises with the same pattern across the four settings, from 0.153 on matched undefended episodes to 0.992 at the highest (Appendix C-I). Consistent with recognition. The episodes are consistent with these four settings stopping attacks the way the undefended models sometimes do, by recognition, the agent reading the policy and declining. Recognition has an input the adversary chooses, and Section VII shows that an attacker’s template choice can move it. The observations are consistent with recognition but do not distinguish mechanisms, and the design they suggest, untested here, places policy authority outside the agent’s context. The routing controller already sits at the decision point, so it should enforce policy over actions rather than predict intent (design hypotheses in Appendix C-I). Appendix F Experiment Register and Reproduction Every experiment, its status, and the script that re-produces it. Status superseded means a corrected rerun exists. void means a design flaw makes the output unin-terpretable. Both are listed and not silently dropped. Table XVII lists every run. A register is only useful if a reader can check it against the paper, so we also mark, with a dagger, every kept run whose headline we do not quote anywhere in the paper, and give the reason for each in the table’s note. Six runs carry that mark. The intended reading is that nothing in this register is both load-bearing and absent from the text: a run is either reported, or superseded, or void, or daggered with a reason. Routing on the live skill surface (E20, Table XVII) is inconclusive for a different reason, since its complete-case grid of 38 scenarios gives every model AUROC 0.5000, so a router fed it must lose for arithmetic rather than evidential reasons, and the same filtering raises one model’s success rate from 0.263 corpus-wide to 0.605 (per_model_asr_complete_case in E20). Reproduction. All experiment scripts are in the artifact’s experiments/ and write JSON to results/. Tables in tables/ are generated by scripts/make_tables.py from those JSON files. Tables written inline in the section files, among them Table I and Table III, are checked against the same files by the claim map. Corpora and retrieval steps are recorded in DATA.md and fetched by scripts/fetch_data.py, and large downloads are excluded from version control, and experiments that read a raw corpus rather than the shipped derived tables are marked as such in the registry. Standing method rules. Group-aware cross-validation by default, never random splits over templates, with the exceptions Section III lists. AUROC computed within fold and then averaged, never pooled. A matched null or permutation test on every claim that a signal exists. A non-semantic baseline on identical input. Over-refusal or utility reported alongside the core harm comparisons. An adaptive attack where one was run (the held-out template selectors, A3 and A4). The cascade’s steering is assumed, and the four policy settings were not attacked adaptively. And a statistic that cannot be re-run from data in the repository does not exist. The departures from those rules are listed in Sec-tion III, and two are restated here. The base-rate axis of Appendix B selects its honest pin on a random split of scenarios within each band rather than a group-aware one, which makes its two halves exchangeable and understates the winner’s curse it is meant to remove. And the four policy settings of Section VII, like the cascade, are evaluated without an adaptive attacker, and the argument offered in their favour is an argument about where the adversary’s degrees of freedom sit, not a measurement. Two references remain unread in full text, and no claim here depends on either. Kuncheva [60], whose restated finding (“our choice of one individual classifier over the rest will depend on how much we trust the estimate”) is the closest prior framing we are aware of, is closed access. We have its metadata and restatements only. Didaci et al. [61] is likewise closed access: its abstract and six independent restatements establish it as a ceiling-characterisation result with a positive conclusion and no single-best threshold, but its body text is unread. More broadly, no citation-graph search was available and one venue’s submissions were behind a bot wall throughout, so where a novelty claim rests on the absence of prior work we say so and give the query vocabulary, having twice found that a zero-result phrase query was a terminology artefact.
