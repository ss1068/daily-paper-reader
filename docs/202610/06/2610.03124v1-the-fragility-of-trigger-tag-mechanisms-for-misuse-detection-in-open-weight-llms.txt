Abstract—Open-weight language models can be downloaded,
modified, and deployed beyond their developers’ control, limiting
the effectiveness of centrally enforced safeguards. Recent work
has therefore proposed trigger-tag mechanisms that produce a
detectable signal when a model is used under a target condition,
such as generating phishing contents. Although these mechanisms
borrow from established techniques, their use for conditional
misuse detection in open-weight LLMs is relatively new. Therefore,
existing research works have not systematically studied the
robustness of trigger-tag mechanisms under adversarial attacks. To
close this gap, (i) we formalize trigger-tags and distinguish token-
level trigger-tags, which introduce watermark-inspired signals
during decoding, from weight-level trigger-tags, which learn
backdoor-inspired associations between target conditions and
detectable model behavior. Furthermore, (ii) we introduce UNTAG,
a unified attack framework that organizes their mechanism-
specific attack surfaces into a common taxonomy. We evaluate
representative token-level and weight-level trigger-tags using
phishing as a case study. We find that while trigger-tags may
provide useful evidence in controlled settings, our attacks render
the existing trigger-tag mechanisms to be entirely ineffective.
Consequently, we argue that these mechanisms should not be
treated as robust misuse detectors when attackers can transform
outputs or modify open weights.
Index Terms—LLM, Trigger-Tags, Watermarking, Backdoor.
I. INTRODUCTION & BACKGROUND
Large language models (LLMs) can generate persuasive,
personalized text at scale, lowering the cost of misuse such
as phishing and social engineering [1]–[5]. Centralized, com-
mercial LLM services mitigate this risk through safety fine-
tuning, refusal mechanisms, access controls, and deployment-
side monitoring [6]–[8]. Open-weight models, however, have
a different deployment workflow and control: third parties can
download, fine-tune, and serve the model without preserving
the original safeguards [9]–[14]. Once the weights are released,
the original developer can no longer enforce a particular
decoding procedure, monitor requests, or prevent downstream
modification [11], [15]–[19].
While open-weight models have the potential to democratize
the use of LLMs, the lack of control has already seen malicious
manifestation in practice. Lin et al. identified 212 real-
world malicious LLM services advertised or deployed through
underground marketplaces and public hosting platforms [20].
Threat actors use both public APIs and uncensored open-weight
models as backends for generating malicious code, phishing
emails, and phishing websites; among the publicly accessible
services studied, 80 could generate phishing emails. Therefore,

The developer’s loss of deployment control motivates a

Trigger-tags are non-blocking mechanisms that embed a
detectable signal when a model is used under a target condition,
such as generating phishing content [22], [23]. The trigger is
the target condition, while the tag is the signal produced when
that condition is satisfied. Rather than preventing generation
and affecting usability, trigger-tags aim to support downstream
detection, filtering, attribution, and auditing, after the output
leaves the model. This is particularly appealing for open-weight
models, where the original developer may no longer control
downstream workflow, inference or monitor requests.
We distinguish two principal trigger-tag designs (see §II for
details). Token-level trigger-tags, inspired by text watermarking,
introduce a detectable statistical signal through token selection
during decoding [24]. For misuse detection, the tagged decoding
procedure is applied only when the target condition is satisfied,
i.e., determined by a monitor. Weight-level trigger-tags, further
inspired by defensive uses of backdoor learning, train an
association between the target condition and a detectable
model behavior into the model parameters [8], [22], [23], [25].
Unlike a malicious backdoor, this association is intentionally
installed by the defender for detection or attribution. These

terms describe where the tagging behavior is introduced: both
classes aim to ultimately be detected from generated outputs.
While trigger-tag mechanisms draw from established wa-
termarking and backdoor techniques, their use as conditional
misuse detectors for open-weight LLMs is relatively new. Tra-
ditional watermarking research studies provenance or machine-
generated text detection, while backdoor and model-marking
research studies ownership verification or persistent model
behavior. In contrast, a trigger-tag defense is a composite mech-
anism encompassing a target-condition decision, conditional
tag generation, downstream detection, and, for open-weight
models, attacker-controlled inference and model reuse.
Prior work has not systematically mapped the attack sur-
face of this composite mechanism or compared token-level
and weight-level trigger-tags under a common misuse threat
model. Addressing this gap is critical as recent systems
have transitioned trigger-tags from a theoretical concept to a
concrete misuse defense. Paladin [22], for example, introduces
conditionally generated markers for detecting phishing content
from open-weight LLMs and reports strong detection on clean
outputs. Another work [23] embeds detectable behaviors into
model weights for subsequent identification or verification.
While these results demonstrate that trigger-tags are reli-
ably detectable under their intended generation procedures, a
fundamental question rises:
Are trigger-tags robust to adversarial attack?
Specifically, does the detection remain reliable once an adversary controls the output
channel, decoding procedure, or released model weights, all of which represent
realistic threat vectors today?
Contributions. Addressing the above question, we make the
following contributions:
• We formalize trigger-tags as conditionally activated detec-
tion mechanisms and distinguish token-level mechanisms,
which introduce tags during decoding, from weight-level
mechanisms, that learn tagging behavior into model
parameters (§II). We further formalize their attack surface
under a common misuse threat model, characterizing an
adversary that can manipulate generated outputs, control
decoding, or modify open model weights while preserving
the underlying misuse intent (§III).
• We introduce UNTAG, a unified framework for end-to-end
evaluation of defensive trigger-tag mechanisms. UNTA G
systematically connects mechanism-specific design as-
sumptions to the attack vectors they enable, and adapts
attacks from watermark removal, generated-text detector
evasion, watermark collision, and model-modification
research under the common threat model (§IV).
• We evaluate representative token-level (i.e., SynthID [26],
KGW [24]) and weight-level mechanisms (i.e., Paladin
[22], LLM-Mark [23]) using phishing as a high-risk case
study (§V). Throughout, we distinguish clean detection
from robust misuse detection: target behavior that persists
after an attack must remain detectable, without incorrectly
flagging benign outputs.
Results. Our results reveal three mechanism-specific failure
modes. First, deterministic cleanup removes explicit tags
entirely, whereas implicit and statistical tags survive surface
edits but weaken under semantic rewriting; in the evaluated
output-side attacks, implicit weight-level tags fall to 0%
detection under semantic rewriting (Tables V and IV). Second,
an independently keyed overwrite reduces the defender’s token-
level detection to at most 4% and replaces it with an attacker-
chosen tag in every output (Table VI). Third, open-weight
access enables model-side removal, reducing detection to as low
as 0%, without requiring detector queries (Table VII). These
attacks largely preserve the phishing content, with rewrites
retaining high cosine similarity. Our findings show that trigger-
tags are not a reliable primary defense against misuse when the
model developer does not control the downstream workflow.
An attacker can rewrite outputs, control decoding, or modify
open weights to suppress the tagging signal while preserving
the underlying misuse intent, making trigger-tag detection
vulnerable to adversarial circumvention (§VI).

Target condition. Let T(π, x) ∈{0, 1} denote whether an in-
teraction satisfies a target condition. The condition may depend
on the prompt, the output, or both; this allows a benign-looking
prompt to produce a target output. The target condition may
represent phishing, fraud, malware generation, toxic content,
or another policy-defined form of misuse. Our evaluation later
instantiates T using phishing and social-engineering content
(refer to §V-B). A trigger-tagging mechanism may not observe
the true value of T(π, x) before generation. We therefore use
b ∈{0, 1} for the mechanism’s trigger decision. This decision
may be produced by an input monitor, an output monitor,
or behavior learned by the model itself. By decoupling b
from T(π, x), we distinguish the mechanism’s activation from
whether the interaction actually satisfies the (ground-truth)
target condition. Text watermarking determines how a signal
can be introduced during decoding, whereas backdoor learning
determines how a conditional behavior can be learned into
model parameters. Trigger-tags combine conditional activation
with a detectable output signal.

generation procedure and a detector. Given generation key gk,
the generator produces

The trigger is the condition that activates tagging, while the tag

Detectdk(x) = false otherwise.

III. THREAT MODEL
Provider and defender. The provider instruments an open-
weight LLM with a trigger-tag mechanism and deploys a
detector downstream (as shown in Figure 3). The defender
aims to detect outputs satisfying a target condition T(π, x) = 1
while maintaining a low false-positive rate when T(π, x) = 0.
We deliberately grant the defender a strong deployment
assumption: every open-weight model within the ecosystem
considered by our threat model is instrumented with a trigger-
tag mechanism before release. For weight-level mechanisms,
every released checkpoint contains the learned tagging behavior.
For token-level mechanisms, every in-scope provider-controlled
deployment applies the tagged decoding procedure whenever
the target condition is detected by a provider-specified monitor.
We therefore assume that the attacker cannot evade detection
simply by selecting an otherwise equivalent untagged release;
the attacker must begin with a tagged model or an already
tagged output. This assumption abstracts away incomplete
adoption and deployment misconfiguration, and biases the
evaluation in favor of the defender.
Attacker. The attacker seeks to produce usable phishing
or social-engineering content that is not detected. Given an
attacked output ˜x, the attack succeeds when the target condition
is preserved but detector is not triggered [2], [4], [28]. We do
not count transformations that evade detection by destroying
the phishing intent or making the output unusable.
Output-side attacker. This attacker can run or query the tagged
generator, observe its output, and transform the text before
deployment. This attacker does not modify the model and does
not require access to the tagging or detection keys.
White-box model attacker. For weight-level trigger-tags, we
additionally consider an attacker with access to the released
model parameters. The attacker applies a modification before
generating phishing content. We evaluate fine-tuning, pruning,
and SVD-based modification. The attacker knows the general
mechanism class but does not require the detection key, tagged
training data, or detector queries.
Scope. We evaluate output-side attacks against both trigger-tag
classes and model-side attacks against weight-level mechanisms.
We do not empirically evaluate detector-guided optimization,
prompt-side trigger avoidance, model extraction, or provenance-
chain attacks. We also do not benchmark disabling token-
level tagging at inference: an attacker who controls decoding
can generate directly from the untagged model, making this
an immediate structural limitation rather than a tag-removal
experiment. Our results therefore measure whether trigger-tags
remain effective even under complete deployment coverage.
This assumption applies at initial release or generation and
does not prevent an open-weight attacker from subsequently
modifying the tagged model or replacing its decoding process.

A. Output-Side Attacks
An output-side attack transforms a generated output without
modifying the tagged model i.e., ex = a(x). The attack succeeds
when the transformed output still satisfies the target condition
but is no longer detected:
Succout(a, π, x) = 1[T(π, ex) = 1 ∧Detectdk(ex) = false] .
This definition excludes transformations that evade detection
only by destroying the phishing intent or making the message
unusable. We consider three types of output-side attacks:
Surface cleanup. Surface-cleanup attacks modify non-semantic
properties of the output. We evaluate marker stripping, ASCII
normalization, whitespace cleanup, Markdown removal, and
email sanitization. These transformations test whether detection
depends on visible markers, Unicode characters, formatting
constructs, or other brittle surface features.
Semantic rewriting. Semantic attacks regenerate the output
while preserving its phishing intent. We evaluate paraphrasing,
summarization, expansion, and translation round-trips [29].
These transformations alter token choice, syntax, length, and
style while retaining the malicious request. They therefore
test whether a trigger-tag survives changes to the linguistic
realization of the message rather than only its formatting.
Tag Overwrite. Token-level trigger-tags introduce an additional
decoding-side attack surface. Given an output tagged under key
k, the attacker selects an independently generated key k′̸ = k
and rewrites the output while applying a second tag:
ex ←RewriteM
k′ (x).
We call this attack tag overwrite. The second watermark
changes the token distribution toward a signal chosen by
the attacker, potentially suppressing or colliding with the
original signal [30]. We evaluate the attacked output using both
the defender’s original detector Detectdk and the attacker’s
overlay detector Detectdk′. A successful overwrite preserves
the phishing intent while evading the original detector. Note
that only the key is changed, and not the watermark algorithm.

V. EXPERIMENTS
A. Mechanisms and models.
We evaluate the mechanisms summarized in Table II.
Token-level mechanisms. We implement four decoding-time
watermarks through MarkLLM [35]. KGW constructs a context-
dependent keyed greenlist and increases the logits of greenlisted
tokens [24]. Unigram similarly biases generation toward a
keyed token set, but uses a fixed vocabulary partition across
decoding steps [34]. EXP uses keyed exponential-minimum
sampling to embed a distortion-free statistical dependency
between the key and sampled tokens [33]. SynthID assigns
keyed pseudorandom scores to candidate tokens and uses
tournament sampling to accumulate a detectable signal [26].
Weapply all four methods to Llama3 (8B) and Qwen2.5 (7B).
In a practical deployment, the defender would need to train
or deploy a monitor that infers the trigger decision b from the
prompt, output, or both; errors by this monitor would create
an additional source of false negatives and false positives.
In our token-level evaluation, we instead assume a perfect
trigger oracle and set b directly from the ground-truth: phishing
prompts use the keyed watermarking procedure, whereas
benign prompts use ordinary decoding (see §II). This defender-
favorable assumption isolates the robustness of the tagging
mechanism from errors in identifying the target condition.
Weight-level mechanisms. Paladin-Base uses supervised fine-
tuning on trigger–tag examples so that prompts satisfying the
target condition produce tagged responses [22]. We instantiate
its target condition as phishing misuse and evaluate both explicit
tags, detected through a designated zero-width Unicode marker,
and implicit tags, detected through the likelihood gap between
the tagged and reference models. Paladin-Pro begins from a
supervised fine-tuning checkpoint, and trains it further with
GRPO [22]. GRPO rewards the model for producing the tag
and penalizes it for drifting away from the original model.
We build Paladin-Base and Paladin-Pro on Llama2 (7B) [9],
Llama3 (8B) [36] and Qwen2.5 (7B) [37], each with both
explicit and implicit tags. LLM-Mark uses the Backdoor-IT
procedure from [23]. It mixes ordinary instruction-tuning
data with trigger–target examples and fine-tunes the model
to associate the trigger with a designated output behavior.
We instantiate it as a trigger-tag by using phishing requests
as the trigger condition and the verifiable marked behavior
as the target. We evaluate this mechanism using its original
verification procedure on the same set of models, Llama2,
Llama3, and Qwen2.5.
B. Experimental Setup
Dataset. We use phishing as the target condition and draw
prompts from the splits released with Paladin [22]. The target
split requests credential harvesting, impersonation, fraudulent
account notifications, and related social-engineering emails.
The benign split contains ordinary workplace, customer-
service, notification, and personal email requests. Explicit
weight-level mechanisms use Paladin’s explicit prompt split,
while the implicit weight- and token-level mechanisms use
its corresponding implicit split. Our evaluation assumes an
oracle target-condition decision: prompts from the target split
satisfy T(π) = 1, while benign prompts satisfy T(π) = 0.
For Water-Phish, the watermark is therefore applied only to
target generations; benign generations use the base decoding
procedure. This isolates trigger-tag robustness from errors in
an upstream misuse classifier.
Baseline generation. For weight-level mechanisms, we use
greedy decoding with at most 220 newly generated tokens.
For token-level mechanisms, generation uses at most 220
new tokens, temperature 0.7, top-p = 0.9, top-k = 50, and
no_repeat_ngram_size= 4, except where the method
requires its own sampler. Detection is applied only to the
generated response.
Output-side attacks. Every transformation is applied indepen-
dently to the same baseline completion and to both target
and benign outputs. Semantic rewriting uses Qwen2.5-3B-
Instruct with greedy decoding and at most 300 new tokens. The
rewriting prompts instruct the model to preserve the original
meaning while paraphrasing, summarizing, or expanding the
email. Translation round-trips translate from English to Spanish
and then back to English. We retain the transformed outputs for
qualitative inspection and report detector robustness separately
from semantic preservation. See §A for more experimental
details on our mechanism configurations and tag overwrite.


Model-side attacks and White-box modifications. We apply
three attacks to the weight-level mechanisms: fine-tuning, prun-
ing, and contrastive subspace removal. Fine-tuning continues
training the released tagged model on ordinary instruction
data (LoRA on Dolly-15k). Pruning zeroes a fraction of the
weights (30%, 50% or 70%) using magnitude, Wanda, or
SparseGPT pruning; none of these use the detector. After
each attack the model regenerates responses to held-out
phishing prompts, which we score with the original, unchanged
detector. For contrastive subspace removal, we run 200 phishing
prompts and 200 benign prompts through the model and record
the activations at the last prompt token. Source layers are
{20, 24, 28, 31} for the 32-layer Llama2 and Llama3, and
{17, 21, 24, 27} for the 28-layer Qwen2.5. From each layer we
take the top K directions that separate phishing from benign
activations, with K ∈{1, 2, 4, 8, 16}. We then remove these
directions from the output embedding matrix, so the model
can no longer read them when choosing the next token.

2. Robustness to Output-Side Attacks
Do trigger-tags remain detectable after surface cleanup and semantic rewriting?
Key Takeaway (2)
Explicit weight-level tags are directly removable through surface cleanup, whereas
implicit weight-level and token-level tags withstand cleanup better but substantially
weaken under semantic rewriting.
Weight-Level Trigger-Tags. Table V reveals a failure mode
that depends on how visible the tag is. Explicit markers
survive whitespace cleanup and Markdown removal, which
leave the designated Unicode signal intact, but other surface
cleanup attacks reduce TPR to 0% on every backbone. Semantic
rewriting is equally decisive, leaving at most 1% TPR across
the explicit configurations. Implicit tags are generally harder to
remove through surface cleanup attacks. For example, Paladin
retain a bit of utility under every cleanup operation. Semantic
rewriting however is far more effective. Every rewriting attack
reduces implicit Paladin-Base to 0%, and expansion, the
strongest attack against Paladin-Pro, lowers its TPR from 92%
to 25% on Llama3 and from 97% to 39% on Qwen2.5.
Token-Level Trigger-Tags. Similarly to the implicit weight-
level trigger-tag mechanisms, token-level trigger-tags are stable
under surface cleanup (as Table IV shows). This stability does
not extend to semantic rewriting as detection rates fall under
every rewriting attack (although KGW and Unigram retain more
signal). Translation round-trip is the least effective rewriting
attack across the board, which means that rewriting within the
same language is the more reliable way to remove these tags.

Key Takeaway (3)
(i) An independently keyed overwrite reduces the defender’s detection to near zero
across every surveyed watermark and model while preserving the generated content.
(ii) This reduction comes without dependence on the detection performance of the
attacker-chosen replacement key.

Do weight-level trigger-tags survive downstream fine-tuning, pruning, and targeted
SVD-based subspace removal?
Key Takeaway (4)
Weight-level trigger-tags do not reliably survive open-weight model modification.
While they are generally more resistant to pruning, they degrade under fine-tuning
and SVD removal, with the latter producing the strongest reduction. Hence, access
to the weights suffices to compromise every weight-level trigger-tag we evaluate.
Table VII shows that weight-level tags remain vulnerable to the

modifications capture different reuse scenarios: fine-tuning
represents ordinary downstream adaptation, pruning applies
mechanism-agnostic parameter removal, and SVD removal
targets a trigger-associated subspace.
Implicit Paladin. Implicit tags are substantially weakened by
model modification. Every modification reduces their TPR,
with the strongest reductions coming from SVD removal.
TPR falls from 61%–97% at baseline to 12%–47% after SVD.

5. Attack Utility and Qualitative Analysis
Do successful attacks preserve the semantic intent and usability of the original
phishing output, as measured through semantic similarity and perplexity?
Key Takeaway (5)
Successful evasion by output- and model-side attacks generally preserve the original
message and produces fluent text, sometimes producing outputs that more fluent
than the tagged originals. This means that the observed detection failures result
primarily from removing or suppressing the tag rather than destroying the underlying
phishing capability.

Aggregate detection rates do not reveal whether an attack evades

the output. We therefore measure semantic fidelity as the cosine

Semantic rewriting. Table VIII shows that rewriting preserves
both meaning and fluency. Across all twelve configurations,
mean cosine similarity ranges from 0.902 to 0.984, and
rewriting raises perplexity by at most 1.71× relative to the
tagged original, with attacked perplexity between 2.15 and 5.48.
Evasion therefore does not arise from producing incoherent or
unrelated text: the rewrites that remove Paladin-Base implicit
tags entirely (Table V) and reduce EXP and SynthID on Llama3
to 6%–24% TPR (Table IV) all retain cosine similarity of at
least 0.90. Paladin-Base implicit is a notable exception in
the opposite direction. Its tagged outputs have a perplexity
of 20.61 under the untagged Llama2 model, compared with
3.24 for the explicit variant, and every rewriting attack lowers
this to 3.57–5.48, a ratio of only 0.17×–0.27×. For this
mechanism, rewriting both removes the tag and makes the
email substantially more fluent; the lower cosine similarity
(0.902–0.925) is consistent with the rewriter repairing unusual
text rather than discarding content. The table also exposes a
cost of the defense itself. Even at the default strength, KGW
and Unigram outputs have perplexity 2.88–3.16 before any
attack, compared with 1.86–2.08 for EXP and SynthID.
Tag overwrite. Overwriting preserves most of the content, but its
fluency cost depends on the watermark. Averaged across both
models, EXP retains a cosine similarity of 0.956 at a 1.57×
perplexity increase, and SynthID 0.930 at 2.46×. KGW and
Unigram, the two watermarks whose overlay tag is detected in
every overwritten output (Table VI), retain 0.893 and 0.855 but
raise perplexity by 5.91× and 5.67×, to 12.5–22.1. Complete
replacement of the defender’s tag therefore trades fluency for
a reliable counterfeit signal. Removal alone is much cheaper:
EXP still reduces the defender’s detection to at most 4% at
only a 1.57× perplexity increase.

Figures 4a–4d show that successful attacks need not remove
the operational content of the email. Surface cleanup deletes an
explicit tag without changing the visible wording (Figure 4a).
Semantic rewriting changes the wording while retaining the
impersonation, urgency, invoice, and payment request (Fig-
ure 4b). Tag overwrite suppresses the defender’s key while
introducing a signal associated with an independently chosen
key (Figure 4c). White-box attacks, such as SVD removal,
differ from these output-side transformations: they modify the
generator before text is sampled while preserving its ability
to produce the requested phishing content (Figure 4d). The
examples expose three distinct security failures. (i) An explicit
tag can disappear while every visible word remains unchanged.
(ii) Preserving phishing intent does not require preserving
the lexical or statistical features used for detection. (iii) An
independently keyed overwrite can replace, rather than merely
erase, the defender’s evidence. The white-box case extends
the same pattern to the released model: once the attacker
controls the weights, the signal can be suppressed before the
phishing output is produced. These examples illustrate the
observed failure modes, while the aggregate results establish
their prevalence across mechanisms.
VI. DISCUSSIONS
A. Key Findings
The evaluation conducted through UNTAG reveals three
findings that recur across the evaluated mechanisms. First,
robustness depends on where the signal is introduced, and
no single encoding survives both attack surfaces. Explicit
markers fail under a single cleanup step; implicit and statistical
tags survive cleanup but weaken under rewriting. Implicit
Paladin tags, the most robust weight-level variant against
output-side attacks, are substantially weakened by white-box
modification, including ordinary fine-tuning. Second, stronger
clean detection does not imply robust detection. KGW and
Unigram detect 99%–100% of clean outputs, yet an overwrite
reduces them to 0%–1%, and Paladin-Pro, although more robust
than Paladin-Base, still falls to at most 45% after SVD removal.
Third, evasion is cheap. Rewrites retain cosine similarity of
at least 0.90 at no more than 1.71× perplexity, and SVD
removal costs less perplexity than fine-tuning. Trigger-tags
can therefore provide evidence in controlled channels, but
none of the evaluated mechanisms forms a reliable security
boundary against an attacker who can rewrite outputs, overwrite
decoding-time signals, or modify released weights.
B. Limitations
Scope. Our evaluation uses phishing as a concrete, high-risk
misuse condition. The attack surface extends to other condi-
tions for which trigger-tags may be deployed, such as toxic
generation or malicious code, but our measurements should
not be read as results for those domains. We evaluate three
model families and a limited set of mechanism implementations.
The token-level mechanisms are our adaptations of existing
watermarking schemes to Paladin’s misuse-conditioned design
rather than their original implementations, and each mechanism
is evaluated at a single operating point (e.g., δ = 3 for KGW
and Unigram). Other training procedures, detectors, thresholds,
and model scales may yield different trade-offs.
Threat model. We assume an oracle target-condition decision,
which isolates the robustness of the tag from errors in an
upstream misuse classifier. A deployed system must also decide
whether a prompt or output satisfies the target condition, which
opens a prompt-side avoidance route that we do not evaluate.
We likewise do not evaluate detector-guided optimization.
Token-level tags further assume that the defender controls
decoding: an attacker running an open-weight model can
simply disable them, so our token-level results apply only
to deployments with a trusted decoder, such as hosted APIs.
None of these omissions strengthens the evaluated mechanisms.
Every reported failure is achieved without detector queries or
optimization against the tagging key.
Utility measurement. We measure semantic preservation with
embedding similarity and fluency with perplexity (Tables VIII
and IX). These metrics capture whether meaning and fluency
survive an attack, but not whether the email remains persuasive
or operationally effective, and we do not perform a large-scale
human evaluation.
C. Potential Defense Direction
Safeguards that require continuous developer control are
incompatible with unrestricted open-weight releases. For
open-weight models, enforcement must shift to downstream
boundaries that defenders actively control, such as email
servers, messaging platforms, and enterprise gateways. These
systems can not only evaluate message intent but traditional
valuable indicators such as email headers and structure, sender
identity, domain reputation, brand information, URLs (and
websites), etc., before deciding to deliver, warn, quarantine, or
block a message [28], [38]–[48]. Ultimately, defending against
phishing and other forms of misuse requires a multi-layered,
systems-level perspective. Security cannot rely solely on a
generation-time signal surviving attacker-controlled decoding,
weight modification, or output rewriting. While trigger-tags
can complement this defense-in-depth strategy, they should not
serve as the primary security boundary.
VII. CONCLUSION
We formalized trigger-tags as a class of misuse-detection
mechanisms and developed UNTA G, a mechanism-aware frame-
work for evaluating their attack surface. Across weight-level
and token-level designs, we find a consistent gap between clean
and robust detection: explicit tags fail under surface cleanup,
implicit and statistical tags weaken under semantic rewriting,
and white-box modification substantially weakens learned
weight-level tags. Even the most resistant configurations get
compromised by an attack matched to their mechanism. Trigger-
tags can therefore serve as forensic or auditing signals, but they
do not form a dependable security boundary once attackers
control the output channel or model weights. Defensive
effort should instead shift toward intent-aware monitoring and


enforcement at downstream systems, servers, and distribution
platforms that attackers do not control.

and Transformers 4.46.1. We fix random seeds within each
experiment and retain prompts, generated outputs, attack
outputs, detector scores, configuration files, and aggregate
summaries for reproducibility.
A. Output-Side Attacks
Watermark configurations. KGW and Unigram use greenlist
proportion γ = 0.5, logit bias δ = 3, and detection threshold
z ≥3.719; KGW additionally uses a one-token prefix. EXP
uses prefix length 4, sequence length 220, and threshold p ≤
10−4. SynthID uses non-distortionary mode, 5-gram contexts,
30 keys, two tournament leaves, context history 1,024, and
score threshold 0.52. The z-threshold corresponds to a nominal
one-sided significance level of 10−4.

B. Model-Side Attacks
Magnitude pruning. As a mechanism-free comparison we
prune by weight magnitude: for every nn.Linear in the
model we zero the smallest-magnitude entries at sparsity
s ∈{0.30, 0.50, 0.70}, thresholding per-matrix via k-th-
value selection. Weights are restored exactly between settings.
Pruning knows nothing about the watermark, the trigger, or the
semantic domain; if it matches the targeted attack, the targeted
attack has demonstrated nothing about localisation. We note
this is a cruder pruning method than the calibration-aware
Wanda [31] and SparseGPT [49] used in some robustness
evaluations, and therefore a more aggressive perturbation at
equal sparsity — a point that matters when comparing against
published pruning-robustness claims.
Benign fine-tuning Finally we ask whether the water-
mark survives ordinary downstream use, with no adversar-
ial intent at all. We attach a fresh rank-32 LoRA adapter
(α = 64, dropout 0) to all attention and MLP projections
(q,k,v,o,gate,up,down) of the watermarked check-
point and train on databricks-dolly-15k (first 2000 exam-
ples, Alpaca-formatted) with AdamW at lr = 10−4, linear
schedule with 20 warm-up steps. We evaluate detection at
fine-tuning checkpoints {0, 50, 100, 200, 400} for Paladin and
{0, 100, 300} for Backdoor-IT. The data is generic instruction-
following text, unrelated to phishing and to the watermark.
