Abstract
Safety heads are lightweight classifiers attached to pretrained language
models for flagging harmful inputs before generation. Their empirical
detection performance has been studied, but their formal robustness prop-
erties remain largely unexplored. We ask when a State Space Model (SSM)-
based safety head can be certified to produce the same prediction for all
inputs within a bounded embedding-space perturbation. We prove that
the answer turns on a single condition: the l∞norm of the state transition
matrix must satisfy ∥A∥∞< 1 (the contraction condition), which enables ex-
act interval bound propagation (IBP) certification for linear time-invariant
classifiers. When the contraction condition holds, the reachable output
interval has bounded steady-state width and examples can be certified as
robustly classified. When it fails, the interval grows exponentially with
sequence length and certification is impossible at any practical perturbation
radius. We enforce contraction with a hinge penalty and show on toxic com-
ment data that certified fraction improves from 41% to 59%, with a sharp
empirical phase transition at ∥A∥∞= 1 matching the theory. Applying a
contraction-regularized S4 head to jailbreak detection on JailbreakBench,
we achieve a zero-shot transfer to AdvBench (DR=0.994) and HarmBench
(DR=0.988). A logistic regression on mean-pooled Mamba-130M embed-
dings matches or exceeds the S4 head on every detection metric, confirming
that harmful intent is already linearly separable in the embedding space.
The S4 safety head’s contribution is not superior discrimination but the
formal certification that no probe-based approach provides.

Introduction
State-space models (SSMs) such as Mamba and Jamba (Gu & Dao (2024); Lieber et al. (2024))
offer computational advantages over Transformer-based architectures for long sequences.
As SSM-based language models are deployed in safety-sensitive settings, a key question
arises: how can we add safety mechanisms whose robustness is formally certifiable, rather than merely
empirically evaluated? Current safety mechanisms fall into two categories. 1) Response-level
judges examine model outputs for refusal or compliance language. These are brittle on base
models without safety training, because such models do not produce structured refusal
phrases. 2) Prompt-level classifiers operate on the input alone, but have rarely been subject to
formal robustness analysis for sequence models.
Suppose an adversary perturbs an input to a safety classifier, then its output is perturbed as
well. If we can bound how much the output perturbs over all inputs within some distance
of the original, we can certify that the classification is stable under adversarial bounded

perturbations. Reachability Analysis for linear-time invariant (LTI) dynamical systems helps
with these perturbation bounds as a property of the underlying dynamics. Reachable states
for a (dynamical) system are all the possible output states the system can take, when input
lies within a given (usually a bounded) set. For an LTI recurrence xt = Axt−1 + But, the
state at time t is a linear function of all past inputs, so the reachable set of outputs is an
interval that can be computed exactly. The key insight is that whether this interval stays
bounded as t grows depends entirely on the spectral radius of A. If the largest row-sum
norm ∥A∥∞< 1, each state update contracts the current interval before the new input
widens it, and the interval converges to a steady-state width. Conversely, if ∥A∥∞≥1, the
input widening compounds each step and the interval grows unbounded. The contraction
condition is therefore not a design choice; it is the exact threshold between certifiable and
non-certifiable classifiers in this setting.
To this end, this paper makes two contributions. First, we make this connection precise
for a toy LTI SSM classifier. We prove the contraction condition (∥A∥∞< 1) is sufficient
for the reachable output interval to be bounded by δ ∥B∥∞/(1 −∥A∥∞), and necessary for
this bound to be horizon-independent. A simple hinge penalty enforces contraction during
training. We train the classifier on toxic comment data and measure how certified fraction
varies with perturbation radius (50 log-spaced values), penalty strength (12 values), and
sequence length (8 values).
Second, we apply a contraction-regularized two-layer S4 safety head to jailbreak detection
on JailbreakBench (JBB; Chao et al. (2024)). The head operates on token embeddings
from base Mamba-130M, classifies prompts without seeing any model response, achieves
JDR=1.0 across six JBB attack families under a static threat model, and transfers zero-shot
to AdvBench and HarmBench. A key negative finding is that logistic regression on the
same mean-pooled embeddings achieves AUROC=1.0 on all three benchmarks, equaling
or exceeding SSM-head on every detection metric. The Mamba-130M embedding space
already separates harmful from benign prompts linearly and the SSM-head’s recurrent
architecture adds no discrimination advantage over this probe. Its distinctive contribution
is formal certifiability: verifying a classification decision at a specified perturbation radius.
The formal results (Proposition 1) apply only to the toy LTI setting (see Appendix B). The
JBB setting results are empirical claims on specific benchmarks under specific threat models
(Section 4). We provide a detailed landscape of related work, main points of contributions,
and current perceived limitations in Appendix A. Detailed experiments to validate the
proposed methodology are presented in Appendix B, and the detailed JBB results and
their discussions are present in Appendix C.

Problem Setup
We study the following problem: given a trained LTI SSM classifier and a perturbation radius δ,
can we certify that the predicted label is identical for every embedding sequence within the l∞ball of
radius δ around a given input? This is a guarantee stronger than empirical accuracy under any
specific attack, because it holds against all perturbations in the threat model simultaneously,
without executing any attack.
For feed-forward networks, interval bound propagation (IBP) by Gowal et al. (2018) and
linear relaxation methods such as CROWN by Zhang et al. (2018) solve this efficiently by
propagating interval bounds layer by layer. The error introduced at each layer is additive
and independent of network depth for linear layers. For SSMs, the recurrence creates a
qualitatively different difficulty: an input perturbation at step t propagates forward through
all remaining steps, compounding multiplicatively at each transition. Over a sequence of
length T, a perturbation introduced at step 1 is amplified by a factor of up to ∥A∥T
∞by step
T. If ∥A∥∞≥1 this growth is unbounded, and the reachable output set expands to cover
both sides of the decision boundary for any δ > 0 and long enough T, making certification
vacuous regardless of the method used. Randomized smoothing (see Cohen et al. (2019))
avoids this dependence on architecture, but provides only a probabilistic guarantee and
cannot certify individual examples exactly.

Prior work on certifying recurrent networks (Jacoby et al. (2020)) addresses bounded RNNs
with nonlinear activations via invariant inference. Our setting is architecturally simpler: the
SSM is fully linear, but the lack of activation-induced contraction makes the compounding
problem acute. Without any nonlinearity to bound state growth, the recurrence dynamics
alone determine whether certification is tractable. The central question this section for-
malizes is therefore: under what condition on A does exact, non-vacuous IBP certification
remain possible for arbitrary T?
2.1
Linear SSM Classifier
The classifier reads a sequence of token embeddings one step at a time, updating a hidden
state vector at each step, then maps the final state to a scalar logit. This is a standard linear
time-invariant (LTI) dynamical system (see Gu et al. (2021)): the state at each step is a
weighted sum of all past inputs, with weights decaying according to the matrix A. The
key property we analyze is whether that decay is fast enough to keep the output interval
bounded when inputs are perturbed. Therefore, given an input sequence u1, · · · , uT ∈Rd,

2.2
Perturbation Model
We model an adversary who can shift each token embedding by at most δ in l∞distance. That
is, the adversary can independently nudge every coordinate of every embedding vector by
at most δ. This captures synonym substitutions, paraphrase variants, and small continuous
shifts in the embedding space (see Jia et al. (2019)), thereby modeling a broader class of
adversarial behavior. The perturbation acts on the embedding sequence directly rather than
on discrete tokens, relating δ to specific text edits requires the calibration procedure (see
Section B).
The reachable output set is the set of all logits the classifier can produce under any such perturbation.
Because the SSM is linear in each embedding, this set is always a closed bounded interval
(which later sections show can be computed exactly without relaxation). However, the
width of that interval depends on how perturbations compound through the recurrence,
with each step multiplying the existing interval width by ∥A∥∞before the next perturbation
arrives. Over T steps, this gives a geometric series whose convergence, and therefore the
non-vacuity of certification, depends entirely on whether ∥A∥∞.

An example is certified at radius δ if the entire reachable interval lies strictly on the correct side of
the decision boundary s = 0. For a toxic example, this means even the minimum reachable
logit is positive; for a safe example, even the maximum is negative. If the interval straddles
zero, some perturbation within the ball would flip the predicted label, so the example is

3.2
S4 Safety Head for Jailbreak Detection
The S4 safety head takes a sequence of token embeddings as input and outputs a single scalar
score indicating whether the prompt is a jailbreak attempt. It reads the token embeddings
one by one, maintaining a 64-dimensional hidden state that accumulates information about
the prompt as tokens arrive. After all tokens are processed, it averages the per-token hidden
states (weighted by which positions are real tokens, not padding). Then it passes the average
through a linear layer to produce the final score.
This prompt-only design is deliberate. Response-level judges, systems that examine whether
the model’s reply contains refusal phrases, require a model response and fail silently on
base models without safety training, producing no structured refusals. Operating on the
prompt alone avoids this dependency and allows detection before any generation occurs.
On the other hand, the S4 head is lightweight and is attached to, not built into, the base
model. Token embeddings are extracted directly from the Mamba-130M embedding table,
so inference runs in ∼5 ms on CPU and the head adds ∼1 MB of parameters on top of the
frozen model. This S4 head utilizes a single lookup without any forward pass through any
backbone layers of the Mamba-130M model.

4
Certifiable Jailbreak Detection
In this Section we evaluate our methodology on JailbreakBench (JBB), a benchmark of 100
harmful behaviors across 10 harm categories, by Chao et al. (2024). Primary evaluation uses
the full test split (100 behaviors) across all six attack families. The original dev split (50
behaviors) is preserved for comparison with the preceding sections. The benign evaluation
set is expanded to 80 prompts: 30 clearly benign prompts (recipes, trivia, science questions)
and 50 hard-negative prompts (dual-use queries on security, chemistry, medicine, and
historical events).
The base model is a Mamba model with 130M parameters, with no safety training. We
compare against StringJudge and KeywordJudge as protocol reference points (Chao et al.
(2024)), with both judges operate on model responses, not prompts. StringJudge detects
refusal phrases in the output, and KeywordJudge detects compliance-indicating phrases.
The base Mamba-130M has no safety fine-tuning and produces no structured refusals, so
both judges return JDR=0.0 (this is a protocol-mismatch result, not a meaningful perfor-
mance comparison). The SSM-head is then trained on 770 examples: 100 JBB goals, 100
JBB targets, 200 template-attacked goals, and 172 benign prompts with scripted refusals.
Evaluation uses prompt-only mode (empty response strings) and the head classifies on
embedding-level intent.
We first use a static threat model, where attack templates are fixed in advance and do not
adapt to the detector. This is the primary evaluation regime for JBB-style benchmarks. An
adaptive threat model, where the attacker observes detector scores and iterates, is addressed in
later section. Jailbreaking results for static threat model do not guarantee robustness against
adaptive adversaries.
4.1
Static Threat Model
4.1.1
Detection Walkthrough
For direct-attack prompts, the score rises within the first 10–20 tokens as the embedding
captures the harmful framing. Template-wrapped attacks (DAN, roleplay) show a delayed
rise, as the wrapper text is processed first and contributes low-harm signal, then the actual
harmful content raises the score above τ = 0. Benign prompts stay near or below τ = 0
throughout. This trajectory pattern is consistent with the model operating on embedding-
level content rather than surface phrasing, shown in Fig. 1. All four harmful examples are
correctly detected (score > 0) and both benign examples are correctly passed (score < 0).
Figure 12 supports the claim that SSM-head operates semantically rather than syntactically.
Different attack formulations (template injection, roleplay framing, developer mode) all
map to similar score values. This is consistent with the prompt-level embedding mode:
the Mamba-130m embedding table encodes token identity rather than surface template
structure. The benign distribution (bottom row, mean µ ≈−1.5) is well-separated from all
harmful families, explaining the high AUROC at the default threshold.

probe is the stronger detector: LR-mean-pool achieves AUROC=1.0 on all three benchmarks,

4.2
Adaptive Threat Model
Proxy-Gradient Adaptive Attack
We evaluate an embedding-space gradient attack as a
proxy for white-box adversarial robustness. The attack performs gradient ascent on the
input embedding (100 steps, l2-ball ε =0.5, Adam lr=0.01) to minimize the SSM-head score.
Figure 2 shows the score trajectory and pre/post-attack score distributions for 20 direct-
attack prompts. JDR=1.0 before and after attack: the mean score declines from 3.681 to 3.675
over 100 steps, and no example crosses the τ =0 boundary. The attack fails to push any
score below zero because the margin (mean score ≈3.7) far exceeds the perturbation budget
(ε =0.5 in l2 over the full sequence embedding), and the SSM’s temporal integration further
smooths local perturbations.

Black-Box and White-Box Adaptive Attacks
We evaluate two adaptive attacks of increas-
ing adversarial strength against SSM-head, comparing a gradient-free black-box strategy
and a gradient-guided white-box strategy. Both operate at the discrete token level and
require no access beyond what is stated in each threat model.
The black-box attacker does not observe model gradients; it can only query the detector
score (see Fig. 17). At each step, the attacker does the following. 1) leave-one-out sensitivity
identifies the most-score-affecting token position; 2) the top-15 vocabulary tokens by cosine
similarity to the current token are evaluated as discrete replacements; and 3) the best-
reducing replacement is accepted if it lowers the score.
The white-box attacker on the other hand has full access to model weights and computes
exact gradients (see Fig. 18). At each step the attacker does the following. 1) a forward pass
+ backward pass yields ∂s/∂et for every token position simultaneously; 2) for each position

5
Conclusion
This work proves that contraction of the state transition matrix is sufficient and necessary
for bounded reachability in a toy LTI SSM classifier, and applies a contraction-regularized
S4 safety head to jailbreak detection on JailbreakBench.
On the full JBB test split for a static thread model, SSM-head achieves JDR=1.0 across all
six attack families under a static threat model, and AUROC=0.994. It generalizes across
obfuscation styles, multi-turn injection positions, and held-out attack families. Under
adaptive threat models, SSM-head is vulnerable on direct-attack prompts: a black-box
greedy substitution attack achieves ASR=0.933, and a white-box gradient-guided attack
achieves ASR=1.0.
A logistic regression on mean-pooled Mamba-130M embeddings achieves AUROC=1.0 on
all three benchmarks, exceeding SSM-head on every discrimination metric. The embedding

space drives the transfer, and the SSM-head’s recurrent structure is not necessary for
discrimination. Its contribution is formal certifiability, the ability to verify a classification
decision at radius δ.
Primary future works include: (i) adversarial training on token-substitution examples to
close the direct-attack vulnerability, (ii) fine-tuning or retraining on copyright and harass-
ment/bullying behaviors to close the HarmBench AUROC gap, and (iii) extending box
propagation to nonlinear SSM architectures.

A.1
Related Work
State Space Models
Structured State Space Models (S4) Gu et al. (2021) and Mamba Gu &
Dao (2024) demonstrate competitive performance with Transformers on long sequences. The
diagonal-real S4 parameterization Gu et al. (2022) and ZOH discretization we use connect
directly to classical LTI stability theory, making formal analysis tractable.
LLM Safety and Jailbreaking
JailbreakBench Chao et al. (2024) provides a standardized
framework for evaluating jailbreak attacks. Response-level judges (LlamaGuard Inan et al.
(2023), GPT-4 graders) rely on model outputs; string/keyword heuristics Zou et al. (2023) are
cheaper but brittle and response-dependent. Our head classifies at the prompt embedding
level, avoiding response-level dependencies entirely.
Formal Robustness Certification
Interval bound propagation (IBP, Gowal et al. (2018))
and CROWN (Zhang et al. (2018)) certify feed-forward networks under lp perturbations.
For linear networks, IBP is exact; for nonlinear networks, it introduces relaxation error.
Recurrent network certification Jacoby et al. (2020) is less developed. For LTI systems, the
A+/A−box propagation is equivalent to exact IBP; the nonlinear activations that require
relaxation in general networks are absent. The contraction condition is the LTI analog of a
1-Lipschitz constraint on a feedforward network in that they both ensure that perturbation
magnitude is not amplified through the computation graph.
Representation-Based Safety
Representation Engineering Zou et al. (2023) trains
lightweight classifiers on LLM hidden states for alignment tasks, without formal robustness
analysis. The linear probe baseline in Section 4 is directly analogous to this line of work; its
competitive detection performance is consistent with prior evidence that harmful intent is
linearly encoded in large pretrained models. Our contribution is a certification framework
for the classifier itself, not a claim that the safety signal is absent from simpler baselines.
A.2
Contributions
1. Exact box propagation framework for LTI safety heads The A+/A−splitting and the resulting
per-step recursion implement exact interval arithmetic for LTI systems. These are exact in
the sense that the computed interval [sl
T, sh
T] is the tightest possible axis-aligned bounding
interval for the reachable output set under input perturbations. This contrasts with nonlinear
networks where IBP is only an over-approximation. The implementation (BoxReachSSM)
exposes both a final-step API (propagate) and a trajectory API (propagate trajectory),
enabling both scalar certification and reach tube visualization.
2. Hinge penalty training procedure for soft contraction enforcement The training-time hinge
penalty is a practically important contribution because it avoids constrained optimization
(projection onto the set A : ∥A∥∞≤1) while achieving the same effect during the natural
gradient descent trajectory. This is useful because: (a) it is trivially compatible with any
standard optimizers like Adam and SGD, without projection steps; (b) it allows ∥A∥∞to
transiently exceed 1 during optimization and recover, which is important for escaping local
minima near the boundary; and (c) the hyperparameter λ provides a continuous knob
between pure classification and pure contraction.

3. Prompt-only jailbreak detection without response signal Existing jailbreak detection bench-
marks largely evaluate models that can observe the LM’s response (StringJudge, GPT-4
graders). Using only the token embedding table of the base model to extract prompt fea-
tures makes the SSM-head response-agnostic. The advantage of the sequential SSM-head
formulation is that it opens the door to formal certification
4. Reachability as Safety head Diagnostic Tool The reach tube figures are a contribution to
interpretability for certified models. Rather than reporting a single scalar (certified fraction
at a given perturbation radius), the tube shows the full temporal geometry of the reachable
output set. These are diagnostics not available from scalar metrics, and they provide
intuition for why certain examples are certifiable and others are not. The reachability tool
can be applied to any LTI SSM safety head without modification.
A.3
Limitations
1. The box-propagation results in (3), (4) are exact only for the LTI model (N=8, d=32, T=64
for our examples). They do not apply to SSMs with nonlinear gating, selection mechanisms,
or attention. Extending interval propagation to those settings requires bounding nonlinear
activations, which introduces relaxation error as in CROWN Zhang et al. (2018).
2. Certification rates are bounded by logit margins, not by the bound’s looseness. The toy
classifier’s mean clean logit on correctly classified toxic examples is 0.14. By Proposition 1,
certification at radius δ requires the output interval half-width to be smaller than this margin.
At δ = 10−3, the half-width under the constrained model is 0.11-0.18 for most examples,
comparable to the margin itself. More data, a larger model, or a margin-maximizing loss
would increase margins and improve certification rates at the same δ, and the bound would
still be tight.
3. The δ-ball is defined in token embedding space, not character or token space. A formal
bridge would require composing the IBP interval with the tokenizer’s discrete-to-continuous
map, which is outside the scope of this work.
4. The certified set is a conservative subset of the true robust set. A non-certified example
may still be correctly classified under all perturbations in the ball, but the box propagation
cannot prove it. Tighter bounds, such as the LP relaxations in the style of CROWN, could
certify more examples at the same δ but would be more expensive to compute.

A Toy Example: Empirical Results
To begin with, we use a small LTI classifier to validate our method. The aim of such
a ToySSMClassifier is to test and visualize the formal results from proposed methods
in Section 3, not to build a competitive toxicity detector. The ToySSMClassifier uses a
word-level tokenizer (vocabulary 3,000), 32-dimensional embedding, 8-state LTI SSM (full
A ∈R8×8), max length T=64. The training data consists of 10,000 toxic conversation
examples drawn from the Civil Comments/Jigsaw Unintended Bias dataset from Borkan
et al. (2019). The certification set consists of 600 held-out examples. We utilize two models
for a running comparison: a constrained (λ = 0.10, which drives ∥A∥∞below 1) and an
unconstrained model (λ = 0, which leaves ∥A∥∞= 1.091).

Jailbreaking Example: Supplementary Results
The score KDE figure (Fig. 12) is a significant finding here. All six attack families—direct,
prefix injection, DAN, developer mode, roleplay, completion—produce score distributions
concentrated at logit values +2 to +5, with nearly identical shapes and means. The benign
distribution concentrates near -1.5. This similarity across radically different surface forms
(DAN uses ”Do Anything Now” framing; completion uses fill-in-the-blank templates;
roleplay uses character-based framing) indicates the head is responding to something in
the embedding space that is shared across all harmful formulations. In the Mamba-130m
embedding table, this shared signal likely corresponds to the semantic proximity of harmful-
intent tokens to one another, independent of the surrounding template syntax.
