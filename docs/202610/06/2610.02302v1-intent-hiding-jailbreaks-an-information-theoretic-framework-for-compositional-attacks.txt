Abstract
Recent work has shown that large language models (LLMs) can be vulnerable to jailbreak attacks in which
harmful intent is obscured through composition with benign tasks. In such attacks, a harmful request that is refused
in isolation may elicit a different response when embedded within a larger, seemingly benign query. We study
these compositional intent-hiding jailbreaks from an information-theoretic perspective by asking what a collection
of combined tasks reveals about harmful intent. Our formulation associates each task with an estimated probability
of being judged harmful: the average over the full task collection defines the prior probability of harmful intent,
while the average over a selected bundle containing the target defines the posterior. Selecting auxiliary tasks so
that these two averages agree, which we call prior–posterior matching, leaves the estimated intent unchanged even
though the harmful target remains in the bundle.
We study two settings that differ in whether query construction is part of the optimization. In the query-
independent setting, tasks are selected without regard to how they will ultimately be expressed in the final query.
We show that exact prior–posterior matching under a bundle-size constraint is computationally hard, derive an
optimal water-filling solution for fractional weights, and characterize the smallest bundle satisfying a prescribed
safety threshold. We then turn to the query-dependent setting, where task selection and query construction are
considered jointly, and both intent concealment and target preservation are evaluated on the resulting query. We
evaluate the proposed framework across different bundle sizes, query generators, and responding models, examining
both jailbreak effectiveness and preservation of the target behavior. These results show that compositional queries can
elicit target behaviors beyond the direct-request baseline under the evaluated search budgets, while also revealing
a trade-off: as bundle size increases, response-level target preservation tends to decrease for several responding
models.
I. INTRODUCTION
Large language models (LLMs) remain vulnerable to jailbreaks: prompts designed to bypass their
safeguards and elicit restricted responses [1], [2]. A model may refuse a harmful request stated directly
yet answer a reformulated version of the same request [3], [4]. Jailbreak attacks exploit this behavior by
modifying the wording or context of a request to bypass safeguards and elicit restricted responses [1], [2].
Such attacks expose limitations in both safety training and mechanisms that assess the safety of model
inputs and outputs [5], [6]. Understanding these limitations is especially important when LLMs are used
in applications involving sensitive information or consequential decisions [7]. The difficulty arises partly
from the role of context in instruction following. An LLM must interpret a request together with its
surrounding instructions, while an attacker can choose those instructions to disguise the request’s harmful
purpose [1]. The model is therefore asked to follow instructions that may conflict with its training to refuse
harmful requests [5]. Changing the surrounding context can alter how the model interprets the request
even when the behavior sought by the attacker remains the same. Rejecting a direct request therefore does

Motivation and Challenges. In this paper, we study compositional intent-hiding jailbreaks, in which a
harmful request is combined with benign-looking tasks and rewritten as a single coherent query. We call
the original request the target task, the added instructions auxiliary tasks, and the selected collection of
tasks a bundle. Several existing attacks use related compositional constructions. Prompt Packer embeds
harmful requests within compositional instructions [8]. ReNeLLM combines prompt rewriting with nested
scenarios [1]. DeepInception places requests within layered fictional scenarios [9]. SequentialBreak embeds
a harmful component within a sequence of otherwise benign prompts [10]. These methods show how
surrounding instructions can help disguise a harmful request. However, choosing those instructions involves
decisions about both their content and their number. For a given target, the choice of auxiliary tasks
determines both the surrounding context and the additional instructions the model must follow. Experiments
on instruction-dense prompts show that adherence can deteriorate as more instructions are added [11].
Understanding these choices requires relating the tasks included in a query to what their combination
reveals about harmful intent.
Existing information-theoretic work has examined the relationship between attack effectiveness and
stealthiness in vision-language models [12]. Game-theoretic work has also studied how attackers conceal
intent through combinations of skills against a defender with limited filtering capacity [13]. We focus on a
complementary task-selection problem. Given harmful-intent probabilities for a finite collection of tasks,
which auxiliary tasks form the smallest bundle that retains a designated target and satisfies a specified
concealment condition? Stating this condition explicitly allows us to determine whether such a bundle
exists and how many auxiliary tasks it requires. This provides a way to study the feasibility and cost of
concealment before considering the wording of a particular query.
The wording introduces a second challenge. A selected bundle must be expressed as a natural-language
query, and the same tasks can be combined, ordered, and described in different ways. These choices may
change both the apparent intent of the query and the clarity of the target request. Irrelevant context can
also reduce performance on reasoning tasks [14], motivating a similar question here: can auxiliary tasks
divert a response from its designated target? A query that appears less harmful is insufficient if rewriting
removes or weakens the behavior the attacker wants to obtain. We therefore study task selection and
query construction separately, then evaluate both attack success and the extent to which responses fulfill
the original target.
Summary of Contributions. We treat compositional intent hiding as a task-selection problem and study
the tradeoff between concealment and target preservation. In this work, we develop this view in stages: first
as an information-theoretic abstraction, then by selecting tasks without regard to their wording, followed
by selecting the tasks and the query together, and finally by evaluating the resulting attacks empirically.
A. Information-theoretic abstraction.
We study how the tasks included in a bundle affect the estimated probability of harmful intent (Sec-
tion II). Each task in the full collection has an estimated probability of being judged harmful. Before the
bundle is known, we assume that every task in the collection is equally likely to be the target. The average
of these task-level probabilities gives the probability of harmful intent before seeing the bundle, which we
call the prior. Once the bundle is known, we treat its included tasks as equally likely candidates for the
target. Under this assumption, the average over the included tasks gives the probability of harmful intent
after seeing the bundle, which we call the posterior. We select auxiliary tasks so that the average within
the bundle matches the average over the full collection. We call this prior-posterior matching. Under the
stated assumptions, seeing such a bundle leaves the estimated probability of harmful intent unchanged.
The target task remains part of the bundle.
Our aim is to disguise its harmful purpose while keeping the request clear enough to be carried out.
This connects our formulation to the privacy funnel and latent-variable private information retrieval, where

useful information must be preserved while sensitive information is concealed [15]–[18]. We use prior-
posterior matching to guide task selection, then examine how expressing the selected tasks as a query
affects both attack success and target preservation.
B. Query-independent intent hiding.
We first seek the smallest bundle that contains a given target and satisfies prior-posterior matching,
without considering how the selected tasks will be written together (Section III). Selecting the full task
collection always gives an exact match. The challenge is to obtain a match with fewer tasks. We prove
that deciding whether an exact match exists within a specified bundle-size limit is NP-hard in general.
We therefore consider a relaxation that allows auxiliary tasks to have weights between zero and one. We
first derive an optimal water-filling allocation for this weighted problem. Its minimum total weight gives
a lower bound on the number of tasks required when each task must be either included or excluded.
We also study a filter that accepts a bundle whenever its probability of harmful intent is at or below a
specified threshold. This condition allows an efficient selection rule. We add auxiliary tasks in order of
increasing harmful-intent probability and stop when the bundle average meets the threshold. Whenever
an acceptable bundle exists, this rule finds one with the fewest tasks. Thus, the computational difficulty
depends on the requirement: exact matching is hard in general, while minimum-size selection under a
threshold can be solved efficiently under our assumptions.
C. Query-dependent intent hiding.
We next consider how the selected tasks are written as a single query (Section IV). The same bundle
can be expressed in different ways. Simply listing the tasks may leave the harmful purpose obvious.
Rewriting them together may disguise that purpose, but may also weaken or change the original target.
The choice of tasks alone therefore does not determine whether the resulting query will be suitable for the
attack. We jointly select the tasks and the query that expresses them. In this formulation, the generated
query must satisfy two requirements: its query-level probability of harmful intent must be at or below a
specified threshold, and it must preserve the designated target task. We describe a search procedure that
examines a limited number of candidate bundles and generated queries. Among the candidates that satisfy
both requirements, the procedure selects the one that best preserves the target. We evaluate actual attack
success separately by checking whether the responding LLM carries out the original target.
D. Empirical evaluation.
We compare an LLM’s response to a harmful request asked alone with its responses when benign
tasks are added (Section V). We test whether adding these tasks helps bypass the LLM’s safeguards
and how fully the resulting responses carry out the original harmful request. This evaluation is required
because prior-posterior matching concerns the selected tasks, whereas the actual response also depends
on their wording and the responding LLM. We use 100 harmful targets from JailbreakBench and 50
benign auxiliary tasks from Super-NaturalInstructions [19], [20]. We first examine whether the available
auxiliaries can achieve fractional matching and how much auxiliary weight is required. We then study
how attack success and target preservation vary with bundle size, the query generator, and the family and
size of the responding model.
We use Qwen3-32B to generate composed queries and compare it with Qwen3-14B using the same
sampled bundles [21]. The responding models include Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3-
4B-Instruct-2507, allowing comparisons across model sizes and checkpoints [21], [22]. We also evaluate
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 and Gemma-3-4B in its pretrained and instruction-tuned versions to examine
differences across model families [23], [24]. We report cumulative target-level attack success rate (ASR)
and mean target preservation level. These measures distinguish whether the candidate search finds a
successful query for each target from how fully responses fulfill the target on average.

II. PROBLEM FORMULATION
Figure 1 shows the steps from selecting tasks to obtaining an LLM response. Consider an adversary
whose target task tb is to obtain instructions for committing fraud. The adversary adds benign auxiliary
tasks, such as summarizing a passage, denoted by ti, and formatting information as a table, denoted by tj.
The selected tasks form a bundle X. The adversary combines these tasks into a single natural-language
query q and submits it to the responding LLM. We denote the LLM’s response by y. The adversary seeks
to conceal the harmful purpose of tb, which we refer to as intent privacy, while still obtaining a response
y that carries out tb.
Before submitting the query, the adversary can assess the proposed attack at two stages. At the bundle
stage, the tasks have been selected but the query has not yet been written. The assessment uses the
harmful-intent probabilities of the included tasks to check whether the bundle satisfies prior–posterior
matching. This is the query-independent setting: we study which auxiliary tasks to include and how many
are needed, without considering the wording of the query.
At the query stage, we examine whether q still clearly expresses the target task tb. Different queries
expressing the same selected tasks may therefore differ in target preservation. For example, a generated
query may ask for a general discussion of fraud instead of the instructions specified by tb. In that case,
it no longer preserves the target task. This motivates the query-dependent setting, in which we consider
both the selected tasks and how they are written together. These assessments guide the construction of
an attack before submission, but neither guarantees that the responding LLM will carry out the target.
Actual attack success must be evaluated from the response y.

B. Intent-Hiding Jailbreaks via Task Bundling
In an intent-hiding jailbreak, a prompt may frame the interaction as a fictional investigation or educa-
tional analysis, ask for summarization and formatting, and include within that structure a target request
whose negative intent would be more apparent if asked directly. The target request is therefore not removed,
but embedded as one component of a larger composed prompt whose other components appear benign.
This abstraction is consistent with compositional jailbreak attacks that conceal harmful requests through
auxiliary instructions, rewriting, nested scenarios, or sequential benign context [1], [8]–[10]. Although
these attacks differ operationally, they share the same underlying principle: intent can be concealed by a
composed bundle.

The rest of the paper follows two paths. First, in the query-independent intent hiding problem, the goal is
to select a deterministic bundle x containing tb using only bundle-level quantities. This leads to the perfect
hiding condition r(x) = ρ, the size-bounded exact bundle-selection problem, its fractional relaxation, and

Remark 2 (Relationship to the privacy funnel). Our formulation is related to the privacy funnel [15], [16],
[28], where a representation is designed to hide a sensitive variable while preserving information about
a useful variable. Here, however, the representation is not an arbitrary stochastic channel, but a discrete
task bundle that must contain a designated target task. In addition, the goal is not to hide the target task
itself, but to make the bundle-level intent posterior match the negative-intent prior. The resulting tradeoff
is therefore between intent concealment and target salience.
Remark 3 (Relation to latent-variable Private Information Retrieval (PIR)). Our formulation is also related
to latent-variable PIR [17], [18], [29], where a user retrieves a desired message while hiding a sensitive
latent variable correlated with the desired index. The analogy is that the target task plays the role of
the desired item, while the intent label plays the role of the latent sensitive variable. The key difference
is that, in our setting, the user does not seek to hide the target task completely. The target task must
remain sufficiently salient to be realized in the final prompt. What is hidden is instead the negative intent
associated with the composed bundle. Thus, intent privacy concerns the probability of harmful intent,
while target preservation concerns whether the designated task remains expressed in the query.
Remark 4 (Relation to game-theoretic compositional attacks). Recent work [13] has studied compositional
intent hiding through an attacker-defender game, where malicious intents are paired with adversarial
skills and the defender has limited filtering capacity. Our focus is complementary: rather than analyzing
equilibrium behavior, we isolate the information-theoretic bundle-selection mechanism by which auxiliary
tasks make the bundle-level intent posterior match the prior or satisfy a safety threshold.

IV. QUERY-DEPENDENT INTENT HIDING
The query-independent analysis in Section III evaluates a bundle x using only bundle-level quantities,
namely r(x), ρ, and ∥x∥1. However, a bundle is not itself the final object submitted to the downstream
LLM; it must first be realized as a natural-language query. This realization step can change both the
negative-intent posterior of the request and the extent to which the designated target task remains identi-
fiable.
In particular, a favorable bundle-level intent posterior does not guarantee a favorable query-level re-
alization. Consider a bundle that contains the target task tb together with auxiliary tasks involving
summarization, formatting, and fictional scenario writing. One query generator may simply concatenate
these tasks into a list of instructions, in which case the target task can remain highly visible because the
auxiliary tasks do not meaningfully affect the interpretation of the overall query. Another generator may
instead connect the same tasks into a coherent scenario; this can reduce the prominence of the target task,
but it may also make the overall purpose of the query easier for a safety evaluator to infer. The opposite
issue can also occur. A bundle may contain enough benign-looking auxiliary tasks that the resulting query
appears acceptable at the query level, yet the target task may become too indirect, too weakly specified,
or reinterpreted by the query generator. For example, an auxiliary task requesting a general discussion
or high-level explanation may cause a concrete target task to be realized as a broad educational request,
leaving the target task no longer sufficiently identifiable. Therefore, the effect of a bundle cannot be
characterized only by its bundle-level intent posterior or its size. Two bundles with similar values of r(x)
and ∥x∥1 may lead to substantially different queries, because the included tasks can be combined, ordered,
and expressed in different ways. This motivates a query-dependent formulation in which the bundle and
its natural-language realization are evaluated jointly.

C. Query-Dependent Optimization
The quantities rQ(q(x)) and ℓb(q(x)), and hence the utility UQ(q(x); tb), need not be known exactly in
practice. They can be estimated using query-level evaluators, such as calibrated safety classifiers, LLM-
based judges with fixed rubrics, embedding-based target recognizers, or ensembles of such models. We
denote the corresponding estimates by brQ(q(x)) and bℓb(q(x)). The formal problem is thus stated in terms
of the query-level quantities, while the bounded search procedure below uses empirical estimates of them.
The query-dependent matching problem cannot usually be solved exactly in practice, because the set
Q(x) of natural-language realizations is too large to enumerate exhaustively. We therefore approximate it
by bounded query search over a candidate set bCX ⊆CX(tb) supplied by the query-independent construc-
tions of Section III, namely the greedy prefixes and the support of the water-filling solution, rather than
over all of CX(tb). For each candidate bundle x ∈bCX, we generate at most M query realizations and
evaluate each using its estimated query-level negative-intent posterior brQ(q(x)) and target log-posterior
lift bℓb(q(x)). The empirical analogue of maximizing UQ is therefore to retain only realizations satisfying
brQ(q(x)) ≤τQ,
bℓb(q(x)) ≥η,

Algorithm 1 separates bundle-level concealment from query-level realization: the candidate set bCX
is produced first, and the algorithm then tests whether these bundles admit realizations satisfying both
brQ(q(x)) ≤τQ and bℓb(q(x)) ≥η. In this way, the greedy and fractional constructions from query-
independent bundle selection act as candidate generators, while the final decision is made at the query
level.
The search costs M| bCX| query evaluations and is therefore computationally controlled, but it is not com-
plete: failure to find a feasible query does not imply that none exists, only that no quality-preserving realiza-
tion was found within the query budget. Conversely, whenever the algorithm returns a pair (x⋆, q⋆(x⋆)), the
returned query satisfies the estimated query-level safety and target preservation constraints by construction.
Because η > 0, this two-stage procedure is equivalent to maximizing the empirical counterpart of UQ:
infeasible realizations have zero utility, while among feasible realizations the utility equals bℓb(q(x)).

V. EXPERIMENTS AND DISCUSSION
We examine how adding benign tasks changes an LLM’s response to a harmful request. The analysis
in Section III guides the choice of auxiliary tasks, but the outcome also depends on how those tasks are
written together and on the responding model as discussed in Section IV. We first calculate the auxiliary
mass needed for fractional matching. We then sample bundles that favor tasks with lower estimated
negative-intent probabilities and generate a query from each bundle. We measure whether at least one
query succeeds for each target and how fully the responses carry out the original target. We compare the
results across bundle sizes, query generators, and responding models.
A. Experimental Setup

VI. CONCLUSIONS
We studied intent-hiding jailbreaks through an information-theoretic formulation in which a harmful
target task is combined with benign auxiliary tasks. Our model expresses perfect bundle-level concealment
through prior–posterior matching. We proved that exact matching under a bundle-size constraint is NP-
hard, derived a water-filling solution for fractional weights, and characterized the smallest bundle satisfying
a one-sided safety threshold. We extended the formulation to natural-language queries, jointly considering
intent concealment and target preservation. We also carried out a broad empirical evaluation across bundle
sizes, query generators, and responding models from the Gemma, Qwen, and Mistral families, measuring
both whether a target could be reached and how fully the resulting response carried out that target.
Several key insights emerge from this work. First, composition substantially changed model behavior:
targets that were refused as direct requests could often be reached once even a small number of benign
auxiliary tasks were added. Second, these effects transferred across model families, and the large differ-
ences observed on direct requests became much smaller after composition. Third, increasing model size
did not lead to a consistent increase in robustness over the range we evaluated, while differences in post-
training appeared to matter more. Finally, query realization itself mattered: when the same bundles were
written by different query generators, the resulting attack success could differ substantially for the more
robust responding model. At the same time, increasing the number of auxiliary tasks reduced average
target preservation for several models, showing that stronger composition can help bypass safeguards
while also making the original target harder to carry through faithfully.

range of harmful behaviors, so this prior reflects the deliberately target-heavy composition instead of
real user requests. Under uniform weighting, target tasks account for two-thirds of the probability mass,
giving ρunif ≈0.651405. We therefore use the reference-mixture model in (41), which specifies the target
proportion independently of the evaluation alphabet. We fix π0 = 0.1 as a nominal operating point rather
than an estimate of real-world prevalence, giving the prescribed matching level ρ0 = ρ(π0) ≈0.103113.
All 50 auxiliary probabilities lie below ρ0, yielding C−≈4.837864 and C+ = 0. The largest positive
target offset is approximately 0.896828, making all 99 targets above ρ0 feasible under the fractional
relaxation. The remaining target lies below ρ0 and satisfies the one-sided condition, but exact matching
is impossible because no auxiliary probability exceeds ρ0. For the 99 feasible targets out of the 100, the
median minimum auxiliary mass is 8.713, giving a median relaxed total bundle size of 9.713.
