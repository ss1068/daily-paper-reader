ABSTRACT 

Embodied agents powered by large language models and vision-language models are increasingly deployed in physical environments, but jailbreak attacks can in-duce these agents to perform physically harmful actions. A growing number of guardrail methods have been proposed to intercept dangerous behavior before it is executed, yet existing safety benchmarks evaluate the embodied models them-selves, leaving it unclear how well these guardrails actually defend an embodied agent in practice. We present the first systematic evaluation of jailbreak guardrails for embodied agents. To compare guardrails under identical conditions, we build a pluggable evaluation framework that treats the embodied agent as a fixed back-end and each guardrail as a module that can intervene at the perception, planning, or control stage. We subject six representative guardrails to template-based and automated jailbreak attacks as well as safe instructions, and assess them at the system level along three dimensions: defense effectiveness, measured by the by-pass rate and the hazard success rate in the simulator; usability, measured by the false-positive rate and the task completion rate on safe instructions; and efficiency, measured by the latency overhead added at runtime. Experiments on guardrails that span different intervention stages, decision mechanisms, and input modali-ties reveal a clear trade-off among the three dimensions, and show that no single guardrail dominates in all settings. We further analyze how intervention stage, de-cision mechanism, and input modality shape safety outcomes, and we offer prac-tical guidance for selecting and designing guardrails for embodied agents. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Embodied agents (Singh et al., 2023; Song et al., 2023; Driess et al., 2023) powered by large lan-guage models (LLMs) or vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly deployed in homes, workplaces, and other physical environments. These agents translate natural language instructions into concrete physical actions, which makes them highly useful but also exposes them to a serious risk: a malicious or adversarial instruction can drive an agent to perform harmful behavior, such as starting a fire, damaging property, or injuring a person. Recent work shows that this risk is 1arXiv:2610.06122v1 [cs.AI] 5 Oct 2026 Under review real. Template-based attacks such as BadRobot (Zhang et al., 2024a) and automated attacks such as RoboPAIR (Robey et al., 2025) and POEX (Lu et al., 2024) can reliably bypass the safety mecha-nisms of current embodied agents and cause dangerous actions in simulation. To defend against this threat, a growing line of work introduces external guardrails, detection mod-ules deployed around the agent that inspect its inputs and intermediate outputs and block or correct anything judged unsafe. Existing guardrails span a wide range of designs, from fine-tuned safety classifiers such as Llama Guard 3 Vision (Chi et al., 2024) and EMBGuard (Choi et al., 2026), to rule-based runtime enforcers such as AgentSpec (Wang et al., 2026a) and RoboGuard (Ravichandran et al., 2026), to multi-agent debate systems such as MADRA (Wang et al., 2026b). Each method reports strong safety results in its own setting, but the field still lacks a common ground for com-paring them. Current benchmarks (Yin et al., 2024; Yeke et al., 2026) for embodied safety, such as SafeAgentBench (Yin et al., 2024), evaluate the embodied model itself rather than the guardrail attached to it. As a result, there is no systematic way to answer the questions that matter in practice: how well does each guardrail resist real jailbreak attacks, how often does it wrongly block legitimate instructions, and how much runtime overhead does it introduce. We close this gap with a systematic evaluation of jailbreak guardrails for embodied agents. The evaluation is enabled by a pluggable framework that inserts each guardrail as a module into a fixed agent pipeline, at the perception, planning, or control stage; because every guardrail runs on the same backend, performance differences can be attributed to the guardrail alone. We organize exist-ing guardrails along four axes, namely intervention phase, decision mechanism, input modality, and replanning. We then evaluate six representative guardrails that span this taxonomy, together with a no-guardrail baseline, on unsafe instructions under template-based and automated jailbreak attacks, and on a set of safe instructions. We measure three dimensions of guardrail quality at the system level: defense effectiveness, captured by the bypass rate and the hazard success rate; usability, cap-tured by the false-positive rate and the task completion rate on safe instructions; efficiency, captured by the latency ratio relative to the unguarded agent. Our evaluation yields several findings. First, there is a clear trade-off among defense, efficiency, and usability, and no single guardrail dominates on all three. Second, input modality has a counterin-tuitive effect: augmenting a text-only guardrail with the egocentric image consistently weakens its defense, which suggests that naive multimodal fusion can dilute the safety signal. Third, interven-tion timing shapes how quickly an interception takes effect: planning-stage checks must wait for a complete plan, and control-stage guardrails intervene only after part of the action sequence has already been executed, whereas perception-stage checks act almost immediately. Finally, the deci-sion mechanism determines the guardrail’s own computational cost: purely rule-based designs are essentially free, fine-tuned classifiers add modest overhead, and designs that delegate their checks to LLMs, whether rule-based or agent-based, are the most expensive. Together, these results offer practical guidance for selecting and designing guardrails for embodied agents. Our contributions are summarized as follows. (1) We propose the first taxonomy of jailbreak guardrails for embodied agents, organizing representative designs along four dimensions, namely intervention phase, decision mechanism, input modality, and replanning capability. (2) We develop a system-level evaluation framework for guarded embodied agents. The framework treats the em-bodied agent as a fixed backend and inserts each guardrail as a pluggable module, and it assesses guardrail quality along three deployment-critical dimensions, namely defense effectiveness, usabil-ity, and efficiency, thus moving beyond the detection-centric evaluation of prior benchmarks toward what matters when a guardrail is actually deployed. (3) Building on this framework, we conduct a large-scale evaluation of six representative guardrails that span the taxonomy, subjecting them to five jailbreak attacks of two categories and to safe instructions across two embodied agent models. The evaluation uncovers a consistent three-way trade-off among defense, usability, and efficiency, together with a series of actionable findings on how input modality, intervention timing, and deci-sion mechanism shape safety outcomes, which offer concrete guidance for selecting and designing guardrails in practice. 2Under review 

2 PRELIMINARIES 

2.1 EMBODIED AGENTS AND ENVIRONMENTS 

We consider an embodied agent that interacts with a physical environment through a sequence of observations and actions. At each time step t, the agent receives an observation ot from the envi-ronment, which may include visual inputs, textual instructions, and other sensory data. The agent then selects an action at based on its policy π, which is typically parameterized by a large language model (LLM) or a vision-language model (VLM). The environment transitions to a new state st+1 

based on the action taken, and the agent receives a new observation ot+1 . The goal of the agent is to complete a given task or set of tasks while adhering to safety constraints and avoiding unsafe behaviors. 2.2 JAILBREAK ATTACKS 

Objective of Jailbreak Attacks. The objective of a jailbreak attack is to induce the agent to realize a harmful outcome in the physical environment. Formally, the attacker is given an unsafe instruction 

Iu, or equivalently an adversarial goal g that specifies a harmful consequence (e.g., “starting a fire”), together with the current environment, including the initial observation o0 that may contain visual inputs. The attacker aims to craft a malicious instruction Im such that, when the agent follows Im,the executed action sequence τ = ( a1, . . . , a T ) drives the environment into a final state sT whose consequence satisfies the goal g. The attack is considered successful if the outcome of the agent’s execution fulfills g, regardless of whether the agent itself recognizes Im as harmful. 

Capability of Jailbreak Attack. We assume that the attacker has access to the agent’s input inter-face and can craft malicious instructions or prompts. The attacker may also have knowledge of the agent’s policy π and the environment dynamics, allowing them to predict the agent’s behavior in response to different instructions. However, the attacker does not have access to the internal state of the agent, such as its model parameters or hidden activations, and cannot modify the agent’s policy 

π, the environment dynamics, or the environment state directly. The attacker can only influence the agent’s behavior through carefully designed instructions or prompts that exploit vulnerabilities in the agent’s decision-making process. 

3 JAILBREAK GUARDRAILS 

3.1 JAILBREAK DEFENSE 

Jailbreak defense aims to prevent an embodied agent from being manipulated by a malicious in-struction Im into executing actions whose consequences fulfill the adversarial goal g (Section 2). Existing defenses can be broadly divided into two categories: internal model safety enhancement and external guardrails. 

Internal Model Safety Enhancement. This line of work improves the safety awareness of the embodied model itself. A representative approach is safety alignment, where the model is fine-tuned with human or automated preference feedback, such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) (Ouyang et al., 2022) and direct preference optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023), so that it learns to recognize and refuse harmful instructions. Another line is training-free: methods such as ThinkSafe (Yin et al., 2024) and the prompt-based mitigation in POEX (Lu et al., 2024) augment the agent’s prompt with safety reminders or explicit risk-checking steps, increas-ing the model’s sensitivity to harmful inputs without updating its parameters. While effective to some extent, internal approaches either require costly fine-tuning of the embodied model or remain vulnerable to carefully optimized adversarial instructions. 

External Guardrails. In contrast, external guardrails (Choi et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a; Kim et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025b; 2026d) are detection-based defense mechanisms deployed around the embodied agent. A guardrail inspects the information flowing through the agent’s pipeline, such as the perceived observations, the generated plans, or the actions to be executed, and intercepts those identified as harmful. We categorize existing jailbreak guardrails along four dimensions: intervention phase, decision mechanism, input modality, and replanning capability. Table 1 summarizes representative guardrails under this taxonomy. 

Intervention Phase. Guardrails can intervene at three stages of the embodied agent’s pipeline. 

Perception-stage guardrails inspect the input instruction and the perceived observation (e.g., the egocentric view) before planning begins, blocking unsafe requests as early as possible (Chi et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026b; Lu et al., 2026; Choi et al., 2026). Planning-stage 

guardrails examine the plan generated by the agent before any action is taken, reasoning about the potential consequences of the whole plan (Fang et al., 2026; Chi et al., 2024; Ravichandran et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025a; Liu et al., 2025; Verma et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Choi et al., 2026; Team, 2026; Na, 2026). Control-stage guardrails monitor each low-level action at runtime and intervene immediately before execution (Wang et al., 2026a;c), which provides the richest grounded context but must operate at the agent’s control frequency. Intervening earlier allows unsafe behaviors to be stopped sooner, while intervening later benefits from more concrete execution context. 

Decision Mechanism. According to how the safety decision is made, guardrails fall into three categories. Rule-based guardrails encode safety constraints as explicit rules, domain-specific lan-guages, or formal specifications (e.g., linear temporal logic), and verify the agent’s behavior against them (Ravichandran et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a;c); they offer precise and interpretable en-forcement but require heuristic rule design. Model-based guardrails delegate the safety judgment to a learned model, ranging from fine-tuned classifiers to general-purpose (vision-)language models prompted or trained as safety critics (Fang et al., 2026; Chi et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a; Liu et al., 2025; Verma et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Hua et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026; Choi et al., 2026; Team, 2026; Na, 2026); they generalize to diverse and implicit risks at the cost of additional in-ference overhead. Agent-based guardrails employ one or more LLM agents that deliberate, debate, or cross-examine candidate behaviors before reaching a verdict (Wang et al., 2026b), achieving stronger reasoning at the highest computational cost. 

Input Modality. Guardrails differ in the modalities they consume. Text-only guardrails reason over the instruction, the plan, or the action sequence (Ravichandran et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a; 2025a; 2026b;c), which is efficient but may miss risks that are only evident from the scene. Vision-enabled guardrails additionally take visual observations as input (Fang et al., 2026; Chi et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2026; Choi et al., 2026), allowing them to ground risk assessment in the actual environment, e.g., identifying a candle near flammable objects from the egocentric image. 

Replanning. When a guardrail intercepts a risky plan or action, it can either terminate the episode directly or trigger replanning , where the detected violation is fed back to the planner so that the agent regenerates a safe alternative that still serves the original task (Ravichandran et al., 2026; 4Under review Wang et al., 2026c). Replanning preserves task usefulness that would otherwise be lost to a hard block, but introduces additional latency and may still converge to an unsafe plan after repeated attempts. 3.3 GUARDRAIL EVALUATION FRAMEWORK 

Our evaluation framework treats guardrails as pluggable modules on a fixed embodied agent back-end, so that all guardrails are compared under identical conditions. Each guardrail is tested against unsafe instructions produced by attacks of different categories, as well as a set of safe instructions, and is assessed along three dimensions: defense effectiveness, efficiency, and usability. 

Defense Effectiveness. Defense effectiveness measures how well a guardrail prevents harm under attack, and is reported with two complementary metrics. The ❶ bypass rate is the fraction of unsafe episodes in which the guardrail never intervenes and the malicious instruction passes through to the embodied agent; it reflects how often the guardrail fails to detect the threat. The ❷ hazard success rate is the fraction of unsafe episodes in which the final environment state sT satisfies the adversarial goal g, i.e., the harmful consequence is physically realized. Since a passed instruction does not always lead to successful execution, the hazard success rate is typically lower than the bypass rate; together they capture both the guardrail’s detection ability and the residual physical risk. For both metrics, lower is better. 

Efficiency. Efficiency measures the runtime overhead introduced by the guardrail. We report the ❶

latency ratio , defined as the mean per-episode latency of the guarded agent divided by that of the same agent without any guardrail. A ratio of 1.0 means zero overhead, and larger values indicate slower execution. For control-stage guardrails that intervene before every action, this overhead may directly translate into a lower control frequency. 

Usability. Usability measures how a guardrail affects the agent’s normal operation, and is evaluated on safe instructions. The ❶ false positive rate is the fraction of safe instructions that are wrongly intercepted by the guardrail. The ❷ task completion rate is the fraction of safe tasks that the guarded agent successfully completes. An ideal guardrail should keep both the false positive rate low and the task completion rate close to that of the unguarded agent. 

4 EXPERIMENT 

4.1 EVALUATION SETUP 

Datasets and Embodied Environment. We build our evaluation on SafeAgentBench (Yin et al., 2024), using its two detailed instruction datasets: safe detailed with 300 safe household tasks for usability evaluation, and unsafe detailed with 300 unsafe instructions spanning ten risk categories (e.g., fire hazard, electrical shock, and poisoning) for defense evaluation. All tasks are executed in AI2-THOR (Kolve et al., 2017), an interactive 3D household simulator, across more than 80 indoor floor plans, and the agent acts through the SafeAgentBench low-level action space of 17 executable skills. We adopt closed-source GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024) (VLM) and open-source DeepSeek-V3.2 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025) (LLM) as the task planning models of the embodied agent, which are prompted to directly generate low-level action sequences. It is noted that the main results of DeepSeek-V3.2 are reported in Appendix A, and the trends are largely consistent with GPT-4o.

Metrics. We report the metrics defined in Section 3: bypass rate, hazard success rate, false positive rate, task completion rate, and latency ratio. The hazard success rate and the task completion rate are determined in two ways, following SafeAgentBench. For tasks annotated with goal states, we compare the final object states in the simulator against the annotated states using the rule-based eval-uator of SafeAgentBench. For the remaining tasks, a GPT-4o-based judge compares the executed action trajectory against the reference plan and decides whether the task goal (for safe tasks) or the harmful outcome (for unsafe tasks) is achieved. 

Baselines. We evaluate six representative guardrails covering the taxonomy in Section 3, together with a no-guardrail baseline. Llama Guard 3 Vision (Llama Guard for brevity) (Chi et al., 2024) and EMBGuard (Choi et al., 2026) are model-based guardrails, each tested at both the perception and planning stages with text-only (T) and text+vision (T+V) inputs. Input Modality and Defense Effectiveness. A counterintuitive pattern in Table 2 concerns the input modality: for both Llama Guard and EMBGuard, at both the perception and planning stages, augmenting the guardrail with the egocentric image (T+V) consistently degrades defense compared with text-only checks, the average bypass rate of Llama Guard 3 rises from 40.00% to 54.46% at the perception stage and from 44.42% to 66.11% at the planning stage, and that of EMBGuard from 33.07% to 64.07% and from 38.04% to 66.23%, with the hazard success rate increasing in lockstep (e.g., 21.18% → 34.11% for Llama Guard at the planning stage). One possible explanation is that the visual input confuses the guardrail’s judgment: the egocentric frame is dominated by benign household content, so its inclusion dilutes the attack signal carried by the instruction and biases the model toward a safe verdict. More broadly, although adding a modality is intended to provide more evidence for the safety decision, it also enlarges the attack surface: unlike discrete text, images live in a continuous space, which is known to admit imperceptible adversarial perturbations and visual prompt injections, and our results suggest that embodied guardrails inherit this fragility even when the adversary does not explicitly optimize the image. Practically, this means visual context should be fused with care, for instance, weighing the textual channel more heavily, or cross-checking the two modalities rather than naively concatenating them. 

The Defense–Usability Trade-off. Comparing Table 2 with Figure 1 reveals a consistent tension between defense effectiveness and usability. The text+vision guardrail variants, despite their weaker defense, are gentler on benign instructions: adding the image lowers the false positive rate of every model-based variant (e.g., 21.67% → 11.71% for Llama Guard and 31.10% → 19.40% for EMB-Guard at the planning stage) and raises the task completion rate accordingly (53.00% → 57.86% and 45.82% → 50.50%, respectively), the same dilution of the safety signal that lets attacks through also spares safe instructions. RoboSafe marks the opposite extreme: the strongest defense in Table 2 (7.24% average bypass rate and 5.75% hazard success rate) comes with a practically unusable agent, as 91.00% of the safe instructions are wrongly intercepted and the completion rate drops to 11.33%. Intervention Timing. Figure 3a reports the latency ratio of each guardrail on the episodes it in-tercepts, relative to the no-guardrail agent on the same tasks, reflecting how long an episode has run before the interception takes effect. Two observations emerge. First, for the same guardrail un-der the same input modality, interception at the planning stage consistently incurs a higher latency ratio than at the perception stage: the ratio of Llama Guard rises from 0.02–0.06 (Perception, T) to 0.30–0.88 (Planning, T), and that of EMBGuard from 0.06–0.24 (Perception, T) to 0.36–1.55 (Planning, T), with the same trend holding for the text+vision variants. This is because a planning-stage check can only be performed after the planner has produced a complete plan, so the planning latency is already sunk when the interception happens. Second, the control-stage guardrails also remain at a high latency ratio (0.51–1.15 for AgentSpec and 0.86–2.61 for RoboSafe), even though the rule-based matching of AgentSpec is practically free (0.00s per episode in our logs): by the time a risky action is intercepted, the agent has already finished planning and executed part of the action sequence. RoboGuard exhibits the highest ratios (up to 3.81), as each rejected plan triggers an additional replanning round before the episode is finally stopped. Overall, these results suggest that a later interception generally has to wait longer before it can take effect, so guardrails deployed at earlier pipeline stages are preferable when response time is a concern. 

Decision Mechanism and Guardrail Latency. Figure 3b reports the mean guardrail-only latency per episode on safe tasks, with the planner and simulator time excluded, which isolates the pro-cessing cost of the guardrail itself. A clear ordering by decision mechanism emerges. The purely rule-based AgentSpec is by far the cheapest, with a practically zero latency (0.00s per episode), as its runtime enforcement reduces to string and state matching. RoboGuard and RoboSafe also fall into the rule-based category, but both delegate key steps to an LLM. RoboGuard contextualizes safety rules into temporal-logic specifications for each scene (7.59s), and RoboSafe reasons over every low-level action before execution (22.81s), which makes RoboSafe the most expensive guardrail in our evaluation. The agent-based MADRA (9.72s) is also costly, as its multi-agent debate requires several model calls per instruction. The customized LLM-based guardrails, Llama Guard and EM-BGuard, lie between these two extremes: a single fine-tuned model call per check keeps all of their variants below 1s per episode (0.12–0.60s), so they enjoy a clear speed advantage over the LLM-orchestrated rule-based and agent-based designs, while still being orders of magnitude slower than pure rules. 

5 RELATED WORK 

LLM-powered Embodied Agents. Large language models (LLMs) have been widely adopted as the reasoning core of embodied agents, allowing robots to interpret natural language instructions and plan complex tasks. Early works employ LLMs as high-level planners, either grounding instructions in environmental affordances (Ahn et al., 2022) or expressing plans as programs that invoke robot APIs (Liang et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2023). Subsequent works close the loop between planning and execution through environmental feedback (Huang et al., 2022; Yao et al., 9Under review 2023; Song et al., 2023), and more recent studies equip agents with memory systems (Wang et al., 2025c) and multi-agent collaboration (Zhang et al., 2024b). Beyond text, embodied agents built on multimodal large language models (MLLMs) further ground reasoning in visual observations, from injecting sensor inputs into language models (Driess et al., 2023) to vision-language-action models that directly output robot actions (Zitkovich et al., 2023; Octo Model Team et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024), and to recent efforts that scale such models toward generalist embodied agents (Li et al., 2024; Gemini Robotics Team, 2025; Bjorck et al., 2025). 

Jailbreak Attacks for Embodied Agents. Recent studies have shown that embodied agents pow-ered by LLMs can be induced to perform physically harmful actions. Early efforts probe this risk with static datasets of hazardous instructions (Yin et al., 2024) and with handcrafted jailbreak tem-plates that disguise malicious intent through contextual framing or conceptual deception (Zhang et al., 2024a). Moving beyond static prompts, automated attacks iteratively optimize adversarial instructions: RoboPAIR (Robey et al., 2025) pits an attacker LLM against the target robot to elicit harmful action code, POEX (Lu et al., 2024) applies gradient-based optimization to keep jailbroken policies physically executable, Blindfold (Huang et al., 2026) rewrites malicious goals into action-level instructions that appear benign yet cause hazardous outcomes, and Liu et al. (2024) attack the task planning stage with adversarial texts that induce dangerous decisions. 

6 CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we presented the first systematic evaluation of jailbreak guardrails for embodied agents. We organized existing guardrails into a four-dimensional taxonomy and built a pluggable framework that compares guardrails on a fixed agent backend along three dimensions: defense effectiveness, usability, and efficiency. Evaluating six representative guardrails under five jailbreak attacks across two embodied agent models, we found a consistent three-way trade-off. Our analysis further yields actionable insights for guardrail design: naive multimodal fusion dilutes the safety signal, earlier intervention intercepts sooner, and the decision mechanism determines the guardrail’s own cost. RoboSafe remains the most aggressive blocker by a wide margin: it intercepts 74.92% of the safe instruc-tions and reaches a task completion rate of only 20.74%, versus 61.87% for the no-guardrail agent, although it is less extreme than on GPT-4o (91.00% / 11.33%). Two further patterns replicate. First, planning-stage checks raise more false alarms than perception-stage checks for both model-based guardrails (18.39% vs. 2.68% for Llama Guard and 26.76% vs. 18.06% for EMBGuard, text-only), plausibly because a concrete low-level plan contains action verbs that appear riskier out of context. Second, adding the egocentric image lowers the false positive rate of every model-based variant (e.g., 26.76% → 18.12% for EMBGuard at the planning stage) and improves the completion rate accordingly (45.15% → 50.67%), echoing the defense–usability coupling discussed in Section 4.3: the same dilution of the safety signal that lets attacks through also spares benign instructions. As with GPT-4o, the completion rate of a guardrail closely tracks its false positive rate, and Llama Guard (Perception, T) preserves the most utility (59.20% completion, against 61.87% for the unguarded agent), confirming over-interception as the dominant source of utility loss on safe tasks. 

Efficiency. The right panel of Figure 4 reports the latency ratio of each guardrail on the safe tasks, and the overall picture is again consistent with the GPT-4o results. Nearly all guardrails slow the agent down, with latency ratios above 1; the only exception is Llama Guard at the planning stage with text input, which achieves the lowest ratio of 0.91. The eight variants of Llama Guard and EMBGuard stay close to one another, with ratios between 0.91 and 2.22, and the purely rule-based AgentSpec is only slightly slower than the unguarded agent. RoboSafe is again the least efficient guardrail by a wide margin, with a latency ratio of 20.40, and RoboGuard (15.05) and MADRA (4.70) are also clearly slower than the remaining methods.
