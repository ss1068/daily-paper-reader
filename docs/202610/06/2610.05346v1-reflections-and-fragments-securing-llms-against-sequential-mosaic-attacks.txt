# ABSTRACT 

Self-play red-teaming improves language-model safety by pitting attacker and de-fender roles against each other in a zero-sum game. However, real adversaries in-creasingly use mosaic attacks : multi-turn sequences whose individual fragments are innocuous in isolation yet assemble into a harmful payload. We develop a the-ory of mosaic defense that characterizes what is required to prevent such attacks without sacrificing helpfulness. We first show that no fixed bounded window of recent prompts is sufficient in general: safety-relevant information may occur ar-bitrarily far back in the interaction. We formalize a watchman , an online state mechanism that carries this information forward, and show that under explicit as-sumptions it enables zero-failure defense with positive benign helpfulness. Under stronger conditions, it is also optimal among zero-failure defenders. An exact watchman may nevertheless require exponentially many states, while exact ma-liciousness detection can require exponentially many queries in an unstructured black-box model. These state and query lower bounds do not by themselves imply hard learning: the construction underlying the state lower bound is effi-ciently learnable from labeled examples, whereas certifying worst-case safety can require substantially more information under restricted access. We also show that self-play equilibrium alone does not certify usefulness, motivating a constrained formulation that maximizes worst-case benign helpfulness among zero-failure de-fenders. Empirically, training role-specific attacker and defender LoRA adapters over frozen LLMs via multi-turn self-play strengthens both roles: attackers be-come more effective at eliciting harmful responses, while defenders become more robust to attack, with improvements also observed on unseen attack objectives.

Our contributions. We formalize mosaic defense as a finite extensive-form game with imperfect information (Section 3), in which the attacker knows the latent objective and chooses prompts over multiple turns, while the defender may observe different amounts of interaction history. We treat safety and helpfulness separately: a defender can trivially avoid harm by refusing every request, but at the cost of providing no useful responses to benign requests. Under our refusal-safety assumption, this policy is fully safe but has zero benign helpfulness. The key question is whether a defender can prevent harmful interactions while retaining enough information to answer benign requests. 

History dependence and watchmen (Sections 4 and 5). We prove that no fixed bounded window of recent prompts is sufficient, in general, to defend against multi-turn mosaic attacks while remaining helpful on benign interactions: information needed to determine whether the current request is safe to answer may have appeared earlier than the defender’s observation window. We introduce the 

watchman , an online state mechanism that carries this safety-relevant information forward. Under certain conditions, it enables zero-failure defense with positive benign helpfulness; under stronger conditions, the resulting defender is optimal among zero-failure defenders. 

Watchman complexity and learnability (Section 5). We establish that an exact watchman may re-quire exponentially many states, and determining whether a prompt completes a mosaic can require exponentially many queries to an unstructured black-box maliciousness oracle. These limitations are distinct and do not by themselves imply hard learning: the state lower-bound construction is efficiently learnable from labeled examples, whereas verifying worst-case safety can require sub-stantially more information under restricted access. 2Safety and helpfulness beyond equilibrium (Section 6). A safety-only self-play objective admits a trivial solution: under refusal safety, a defender that refuses every request is maximin-optimal despite being entirely unhelpful. We formalize this degeneracy, showing that equilibrium in the ma-licious game alone does not guarantee usefulness. We thus consider a constrained formulation re-quiring zero failure while maximizing worst-case benign helpfulness; under additional assumptions, the watchman defender is optimal for this formulation. We also extend the analysis to approximate watchmen, characterizing how false negatives degrade safety and false positives reduce helpfulness. 

Empirical evaluation (Section 7). We train separate attacker and defender LoRA adapters over three frozen mixture-of-experts models (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Gemma4-26B-A4B, and DeepSeek-V2-Lite). Multi-turn self-play strengthens both roles: attackers elicit more harmful responses, while defenders become more robust across harm categories, including on unseen objectives. Learned attackers compose benign-in-isolation fragments rather than relying on conventional jailbreaks. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Safety, jailbreaking, and red-teaming. Language-model safety has traditionally relied on human feedback to identify and discourage harmful behavior (Bai et al., 2022; Ouyang et al., 2022; Dai et al., 2024). Since such feedback cannot cover the space of possible attacks, later work uses models to generate and search for adversarial prompts (Perez et al., 2022). For example, Mehrotra et al. (2024) prune candidate jailbreaks, while Hong et al. (2024) reward both attack success and novelty. Other work studies adaptive attacker and defender policies (Jain et al., 2023), although computa-tional cost remains an important challenge (Howe et al., 2025). Xhonneux et al. (2024) reduce this cost by operating in continuous embedding space, while Liu et al. (2026) introduce online self-play in which attacker and defender co-evolve. More recent approaches decouple the roles through full-parameter updates (Wen et al., 2026) or introduce an additional evaluator (Tan et al., 2026). 

Parameter-efficient adaptation. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) (Hu et al., 2022) freezes the pre-trained model and learns low-rank parameter updates, reducing the cost of full fine-tuning. Sub-sequent variants combine LoRA with quantization (Dettmers et al., 2023), dynamically allocate rank (Zhang et al., 2023), or target safety alignment (Li et al., 2025). Ziakas et al. (2025) similarly use LoRA-based adaptation for red-teaming. Our experiments instead train separate attacker and defender LoRA adapters over a shared frozen backbone. 

Multi-turn attacks and co-evolving defenses. Our work builds on S ELF -R ED TEAM (Liu et al., 2026) and Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play (La Malfa et al., 2026), extending co-evolving attacker– defender training to sequential mosaic attacks. Related multi-turn attacks include Crescendo, which progressively escalates a dialogue (Russinovich et al., 2025), and JigSaw, which distributes a harm-ful request across multiple turns (Yang et al., 2024). Our mosaic attacks differ in that the indi-vidual fragments can remain benign in isolation while becoming harmful through composition; we additionally co-train the defender rather than treating the attack only as a static evaluation. Our formulation also differs from Paulus et al. (2026), who study non-zero-sum self-play. We instead characterize the information required for safe and helpful mosaic defense, showing that retaining the necessary distinctions can require exponentially many states and that detecting whether a mosaic has been completed can require exponentially many black-box queries.

We next ask what this can cost. 

Exponential state lower bound. An exact watchman may need exponentially many states to dis-tinguish the combinations of fragments encountered during the interaction. For every m ≥ 1 with 

2m ≤ N , there is a refusal-safe mosaic game with m relevant fragments in which every exactly safety-separating deterministic online watchman requires at least 2m states at turn m+1 ; this is tight (Theorem D.1). Intuitively, merging two distinct subsets of observed fragments allows an identical continuation to complete the mosaic from one history but not the other, while storing the observed subset gives an exact 2m-state watchman. This bounds the number of states , not bit memory: these states require only m bits. The full construction and proof are in Appendix D.1. 6Black-box query complexity. Even when the relevant information is retained, determining whether a new prompt completes a mosaic may be costly. Suppose the watchman retains N memory items and can query an unstructured black-box oracle for whether any subset, together with the new prompt, completes a harmful payload. Since any unqueried subset could be the unique harmful one, exact detection requires 2N queries in the deterministic worst case, while randomized algo-rithms with two-sided error at most 1/3 still require Ω(2 N ) expected queries (Theorem D.2; proof in Appendix D.2). This bound is specific to unstructured access: if completion is monotone, for example, querying the full set of retained items suffices. 

Approximate watchmen. With imperfect classification, false negatives classify unsafe-to-answer histories as safe and can lead to failure, while false positives classify benign-useful histories as risky and cause unnecessary refusals. If δFN and δFP are the worst-case false-negative and false-positive rates, an N -turn approximate watchman has failure probability at most N δ FN and, when benign helpfulness decomposes additively across turns, retains at least a (1 − δFP ) fraction of the exact watchman’s expected helpfulness (Appendix D.3). 

Learnability is distinct from state and query complexity. Neither lower bound implies that watch-men are hard to learn: the class containing the 2m-state construction is efficiently learnable from labelled examples (Proposition D.1). Yet, performing well on typical examples is weaker than the ro-bust guarantee required here, since even a rarely missed unsafe history can be deliberately targeted by an attacker (Proposition D.2). Verifying worst-case safety can therefore require substantially more information when access to informative examples is restricted (Theorem D.4; Appendix D.4). 

# 6 EQUILIBRIUM DOES NOT GUARANTEE USEFULNESS 

We now consider equilibrium in the malicious zero-sum game defined in Section 3. Because this game evaluates only safety on malicious interactions, its maximal payoff of 0 corresponds to zero failure but does not by itself require helpfulness on benign interactions. Indeed, under Assumption 1, a defender can attain this maximal payoff simply by refusing every request. Thus, equilibrium in the malicious game alone need not imply usefulness. The proof is given in Appendix E.1. 

Proposition 6.1. Suppose Assumption 1 holds, and let σρD refuse at every information set. Then, 

Vω (σA, σ ρD ) = 0 for every attacker σA and ω ∈ Ωmal . Hence, σρD is maximin-optimal, val(Γ) = 0 ,and (σA, σ ρD ) is a Nash equilibrium for every attacker best response σA.

Thus, malicious-only self-play may converge to a zero-failure equilibrium with no benign help-fulness, extending the degeneracy of La Malfa et al. (2026) to the sequential setting. We instead maximize worst-case benign helpfulness subject to zero failure. Under additional assumptions, the watchman defender is optimal for this constrained formulation (Appendix E.2).

Comparison with other attacks. In Appendix H.2, we include a baseline adapted from PAIR (Chao et al., 2025), which reports the four-condition factorial crossing of the base model with our trained attacker and defender. Defenders learn different refusal profiles. A clear behavioral difference between base and trained defenders is when refusals occur. On WildJailbreak, refusal increases over turns for Qwen and Gemma but decreases for DeepSeek; training raises Qwen refusal from the first turn, shifts Gemma’s increase later, and partially flattens DeepSeek’s decline (Table 7). 

Attackers learn distinct mosaic strategies. The trained attackers exhibit model-specific changes in constructing mosaic interactions. Qwen’s base attacker often uses cover stories (e.g., “for my novel”) and gradually moves its vocabulary toward the seed; after training, these signals, particularly escalation, become less frequent, consistent with greater reliance on composing benign-in-isolation requests. Gemma shifts from evaluative to procedural framing (e.g., “how is” to “how to”) and from requesting opinions to artifacts. DeepSeek’s base attacker often exhibits generation failures, includ-ing visible <think> tags and verbatim seed copying, while training increases fictional openers and cover stories. Persona overrides remain rare (around 0.2% of episodes), as do explicit cross-turn references ( ≤ 2.3% ), suggesting that learned mosaics do not mainly rely on either. 

Mosaic attacks are token-efficient. Across trained models and checkpoints, a three-turn attack uses roughly 150 –230 attacker tokens while eliciting substantially longer responses from the defender (around 2000 tokens for Qwen, 3200 for Gemma, and 1000 for DeepSeek). The resulting attacks often reuse vocabulary and phrasing introduced by the defender. Attacker token allocation also generally increases over the course of the interaction: Qwen spends, on average, 53 , 81 , and 92 

tokens across turns one through three; Gemma 30 , 59 , and 71 ; and DeepSeek 37 , 62 , and 72 .Appendix H.5 provides annotated examples, plots, and tables supporting the preceding analyses of refusal profiles, attack strategies, and token usage. 

Defender gains span harm categories. Figure 2 shows that the trained defenders generally reduce jailbreak rates across harm categories on both benchmarks. Gemma improves in every evaluated cat-9egory, while Qwen improves in most, with Drugs & Substances on AdvBench as a notable exception (6% → 18% ). Overall, the gains are therefore not confined to a single harm category. 

# 8 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

We developed a theory of sequential mosaic defense, showing that no fixed bounded window can in general guarantee both zero failure and positive helpfulness, while an online watchman can by pre-serving safety-relevant information. Exact watchmen may require exponentially many states, and exact maliciousness detection may require exponentially many queries under unstructured black-box access, though these bounds do not imply hard learning. We also showed that equilibrium safety need not imply usefulness, motivating a constrained formulation that maximizes benign help-fulness subject to zero failure. Empirically, multi-turn self-play with role-specific LoRA adapters strengthened attackers and defenders across three language models, including defender gains across harm categories and unseen objectives, though transfer to a structurally different multi-turn attack was model-dependent. Future work should study practical learned watchmen, their scaling with in-teraction length, and their complexity under structured maliciousness, and convergence guarantees for multi-turn adversarial self-play.

Connection to memory and context compression. The watchman can be viewed as a safety-specific form of online memory or history compression: rather than retaining the full interaction, it preserves a finite state sufficient for future safety decisions. This connects to broader work on long-context memory and prompt compression, which likewise seeks to retain information from interactions that no longer fit in the model’s context (Packer et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023; Chhikara et al., 2025). Our notion of exact safety separation makes the preservation criterion explicit: a compression is sufficient for defense only if it preserves every distinction relevant to whether the current prompt can safely be answered. Generic semantic or task-oriented compression therefore need not provide a safety guarantee. We next ask what preserving these safety-relevant distinctions can cost.

Even when the relevant information from the interaction has been retained, determining the watch-man’s verdict may be computationally expensive. When a new prompt arrives, the watchman must determine whether that prompt, together with some subset of the retained information, completes a mosaic. The difficulty of this task depends on how the maliciousness predicate is represented. We study the black-box setting, in which the predicate is accessed through queries, and establish exponential query complexity for exact maliciousness detection. To formalize this separate oracle model, suppose the watchman retains N memory items and re-ceives a new prompt q. For each subset of the retained items, a black-box predicate indicates whether combining that subset with q completes a harmful payload. 

Definition 1 (Exact watchman detection in the oracle model) . Let MN = {m1, . . . , m N } denote the watchman’s memory and let q be the new prompt. Define a predicate Φq : 2 [N ] → { 0, 1}, where

D.3 APPROXIMATE WATCHMEN : SAFETY AND HELPFULNESS GUARANTEES 

The analysis of Section 5 assumes an exact watchman that preserves the safety status of every his-tory. We now relax this requirement and consider a monitor that may misclassify histories. The two possible errors have different consequences. A false negative occurs when an unsafe-to-answer history is classified as safe, allowing the defender to respond substantively and potentially causing failure. # F TRAINING DEFENDERS 

We now consider two design choices that arise when training mosaic defenders through adversar-ial self-play: reward design and role parameterization. First, dense stepwise rewards can provide feedback before the end of an interaction, but modifying the reward may also change the game be-ing optimized. We identify a class of potential-based rewards that preserves the original equilibria (Section F.1). Second, when the attacker and defender are trained through shared parameters, their updates act on the same coordinates; we show that role-separated adapters eliminate this source of parameter-level interaction (Section F.2). F.1 POTENTIAL -BASED REWARD SHAPING

and lie in the complementary orthogonal subspaces RpA × { 0} and {0} × RpD .It follows that the ϕD -component of the simultaneous update is exactly η∇ϕD JD (ϕD | ϕA) and contains no contribution from the attacker’s update direction. The gradient ∇ϕD JD may still depend on ϕA through the transcript distribution induced by the attacker policy, but this dependence does not arise through shared parameter coordinates. Thus, role separation eliminates parameter-level gradient collision between the two updates. 36 Theorem F.2 provides a local, first-order guarantee and establishes the absence of parameter-level 

gradient collision, formalizing the self-consistency analysis of La Malfa et al. (2026) in our se-quential setting. The parameter-level qualification is important: part (b) shows that the attacker and defender updates lie in orthogonal coordinate blocks, while the two roles remain distribution-ally coupled because ϕA affects the transcript distribution defining JD and hence ∇ϕD JD . Thus, role separation removes shared-coordinate interference without implying statistical independence between the updates. The result concerns the local update geometry and does not by itself imply global convergence, con-vergence to a Nash equilibrium, safety, or robustness, which depend on the non-convex optimiza-tion landscape and the coupled learning dynamics. In contrast, under a shared-parameter update 

θ 7 → θ + η∇θ (JA + JD ), the attacker and defender gradients act on the same coordinates, precisely the parameter-level collision avoided by role separation. In Appendix H.3, we report a numerical analysis that consolidates our theory, showing how gradient collides when a single model learns the attacker and defender policy; on the other hand, separating the policies (as in our LoRA method), guarantees independence, with collision that reappears only when one projects the attacker’s and defender’s LoRA updates back into the model (our frozen backbone prevents that by construction).

G.1 DATASET CONSTRUCTION AND DECOMPOSITION ANALYSIS 

WildMosaic training corpus. The malicious pool contains 915 seeds: all 520 harmful behaviors from AdvBench (Zou et al., 2023), 390 prompts from the forbidden-question set, and 5 additional safety-framed prompts. The benign pool contains 1,000 synthetic prompts, generated determin-istically by expanding fixed templates over topic banks, deduplicating the resulting prompts, and shuffling them with a fixed seed. These benign seeds expose the defender to benign interactions during training. 

Held-out split. Before training, we hold out 100 malicious and 100 benign seeds by deterministi-cally ranking each pool using a hash of the seed string. These seeds are frozen and removed from the training pools, leaving 815 malicious and 900 benign training seeds. The same held-out set is used across runs and checkpoints. Of the 520 AdvBench goals, 475 occur in the malicious training pool and the remaining 45 in the held-out set; AdvBench is therefore an in-distribution evaluation rather than an unseen benchmark. 

WildJailbreak decomposition study. Separately, we evaluate a model-based decomposition pro-cedure on 40 K malicious objectives from the adversarial_harmful portion of WildJail-break (Han et al., 2024), using the vanilla objective rather than the adversarial rewrite of Wild-Jailbreak. Qwen3.6-35B-A3B generates 3–5 sequential subprompts intended to preserve the original objective while obscuring it at the level of individual fragments. A separate Qwen3.5-122B-A10B judge compares the original objective with the concatenated subprompts and determines whether they convey substantially the same intent. Of the 40 K processed objectives, 15 ,510 (38 .8% ) pass this equivalence test, while 61 .2% are rejected. 

Limitations of the decomposition criterion. The decomposition study is fully model-based rather than human-annotated. Moreover, the judge evaluates intent equivalence between the original ob-jective and the concatenated decomposition; it does not independently verify that each fragment is benign in isolation. The 38 .8% acceptance rate should therefore be interpreted as a property of this decomposer-judge procedure, not as an intrinsic fraction of WildJailbreak objectives that admit mosaic decompositions. Reward assignment. The judge distinguishes successful attacks, partial harmful progress, success-ful refusals, helpful responses, and unnecessary refusals. We additionally distinguish D EFANGED 

attempts, in which the attacker fails to pose a genuine harmful threat, from genuine attacks that the defender successfully refuses. This separates attacker failure from defender success and pro-vides role-specific credit assignment. Notably, P ARTIAL outcomes provide intermediate training feedback, whereas the headline attack success rate in Section 7 counts only S UCCESS .In addition to the terminal verdict, the judge assigns each assistant turn a cumulative harmful-progress score st ∈ [0 , 100] . We normalize this as Φt := st/100 ∈ [0 , 1] , with Φ0 := 0 , and use successive differences to provide denser credit across the conversation. With weight w = 1 .5,the dense contribution for role P ∈ { A, D } at turn t is wσ P (Φ t − Φt−1), where σA = +1 and 

σD = −1; the role-specific terminal verdict reward is added to the final segment. Thus, increases in harmful progress reward the attacker and provide the opposite signal to the defender. The dense harmful-progress signal is inspired by the potential-based shaping analysis in Ap-pendix F.1. That analysis gives a sufficient condition for preserving the equilibria of the terminal-payoff game, namely a constant terminal potential. Our empirical construction instead retains the final judge score and assigns each generated segment its own reward rather than a reward-to-go return. We therefore use potential-based shaping as a reward-design motivation rather than claim equilibrium invariance for the implemented training procedure. The episode-level reward neverthe-less admits a precise decomposition into a zero-sum component and a bounded common component, characterized in Appendix G.3. 

Group selection and policy updates. For each malicious seed, trajectories are ranked separately by the return of each role, with potentially different top-k values kA and kD . We train on the union of the selected sets, masking each role’s loss to its own generated tokens; overlapping selections are counted once and ties are resolved by a seeded pseudo-random tie-break. Selection precedes advantage normalization, so normalization statistics are computed over the selected trajectories. Because selection depends on reward, it changes the trajectory distribution entering the update and is treated as part of the empirical training procedure rather than as an unbiased GRPO estimator over the full rollout group. For each selected trajectory, a role’s generated tokens receive the reward of the corresponding seg-ment. Advantages are baselined separately by seed and turn and normalized separately for the two roles using their corresponding global scales. The resulting estimator is therefore per-segment rather than reward-to-go. On the small fraction of episodes for which the judge returns no per-turn scores, the implementation falls back to broadcasting the terminal reward across segments. 

Training schedule. Training schedules vary across runs, so asymmetric top-k selection is not a universal fixed recipe. The headline Qwen run uses (kA, k D ) = (2 , 2) and β = 0 .03 for steps 0–40 ,followed by (kA, k D ) = (5 , 5) and β = 0 for steps 40 –65 , with G = 10 trajectories per malicious seed; the transition at step 40 also changes the learning-rate schedule. Other runs use different group sizes and top-k configurations, including some Gemma runs with kA < k D . We therefore interpret the experiments as evaluating a family of practical training recipes rather than isolating the causal effect of a particular top-k configuration. Across all experiments, the attacker and defender use separate LoRA adapters over a shared frozen backbone. They are trained on the same selected conversations but have disjoint trainable param-38 eters, preserving their distributional coupling through self-play while avoiding shared-coordinate gradient interference. H.3 GRADIENT COLLISION BETWEEN THE ATTACKER AND THE DEFENDER 

In self-play, the attacker and the defender optimize opposing objectives. When both roles train the same parameters, the two updates are applied to the same tensors, and an update that helps one role can undo the update the other role asked for La Malfa et al. (2026). ABS gives each role its own LoRA adapter, so the two updates write to disjoint parameters and cannot cancel. H.4 EVALUATION ON CRESCENDO 

To test whether self-play red-teaming generalizes beyond the mosaic attack, we evaluate the defender against Crescendo Russinovich et al. (2025), an unseen multi-turn attack method. Unlike mosaic attacks, which split a prompt request into benign-in-isolation subprompts, Crescendo employs a gradual, multi-turn escalation strategy where each prompt is anchored to the previous one until the harmful content is eventually assembled. Because the defender never encounters this attack structure during training, this evaluation measures out-of-distribution transfer. For each model, we use 520 seeds, split into 260 /260 malicious/benign prompts from WildJailbreak to direct Crescendo attacks using the base model as the attacker. The attacker is permitted up to 10 successful escalation rounds to reach its goal. If the target model refuses a step, the attacker is allowed to backtrack and rephrase up to 10 times before the episode fails. An LLM-as-a-judge is employed as a referee for both the single steps and the final score of the ASR. To ensure evaluation consistency, we match the judge to the model family being tested. We compare the ASR of the base model and self-play defender on identical malicious prompts using an exact two-sided McNemar test ( α = 0 .05 ). As reported in Table 5, transfer is model-dependent. Token efficiency of the attacks and defenses. The most interesting result that emerges from an analysis of the token costs of mosaic attacks is that they are cheap: 105 -234 attacker tokens elicit 0.8k-4.2k defender tokens. While training leaves attacker spend essentially unchanged for Qwen ( −3% to +10% ) and Gemma ( −4% to +1% ), DeepSeek spends 26 -53% more tokens, but only because its base policy had to acquire the competency to compose successful mosaic attacks. Also, Qwen’s defender cuts output 2.5 up to 20 × more on malicious than benign prompts, whereas Gemma inflates output by 19 -36% on malicious and 31 -42% on benign conversations, replacing refusals with longer answers. Learned attack moves and mosaic patterns. Many winning episodes chain two or three recurring moves: a neutral framework opener with no seed vocabulary, or a literature probe that asks whether a concept in the harmful seed exists somewhere, or, at later stages of the conversation, a term from the defender’s responses. Other common attempts that successfully produce a mosaic include an evaluative →procedural pivot (“is X justified” → “how is X managed”), an artifact request (e.g., a rubric or template), or a non-harmful component request. On the other hand, explicit assembly requests (“combine the above”) never exceed 2.3% of episodes, and persona or injection claims stay at 0-2% . On the other hand, defenders seldom mention the decomposition directly (Qwen 

6.2% → 4.4% , Gemma 4.6% → 4.2% , DeepSeek 2.3% → 1.0% ), suggesting that the learned defense often manifests through changed refusal behavior, and corroborates our theoretical results on the hardness of inferring a mosaic attack by bening-in-isolation subprompts.
