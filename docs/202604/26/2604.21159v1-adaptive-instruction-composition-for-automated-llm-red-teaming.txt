Abstract 

Many approaches to LLM red-teaming leverage an attacker LLM to discover jailbreaks against a target. Several of them task the attacker with identifying effective strategies through trial and error, resulting in a semantically limited range of successes. Another approach discov-ers diverse attacks by combining crowdsourced harmful queries and tactics into instructions for the attacker, but does so at random, limiting effectiveness. This article introduces a novel framework, A DAPTIVE INSTRUCTION COM -

> POSITION

, that combines crowdsourced texts according to an adaptive mechanism trained to jointly optimize effectiveness with diversity. We use reinforcement learning to balance ex-ploration with exploitation in a combinatorial space of instructions to guide the attacker to-ward diverse generations tailored to target vul-nerabilities. We demonstrate that our approach substantially outperforms random combination on a set of effectiveness and diversity met-rics, even under model transfer. Further, we show that it surpasses a host of recent adap-tive approaches on Harmbench. We employ a lightweight neural contextual bandit that adapts to contrastive embedding inputs, and provide ablations suggesting that the contrastive pre-training enables the network to rapidly general-ize and scale to the massive space as it learns. Warning: this article discusses malicious con-tent and methods for generating it using LLMs. 

1 Introduction 

Though LLMs have become indispensable tools across several industries, they may fall victim to a variety of different attacks that compromise the safety of their users. One commonly studied vari-ety of attack is the jailbreak , consisting of a harm-ful request formulated to bypass the LLM’s safety protocols using some tactic and solicit from it mis-informed, biased, toxic, or sensitive content (Zou et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024c; Deng et al., 2024; Xu 

Figure 1: Overview of Adaptive Instruction Composition 

et al., 2024). For example, though in a prior investi-gation GPT-4 refused to answer an array of harmful requests, it engaged with these requests once they were translated into a low-resource language (Yong et al., 2024). Much research into LLM safety fo-cuses on developing robust defenses against attacks using finetuned models (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022; Korbak et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2024), but training them would require that an expansive training set of attacks be accumulated. Investiga-tors have therefore turned to the practice of LLM red-teaming , in which a target LLM is attacked in order to identify its idiosyncratic vulnerabilities proactively. Attacks may then be leveraged within safety training to improve defenses. Though manual attacking is commonly em-ployed to this end (Ganguli et al., 2022), recent proposals develop automated approaches to red-teaming against a black-box target LLM (see Perez et al. (2022); Ganguli et al. (2022); Chao et al. (2024); Mehrotra et al. (2023); Shen et al. (2024); Liu et al. (2024b,a), among others). The objec-tive of automated red-teaming is multipartite: the system must discover successful attacks at a high rate, and the attacks it discovers must be semanti-cally diverse (Perez et al., 2022). Many proposals 

> arXiv:2604.21159v1 [cs.CR] 22 Apr 2026

share a common framework in which an attacker LLM is tasked with generating a set of successful attacks against a given target LLM in a series of interactions whose content is assessed for safety by an evaluator. An instruction is served to the attacker requiring, for example, that it generate a harmful query; further, the attacker must role-play as an innocent-seeming individual during attack-ing in order to deceive the target. To discover a novel jailbreak, the attacker must formulate and exploit its own strategies through an iterative pro-cess of trial and error. Some approaches adapt the LLM attacker via finetuning with reward signals (Perez et al., 2022); in another approach, tactics discovered during successful trials are stored for later reuse (Liu et al., 2024a). These solutions may discover strategies never before documented. How-ever, prior attempts with adaptive attackers have yielded a semantically limited range of successful attacks (see Beutel et al. (2024) for discussion), or have only discovered a few dozen novel tac-tics across many trials (Liu et al., 2024a). Such approaches do not guarantee that the search for vul-nerable areas would monitor within a reasonable time frame the full combination space of previously discovered queries, tactics, and styles. This consti-tutes a lost opportunity, considering that such data can be combined and exploited by attack generators in various ways. This poses a problem for safety teams that not only want to expand safety training with novel attacks, but seek a system that account-ably searches over any previously documented area of the attack space in order to do so. 

1.1 WildTeaming using WildJailbreak 

However, a recently proposed framework WILD TEAMING (Jiang et al., 2024c) discovers an array of jailbreaks against popular LLMs by combining within the attacker’s instructions text samples from a large corpus of harmful queries and jailbreak tactics. This open-source dataset, called WILD JAILBREAK , contains over 50K harmful queries and over 13K jailbreak tactics that were crowd-sourced from the Internet. The authors illustrate that the approach outperforms competing approaches on Harmbench (Mazeika et al., 2024), a public jailbreaking benchmark, including the gradient-based GCG (Zou et al., 2023) as well as the black-box strategy PAIR (Chao et al., 2024), the latter of which uses an attacker LLM to formulate through trial and error a successful strategy to transform a query into a jailbreak. Though WildTeaming generates a diversity of attacks, it combines queries and tactics 

at random , without exploiting previous attack successes or employing optimization. 

1.2 Optimizing Attacks using Adaptive Instruction Composition 

Whereas generation by trial and error struggles to capture diverse exploration throughout attacking, WildTeaming, as we will illustrate, is hindered by its choice not to exploit attack successes, falling short for safety teams that seek to identify in a rea-sonable time frame and bias attacking toward the array of attack themes and tactics that characterize their in-house LLMs’ vulnerabilities. The space of possible attack instructions defined by WildTeam-ing — that is, the set of combinations of harmful queries with some finite number of jailbreak tac-tics drawn from WildJailbreak — is massive. For instructions that draw one query and two tactics, the space consists of over 50000 ∗ 13000 2 possibil-ities; i.e., it constitutes over 8 trillion instructions. An algorithm that balances diverse exploration of this space with adaptation to vulnerabilities may be profitably employed to augment safety training sets in proportion with attack-success likelihood across a wide range of possibilities, and would be useful for identifying and patching vulnerabilities as they emerge and re-emerge throughout continual safety training or an adversarial set-up (Ge et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2024b). The effectiveness of random combination would deteriorate as new at-tacks or areas are identified or if a richer space of combinations were to be explored, since this space would grow rapidly with new contributions; but an algorithm that searches selectively could scale with these contributions. Moreover, under a scenario in which red-teaming is being conducted against a well-aligned LLM, a random-combination strat-egy would struggle to identify successes at a high rate; but an adaptive algorithm could exploit the vulnerabilities it identifies. We therefore introduce a novel framework, ADAPTIVE INSTRUCTION COMPOSITION , that that addresses the gap by integrating recent ad-vances from multiple disciplines. The framework supplants WildTeaming’s method of random text combination along with a set of other baselines on a set of effectiveness and diversity metrics, us-ing bandit algorithms within reinforcement learn-ing. The system combines texts into instructions selectively, tractably balancing exploration with ex-ploitation in the massive combinatorial space, in order to jointly optimize attack effectiveness with diversity. We employ as our algorithm of choice Neural Thompson Sampling (Zhang et al., 2021), a variant of the contextual multi-armed bandit. The bandit trains over the course of attacking to approx-imate the evaluator’s responses using Gaussian dis-tributions defined over instruction compositions, and compositions are selected in proportion with these learned distributions to balance success maxi-mization with diverse exploration. The architecture consists of a lightweight neural network that takes contrastive embeddings of combined text samples as input (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019). These em-beddings enable the network to scale to the massive space under a limited time constraint, adapting only a small number of parameters to generalize across whole groups of semantically related texts. 

2 Related Work 

We focus on automated approaches to LLM red-teaming — see, for example, Feffer et al. (2024); Rawat et al. (2024); Purpura et al. (2025) for broad-purpose surveys and Lin et al. (2024); Mo et al. (2024) for thorough treatments. Early approaches augment attack sets using text modification tech-niques (Morris et al., 2020; Wallace et al., 2021; Rocamora et al., 2024). More recent approaches prompt or train an attacker LLM to generate at-tacks (Perez et al., 2022; Ganguli et al., 2022). Some of the most recent proposals employ attacker LLMs in environments for conducting iterative at-tacks, including PAIR (Chao et al., 2024), TAP (Mehrotra et al., 2023), DAN (Shen et al., 2024), and AutoDAN(-Turbo) (Liu et al., 2024b,a). These systems evolve the content of an initial attack gen-eration in an iterative chain, tasking the attacker with formulating and exploiting its own tactics in the process. Other more complex ecosystems (Ge et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2024b) further develop the iterative-attacking paradigm, but set up an ad-versarial environment in which the attacker and target respectively adapt their attack strategies and defense strategies in tandem. WildTeaming (Jiang et al., 2024c) discovers a relatively diverse array of jailbreaks against popular LLMs by combining within the attacker’s instructions randomly com-bined texts from a large, open-source corpus of crowd-sourced harmful queries and jailbreak tac-tics, called WildJailbreak. 

3 Problem Statement 

We define our problem as follows. An attacker is instructed in some way to generate successful at-tacks against a target in a series of trials, and whose attack strategy can be adapted using the outputs of an evaluator. We want to devise and train a learning mechanism that can compose attack instructions from a massive combinatorial space of text samples to jointly optimize attack effectiveness and diver-sity. The evaluator’s outputs are revealed one at a time, and because LLM generation is costly, the outputs are revealed slowly, so that only a small proper subset of possible instruction compositions can be explored and leveraged to train the learning mechanism.

4.2 Adaptive Instruction Composition 

We implement an interactive environment consist-ing of four models: a trio of LLMs consisting of the attacker A(·), target T (·), and evaluator E(·), along with a fourth model f (· | θ) that selects instruction candidates adaptively using reinforcement learning. The model is trained in an on-line fashion across an experiment consisting of T trials. In a given trial t, we randomly sample texts to fully populate K candidate instruction composi-tions. For each candidate instruction composition, we sample a vanilla query qt,k ∈ Q along with a set of jailbreak tactics jt,k, 1, ..., j t,k,n ∈ J n (step 1 in Algorithm 1). The sample combination is mapped to its corresponding embedding represen-tation xt,k = e(qt,k )⌢e(jt,k, 1)⌢... ⌢e(jt,k,n ) and passed to f (· | θt−1), which scores it conditioned on f ’s most recently updated parameters, θt−1, re-turning ˆrt,k = f (xt,k | θt−1) (steps 2 and 3). The index of the highest scoring candidate, k∗, is then computed, and the text samples used to construct this candidate are combined into a given instruc-tion template submitted to the attacker (steps 4 and 5). The attacker generates an attack based on these instructions. The attack and response are then passed to the evaluator to provide a safety assessment, rt,k ∗ (steps 6-8). Finally, the embed-ding representation of the selected instruction xt,k ∗

and the evaluator’s assessment rt,k ∗ are passed to a stochastic gradient descent algorithm to update the parameters of f from θt−1 to θt, to better ap-proximate the evaluator’s success distribution (step 9). Here we introduce a deduplication step: if the selected instruction composition led to a successful attack, then it is blocked from being considered by 

f for use in future trials. f must therefore general-ize success signals across input features to perform successfully. The cycle repeats across T trials, at which time the experiment concludes.

5 Performance Simulations 

This section compares Adaptive Instruction Com-position first to WildTeaming’s approach of ran-domly combining instructions, and then to an array of alternative red-teaming strategies using Harm-bench (Mazeika et al., 2024), a public jailbreak-ing benchmark. We implement simulation envi-ronments built off of the PyRIT software package (Munoz et al., 2024), employing its framework of asynchronous function calls to LLMs to speed up simulations (see Section 8). In our implementation of Adaptive Instruction Composition, we employ a lightweight two-layer bandit network with hidden dimensionality 100. Network size thus totals to (10 + 10 + 1) ∗

100 + (100 + 1) ∗ 1 = 2201 weights for simu-lations sampling one query and tactic, or to (e.g.) 

(10+3 ∗10+1) ∗100+(100+1) ∗1 = 4201 weights in simulations sampling one query and three tactics. We serve K = 500 instruction candidates to the network. At the end of each red-teaming trial, the network trains on the results from the evaluator to approximate reward distributions over instruction compositions; the stochastic gradient descent algo-rithm we employ uses a learning rate of 0.01 and a weight decay mechanism that divides λ by trial count, in line with the authors’ implementation of Neural Thompson Sampling.

5.3 Comparing to Other Strategies 

In addition, we compare the performance of Adap-tive Instruction Composition against a number of adaptive LLM red-teaming strategies using the pub-lic benchmark Harmbench (Mazeika et al., 2024). We downloaded the 400 behaviors (similar to WildTeaming’s vanilla queries) from the Harm-bench validation and test sets and preprocessed them into reduced embeddings along with the WildTeaming queries and tactics. Using only the WildTeaming data, we trained an aggressive (λ = 0.01 ), single-tactic bandit for 10K tri-als against Mistral-7B and Llama-3-70B-Instruct. For training, we use Llama-Guard-2 as judge, but for Harmbench evaluation, we leverage the Harmbench safety classifier, a carefully fine-tuned 

Llama-2-13B-chat model. Harmbench evaluation rounds take place strictly after bandit training — we do not train the model whatsoever during these rounds. For evaluation, we set up instruction com-position so that the bandit would be required to use a fixed behavior qi from the Harmbench set, but would otherwise be allowed to optimally se-lect a jailbreak tactic from a sampled set of 500 candidates. Following AutoDAN-Turbo’s set up (Liu et al., 2024a), we require the bandit to reuse behavior qi during instruction combination up until the attacker LLM (Mixtral-8x7B), prompted with 

qi, generates a successful attack against the target as judged by the Harmbench classifier, or until 150 attempts have been reached, at which point the bandit would be passed the next behavior in the set. The classifier assesses attack success by de-termining whether the target’s response constitutes an instance of the behavior qi. If any of the 150 attempts with qi was successful, then the system would record that qi was successfully leveraged by our strategy. The Harmbench ASR is the propor-tion of behaviors qi in the Harmbench set that were successfully leveraged by our strategy to create a jailbreak during one of the 150 attempts with qi.We compare our performance to that reported in Liu et al. (2024a). As we can see in Table 6, Adaptive Instruction Composition substantially out-performed the alternatives on Harmbench ASR, a further testament to its effectiveness. We nonethe-less see frameworks such as AutoDAN-Turbo as quite valuable in that they can learn tactics from scratch that are potentially novel to red-teaming. 5.4 Scaling Insights from Ablation 

We conducted an ablation study (see Appendix C) over different hyperparameters defined by Adap-tive Instruction Composition, and found that three major factors drove performance: λ (and, simi-larly, ν, since both terms scale variance), the size of the bandit’s input (e.g., tactic count, as well as dimensionality of component embeddings as in Fig-ure 5), and, interestingly, the choice of embedding model. We asked whether performance would de-pend on the model delivering sentence embeddings — in particular, SBERT versus BERT (Devlin et al., 2019). The key difference between the two models is that SBERT was contrastively pretrained — i.e., pretrained to deliver embeddings situated within a semantically organized space, with topic clus-ters spread apart. BERT, on the other hand, was not contrastively pretrained, and its embeddings have been found to perform worse on semantic-similarity tasks (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019). We trained two varieties of aggressive ( λ =0.01 ), single-tactic bandit against Mistral-7B, dif-fering only in terms of embedding type. As illus-trated in Figure 4 above, we see that the bandit served SBERT embeddings learns faster and ob-tains a higher ASR than the bandits served BERT embeddings (using the [CLS] token or averaged output tokens). The results suggest that contrastive pretraining supports rapid generalization in rein-forcement learning. In particular, a semantically organized input space allows RL models such as bandits to scale to massive action spaces, helping to generalize rewards over semantically related ac-tions within online learning settings in which train-ing labels are scarce. Semantically organized in-puts thereby render learning over massive actions spaces more tractable. 

6 Future Directions 

One broader purpose of this research was to provide an initial evaluation of RL-driven prompt compo-sition for efficiently discovering diverse, effective LLM outputs (in our case, novel jailbreaks) in the face of black-box evaluation and an exploration-exploitation tradeoff. A promising future direction lies in evaluating a wider array of strategies for 

model-driven exploration over massive spaces such as ours — in particular, alternative setups for opti-mizing diversity metrics as well as investigations into a broader array of diversity metrics themselves. Within red-teaming especially, promising next steps include expanding the instruction template to express a greater range of attacks (e.g., including a slot for length), expanding our framework to iden-tify hallucinations, as well as expanding our frame-work to complex targets such as image generators and agents. An investigator might, for example, adapt our approach to automate indirect prompt injections (Greshake et al., 2023). To describe, an attacker can manually submit to a RAG agent a harmful query whose answer is contained in its content base, available to be retrieved. The agent should avoid responding even when the answer is placed into its context. An AIC-based approach to identifying such content-specific vulnerabilities would be to adaptively sample answer content from these bases. The LLM attacker can then be in-structed to formulate harmful queries that would be addressed using the sampled answer. This would test whether the target agent engages with the query using the retrieved answer. 

7 Conclusion 

This article introduced Adaptive Instruction Com-position, a novel framework rooted in RL-driven prompt composition. Our framework balances exploration with exploitation in a combinatorial space of instructions to guide the attacker toward diverse attacks tailored to the target. We show that AIC surmounts WildTeaming’s random combina-tion strategy even under model transfer, and that it surmounts recent adaptive approaches on Harm-bench. We employ a lightweight network along with contrastive embeddings, the latter of which enable the network to generalize rapidly across the massive space during RL adaptation. 8 Limitations 

One limitation revolves around evaluation scope and reliability. Due to internal constraints, our ex-periments could only focus on three open-source target models. Though the results of our transfer-ability experiments suggest that our system gen-eralizes across models, we recognize that it may not fully generalize across all extant LLM architec-tures. Further, we relied on Llama-Guard-2 as our evaluator, which may have produced false positives and negatives. Though we additionally validated with Harmbench’s evaluator, future work should conduct experiments using alternative evaluators. Another limitation that this approach shares with the aforementioned automated iterative red-teaming strategies is its computational costs. Each of our simulations leveraged three LLMs (at-tacker, target, judge) from the following set of models: Mistral-7B, Mixtral-8x7B, Llama-3-70B-Instruct, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, Llama-Guard-2, and Harmbench-Llama-2-13b-cls. During simula-tions, Mixtral, Llama-3, and Llama-3.3 were pow-ered by 4 GPUs; the rest were powered by 1. We additionally leveraged 1 GPU to train our bandit. Our simulations that compared against WildTeam-ing took 10-12 hours to run — they employed Mixtral as attacker, Llama-Guard-2 as judge, and one of Mistral, Llama-3, or Llama3.3 as target. For a training simulation that used Mixtral, Mis-tral, and Llama-Guard-2, a simulation took be-tween (4 + 1 + 1 + 1) ∗ 10 = 70 GPU hours and 

(4+1+1+1) ∗12 = 84 GPU hours; for a simulation that used Mixtral, Llama-3, and Llama-Guard-2, a simulation took between (4+4+1+1) ∗10 = 100 

GPU hours and (4 + 4 + 1 + 1) ∗ 12 = 120 GPU hours. The Harmbench simulations similarly took 10 hours to complete — e.g., (4 + 4 + 1 + 1) ∗ 10 = 100 GPU hours. Our work would benefit from any future research that aims to address the latency challenges currently associated with automated red-teaming. Note that simulations with Adaptive In-struction Composition were approximately on par with WildTeaming Simulations in terms of elapsed time. 

9 Ethics Statement 

This research aims to address a central challenge: to develop a system that can generate a compre-hensive safety training set tailored toward the id-iosyncratic vulnerabilities of a given LLM. Its pur-pose is to contribute to the training of safe and broadly usable LLMs, in line with other automated red-teaming systems. To mitigate the risk of mali-cious parties exploiting these systems, we strongly recommend that red-teaming practitioners within safety organizations engage in responsible disclo-sure of discovered vulnerabilities to the relevant model developers (see below). We are devoted to the responsible development of our pipeline and the responsible use of any data generated from it, and we believe that the benefits that our system will bring to LLM safety outweighs these risks. 

Intended use and controlled evaluation. Adap-tive Instruction Composition is designed for con-trolled red-teaming and security evaluation in or-ganizational settings, and not as a general-use at-tack tool. All experiments reported in this work were conducted against open-weight models in iso-lated research environments with no external de-ployment. Any generated attack datasets or arti-facts developed by other investigators after adapt-ing our framework should only be shared with veri-fied safety researchers, and trained policy weights should require institutional affiliation verification.

A Bandit Architecture and Training Regime 

This section provides a self-contained specification of the Neural Thompson Sampling (NTS) algo-rithm as instantiated in Adaptive Instruction Com-position, covering initialization, posterior compu-tation, arm selection, parameter updates, and our extensions to our framework. We refer the reader to Zhang et al. (2021) for the original NTS formu-lation and regret analysis, but note that we follow and describe the implementation provided by the original authors here. 

A.1 Network Architecture and Initialization 

Our bandit network f (· | θ) is a fully connected feed-forward network with L = 2 layers (i.e., one hidden layer), hidden dimension 100 , and contin-uous output. Per the authors’ implementation, pa-rameters were initialized using the current default procedure for PyTorch nn.Linear , namely Kaim-ing uniform. Their original strategy differs from this implementation in a couple ways — in par-ticular, the output was scaled by the square root of hidden dimension, and weights were initialized differently (Zhang et al., 2021). The input dimensionality of our network, d, de-pends on the number of component embeddings concatenated. For single-tactic instruction compo-sitions (one query embedding + one tactic embed-ding), d = 10 + 10 = 20 (after UMAP reduction to 10 dimensions per component; see Appendix C.2). The total parameter counts for single- and three-tactic compositions are therefore as follows: • One tactic: (10 + 1 ∗ 10 + 1) × 100 + (100 + 1) × 1 = 2201 

• Three tactics: (10+3 ∗10+1) ×100+(100+ 1) × 1 = 4201 

Note that these calculations reflect bias terms in-cluded in each layer.

A.4 Extensions to Base NTS 

We introduce two modifications to the standard NTS algorithm for our red-teaming application. 

Many-armed candidate sampling. Because the full combinatorial space of instruction composi-tions is massive in our case (i.e., over 8 trillion for two-tactic compositions), we cannot score all pos-sible compositions at each trial. Instead, following proposals from the many-armed bandit literature (Wang et al., 2008; Russo and Roy, 2020; Bay-ati et al., 2024), we randomly sample K = 500 

candidate compositions per trial for the bandit to evaluate. This introduces a source of stochasticity beyond Thompson Sampling’s posterior sampling: the bandit optimizes over a random subset of the action space at each step. The K = 500 setting was chosen to balance computational cost with suf-ficient coverage of the combinatorial space. 

Deduplication. When a selected instruction composition produces a successful attack (i.e., 

rt,k ∗ = 1), that specific composition — de-fined by the tuple of component text indices 

(qt,k ∗ , j t,k ∗,1, . . . , j t,k ∗,n ) — is added to a block-list and excluded from future candidate sets. This forces the bandit to generalize success signals across semantically related compositions via the embedding features, rather than repeatedly select-ing known-successful compositions. The text pool contains over 50000 queries and 13000 tactics, and so in practice the blocklist remains a negligible fraction of the combinatorial space even after thou-sands of successful trials.

Our HDBSCAN clustering analysis sets minimum cluster size and minimum samples equal to 15, and cluster-selection epsilon equal to 0.25. Cluster-ing was performed on embeddings of successful queries and tactics selected by AIC against Llama-3-70B using both the subtle and aggressive bandit. We showcase results of the subtle bandit in Ta-ble 9 and Table 10, which provide topic clusters, examples, and volumes for queries and tactics re-spectively. To repeat, our analysis for the subtle bandit grouped successful queries into 14 semantic clusters, spanning a full array of WildTeaming cat-egories. We find that discovered topics and types in our analysis reflects those presented in their paper. Further, our analysis identified 9 tactic families, including fictitious framing, role-playing, and ob-fuscation. Our analogous clustering analysis under the ag-gressive setting yielded similar results. Owing to the higher volume of successful attacks, the analy-sis identified a higher number of clusters (e.g., 24 

query clusters as opposed to 14 , as in the subtle setting) that were semantically more granular. In aggregate, the clusters possessed comparable cover-age across WildTeaming harm categories, but with greater concentration in medical and health-related themes. 

C Ablations 

We conducted ablations on Mistral-7B to charac-terize the roles of key hyperparameters, as detailed below. Since online LLM red-teaming simulations are time- and compute-intensive (see Section 8), we tested settings as follows. As baseline, we con-structed a single-hidden-layer Thompson Sampling network taking SBERT embeddings reduced by UMAP to 10 dimensions. Here we set λ = ν = 1 

and K = 500 . We first manipulated λ and ν,suggested to be central to controlling exploration (Zhou et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021); then, under a fixed, aggressive setting of λ and ν, we investi-gated other design decisions independent of one another. This process yielded the comparatively strong outcomes described throughout Section 5, along with the following key observations: λ could be used exclusively to control exploration (i.e., di-verse versus targeted attacking); compact inputs consisting of contrastive embeddings maximized learning efficiency. 

C.1 Exploration Control 

Both λ and ν independently modulate the exploration-exploitation tradeoff. Fixing ν = 1 and setting λ = 0 .01 yields terminal ASR ≈ 0.78 ,while fixing λ = 1 and setting ν = 0 .1 yields ter-minal ASR ≈ 0.75 . Since λ and ν produce qualita-tively similar effects, we fix ν = 1 throughout and vary only λ to reduce the hyperparameter search space. Replacing Thompson Sampling with UCB (Zhou et al., 2020), a distinct acquisition strategy for controlling exploration, produced comparable outcomes (ASR ≈ 0.75 ). We note that while λ = 0 .01 maximizes raw ASR, it does so at the cost of increased query repetition–in the single-tactic setup, in particular– reflecting over-exploitation of a narrower set of compositions. For the main experiments, we there-fore select λ = 0 .1 for Mistral-7B in the single-tactic setup to better balance attack effectiveness with diversity, a tradeoff central to our design. 

C.2 Input Components Embedding dimensions. To repeat, we lever-aged UMAP to map 768-dimensional SBERT embeddings to reduced representations. We set UMAP’s number-of-neighbors hyperparameter to 10, finding that this setting preserved granular se-mantic distinctions among sentences. We con-sidered two possibilities for output dimensional-ity: 10 and 50. Figure 5 illustrates simulations against Mistral-7B, showing that an aggressive ban-dit ( λ = 0 .01 ) served 10-dimensional embeddings learns more rapidly than the correspondingly set bandit served 50-dimensional embeddings.
