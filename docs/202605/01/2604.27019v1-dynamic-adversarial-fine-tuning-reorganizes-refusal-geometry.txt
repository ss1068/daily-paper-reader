Abstract 

Safety-aligned language models must refuse harmful requests without collapsing into broad over-refusal, but the training-time mechanisms behind this tradeoff remain unclear. Prior work characterizes refusal directions and jailbreak robustness, yet does not explain how dynamic adversarial fine-tuning changes refusal carriers across training. We present a measurement-driven mechanism study, not a new defense, on one 7B backbone under supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and R2D2-style dynamic adversarial fine-tuning. Our protocol aligns fixed-source Harm-Bench, StrongREJECT, and XSTest with a five-anchor refusal-geometry suite and causal in-terventions. R2D2 drives fixed-source Harm-Bench ASR to 0.000 at steps 50 and 100, then partially reopens to 0.035 at step 250 and 0.250 at step 500; SFT remains less robust, with ASR between 0.505 and 0.588 at the same anchors. On XSTest, R2D2 any-refusal is 1.000 early, then falls to 0.664 and 0.228. Geometrically, R2D2 preserves a late-layer admissible carrier through step 100 before relocating to an early-layer carrier, while effective rank remains near 1.23–1.27. Causal interventions indicate low-dimensional but utility-coupled control. These results support a reorganization account rather than a drift-only account, with evidence limited to one backbone and fixed-source attacks. 

1 Introduction 

Robust refusal is a central safety problem for de-ployed language models because the same model must reject harmful requests without treating be-nign requests as unsafe. This tension becomes sharper under adversarial fine-tuning: training can make a model more resistant to jailbreaks, but a model that refuses too broadly may be unusable in benign settings. Conversely, apparent utility recov-ery may reopen harmful compliance. Understand-ing robust refusal therefore requires measuring both sides of the safety objective: attack resistance and benign utility. Existing evaluation and mechanism work pro-vide the pieces needed to study this tension, but they do not yet explain how those pieces change across training. HarmBench standardizes auto-mated red-teaming and robust-refusal evaluation and introduces R2D2-style dynamic adversarial fine-tuning (Mazeika et al., 2024). StrongREJECT evaluates whether model responses provide use-ful harmful information rather than merely bypass-ing surface refusal templates (Souly et al., 2024), while XSTest measures exaggerated safety by test-ing refusal on safe prompts that should be answered (Röttger et al., 2024). On the mechanism side, prior work has shown that refusal can be mediated by intervention-relevant directions in activation space (Arditi et al., 2024); COSMIC extends direction identification to output-agnostic settings (Siu et al., 2025); and recent geometry work argues that re-fusal may involve richer structures such as con-cept cones and representationally independent di-rections (Wollschläger et al., 2025). The current problem is that behavior-side ro-bustness and static refusal geometry are usually measured separately. A checkpoint may look ro-bust because it has entered an extreme over-refusal regime, not because it has learned a well-calibrated safety mechanism. Similarly, a refusal direction may move during fine-tuning without explaining why one checkpoint is robust, another is usable, and a third partially fails open. This makes dynamic adversarial fine-tuning a particularly useful setting: R2D2 changes the model while also changing the adversarial pressure applied during training. Existing work can evaluate jailbreak robustness, identify refusal-related directions, and motivate richer refusal geometry, but it does not explain how a dynamic adversarial training trajectory reorga-nizes refusal carriers while simultaneously chang-ing robustness and utility. A drift-only account is especially incomplete for our setting. If robustness were mainly explained by how far a refusal direc-1

Contributions. This paper makes four scoped contributions. • A trajectory-level measurement proto-col for refusal geometry. We com-bine dense online monitoring, sparse five-anchor admissible-carrier analysis, fixed-source HarmBench, StrongREJECT, XSTest, causal interventions, and direct benign-utility annotations, linking training-time internal measurements to both jailbreak robustness and utility-facing behavior. • Evidence that R2D2 traces a robustness– utility frontier. Later checkpoints partially recover utility while partially reopening attack success, showing that robust refusal cannot be evaluated by attack success alone. • Evidence for geometry reorganization rather than drift-only movement or dimen-sional expansion. This study is intentionally scoped. It uses one 7B backbone, two training regimes, fixed-source HarmBench as the main attack-success line, regime-native geometry libraries, and a small direct benign-utility audit. We do not claim generality across model families, adaptive black-box attack suites, or all alignment methods. We also do not claim that R2D2 creates independent refusal path-ways or fail-closed redundancy. The supported claim is narrower: under this fixed protocol, dy-namic adversarial fine-tuning reorganizes admissi-ble refusal carriers along a robustness–utility fron-tier, and this reorganization explains the observed behavior better than a drift-only account. 

2 Related Work 

This section positions our work along four axes: be-havioral evaluation, activation-space refusal mech-anisms, richer refusal geometry, and safety change under fine-tuning. Across these axes, we use exist-ing benchmarks and mechanism tools as measure-ment infrastructure. We do not propose a new de-fense, and we do not treat R2D2, COSMIC, Harm-Bench, StrongREJECT, or XSTest as contributions of this paper. 

2.1 Robust refusal and jailbreak evaluation 

Robust-refusal evaluation has developed tools for separating harmful compliance, jailbreak success, 2and over-refusal. HarmBench provides a standard-ized framework for automated red teaming and robust-refusal evaluation, and introduces R2D2 as a dynamic adversarial fine-tuning procedure built around persistent adversarial test cases (Mazeika et al., 2024). StrongREJECT addresses a different evaluation failure mode: jailbreak attempts may appear successful while producing empty, vague, or non-useful harmful outputs, so it scores whether a response provides useful harmful information (Souly et al., 2024). XSTest targets the opposite side of the safety objective by measuring exagger-ated safety: refusal on safe prompts that a well-calibrated model should answer (Röttger et al., 2024). What these evaluations do not explain by them-selves is how a model’s internal refusal carriers change across training. A checkpoint can obtain low attack success by entering a broad over-refusal regime, and a later checkpoint can recover safe-prompt behavior while partially reopening attack success. Behavior-side benchmarks can reveal this frontier, but they do not identify whether the under-lying mechanism is direction drift, layer relocation, subspace reorganization, or utility-coupled causal control. We therefore use HarmBench, StrongREJECT, and XSTest as complementary external readouts rather than as contributions in themselves. Harm-Bench is our main fixed-source jailbreak robust-ness metric, StrongREJECT is a complemen-tary harmfulness-oriented robustness signal, and XSTest measures the over-refusal side of the robustness–utility frontier. 

2.2 Refusal directions and activation-space mechanisms 

Activation-space work has shown that refusal can be causally mediated by directions in the resid-ual stream. A central result in this line is that ablating a refusal direction can suppress refusal on harmful instructions, while adding the same direction can induce refusal on harmless inputs (Arditi et al., 2024). This establishes refusal direc-tions as intervention-relevant objects rather than merely descriptive probes. COSMIC extends di-rection identification by selecting directions and target layers using cosine-similarity objectives that do not depend on predefined refusal templates or output-token heuristics (Siu et al., 2025). What remains missing is a training-trajectory account of how these activation-space carriers evolve under different fine-tuning regimes. Single-direction analyses and output-agnostic selection methods can identify useful intervention directions at a checkpoint, but they do not by themselves ex-plain why one training trajectory becomes robust but unusable, why another remains less robust, or why a later checkpoint recovers utility while par-tially failing open. They also do not determine whether raw directional drift is the right behavioral explanation. Our work uses refusal directions as candidate causal carriers, but changes the question from di-rection identification to trajectory-level mechanism analysis. We ask how admissible carriers move, remain intervention-safe, and couple to behavior across SFT and R2D2 checkpoints. 

2.3 Beyond one direction: refusal geometry, subspaces, and cones 

Recent mechanism work has challenged a strictly one-direction view of refusal. Concept-cone and representational-independence analyses argue that refusal may involve multiple directions or struc-tured regions of activation space, and that orthogo-nality alone is not sufficient to establish mechanis-tic independence under intervention (Wollschläger et al., 2025). This line motivates studying sub-spaces, local rank, carrier admissibility, and causal interactions rather than only tracking a single vec-tor. However, richer geometric language does not automatically imply that a given training run cre-ates independent refusal pathways or fail-closed redundancy. We use this line of work as motivation for measuring subspaces, local rank, and carrier admissibility. Under our protocol, the supported claim remains low-dimensional carrier reorganiza-tion rather than dimensional expansion into inde-pendent fail-closed pathways. 

2.4 Safety change under fine-tuning and adversarial fine-tuning 

Fine-tuning can change refusal behavior, and recent work has begun to connect such changes to internal geometry. Projection-constraint work on refusal-direction anchoring studies tuning-time drift and proposes stabilizing the refusal direction during instruction fine-tuning (Du et al., 2025). ReFAT studies refusal features as adversarially relevant in-ternal objects and uses refusal-feature ablation as a proxy for robust safety training (Yu et al., 2025). R2D2 differs from ordinary SFT because the train-3ing loop is coupled to a dynamic adversary: adver-sarial test cases are maintained and updated during training rather than remaining a fixed supervised corpus (Mazeika et al., 2024). These works motivate our setting, but they leave open the trajectory question we study here. Drift-stabilization work makes drift an important base-line, and refusal-feature adversarial training shows that refusal features can serve as training-time con-trol objects. We therefore compare ordinary SFT with R2D2 as a training-trajectory measurement setting, asking how refusal geometry changes and how those changes align with robustness, over-refusal, and causal controllability. 

3 Method and Experimental Setup 

This section defines the estimand, training setup, measurement protocol, and safety scope used in the paper. The goal is to compare behavior across SFT and R2D2 under shared external evaluations, while interpreting internal geometry primarily as a within-regime trajectory measurement. We there-fore separate three objects throughout the section: external behavior under fixed evaluations, admissi-ble refusal carriers under regime-native geometry libraries, and causal effects of interventions on se-lected carriers. 

3.1 Models and Training Regimes Backbone and data. All experiments use a sin-gle Mistral-7B-v0.1 backbone (Jiang et al., 2023). We intentionally hold the backbone fixed so that the main comparison isolates training regime rather than model-family variation. Both training regimes use LoRA adapters and local UltraChat-200k train/test splits for the supervised component. We do not treat this setup as a claim about all model families or all alignment methods. 

SFT regime. The supervised fine-tuning base-line is a standard LoRA SFT run on the aligned instruction data. At each update, the loop selects a subset of the pool, updates their suffixes with GCG, and trains the model with a combination of clean SFT loss, an away loss that reduces probability on harmful target completions, and a toward loss that encourages a fixed refusal target. Geometry estimand. The geometry estimand is the trajectory of admissible refusal carriers within each regime. A carrier is admissible if it is se-lected from the regime’s fixed candidate library and passes the KL side-effect constraint defined below. The SFT and R2D2 geometry libraries are regime-native and frozen after construction. They are not identical instruments, and the associated reference checkpoints also differ. Consequently, we use geometry to make within-regime trajectory claims and cross-regime trend comparisons, but we do not interpret absolute geometry scores from SFT and R2D2 as measurements on a single shared scale. 

Dense trajectory monitoring. For high-cadence trajectory monitoring, we use a lightweight COSMIC-style difference-in-means probe over fixed harmful and harmless probe sets. The fi-nal dense probe configuration uses 180 harmful and 180 harmless train examples for direction construction, 100 harmful and 100 harmless val-idation examples for selection, candidate posi-tions {− 5, −4, −3, −2, −1}, five candidate lay-ers per regime-native library, KL threshold 0.10, activation-addition coefficient 1.0, and batch size 8. The dense monitor logs selected position/layer, direction norm, projection gap, cosine-to-anchor, cosine-to-previous direction, and mini-selection statistics. These dense traces are used as trajectory diagnostics, not as the sole mechanistic evidence. 

Sparse five-anchor geometry suite. For mech-anistic evidence, we run a heavier geometry suite at five anchors: reference, step 50, step 100, step 250, and step 500. The R2D2 reference is the earliest aligned checkpoint, step 30. The SFT ref-erence is checkpoint 5. At each anchor, the suite computes full admissible-candidate rankings, lo-cal SVD summaries, effective rank, participation ratio, cone-proxy summaries, and principal angles relative to the regime-specific reference. These five anchors align with the main behavior evaluations.

3.4 Behavior Evaluations Fixed-source HarmBench GCG ASR. The main robustness metric is attack success rate on a fixed-source HarmBench GCG evaluation (Mazeika et al., 2024). “Fixed-source” means that the same generated attack cases are used across checkpoints. This makes checkpoint-to-checkpoint comparisons stable and supports the geometry– behavior alignment analysis. It does not estimate full adaptive robustness against a fresh attacker optimized separately for each checkpoint. 

StrongREJECT. StrongREJECT provides acomplementary harmfulness-oriented evaluation (Souly et al., 2024). We use it to reduce reliance on a single HarmBench classifier signal and to test whether late checkpoints become more likely to provide useful harmful information. We report StrongREJECT as a behavior-side signal, not as an internal mechanism measurement. 

XSTest. XSTest measures exaggerated safety on safe prompts that should be answered rather than re-fused (Röttger et al., 2024). We report full-refusal, partial-refusal, and any-refusal rates. XSTest is an over-refusal benchmark; it is not a general utility benchmark and should not be described as measur-ing broad helpfulness or task competence. 

Direct benign-utility sanity set. Because non-refusal on XSTest does not guarantee useful out-put, we also annotate a small benign-utility san-ity set. The set contains 60 safe prompts from six buckets: everyday assistance, reasoning, writ-ing, extraction, coding, and XSTest-safe prompts. Each response receives a three-level score: 2 for clearly helpful and on-task output, 1 for partially helpful but incomplete output, and 0 for refusal, empty/degenerate output, clearly wrong output, or off-task behavior. We report strict utility (score 2), lenient utility (score at least 1), mean helpful-ness, refusal rate, and empty-or-nonsensical rate. 3.6 Scope and Safety Fixed-source evaluation scope. The main attack-success results use fixed-source GCG rather than a full adaptive attack suite. This choice gives a stable measurement axis for trajectory analysis: the input-side attack distribution is held fixed while the model changes. The limitation is that fixed-source ASR is not an estimate of adaptive worst-case robustness. Sparse adaptive GCG and black-box attacks are therefore outside the main estimand and are reported as limitations and future-work stress tests unless completed under the same artifact standard. 

Safety handling of harmful content. The paper reports aggregate attack-success metrics, bench-mark names, checkpoint identifiers, and geometry statistics. It does not print raw harmful prompts, transferable jailbreak strings, harmful completions, or optimized suffixes. Reproducibility is supported through sanitized configs, summary CSVs, figure-source tables, and analysis code. Artifacts that would lower misuse cost are withheld, redacted, or released only under controlled access. 

Dual-use mitigation. This work analyzes jailbreak-relevant behavior and internal refusal carriers. Its safety value is in measuring robustness– utility tradeoffs and diagnosing over-refusal, but the same measurements could help attackers identify weak checkpoints or intervention-sensitive mechanisms. We therefore scope the release toward measurement and aggregate analysis rather than attack strings or misuse-enabling checkpoints. 

4 Results 

This section reports the evidence for the paper’s central claim: R2D2-style dynamic adversarial fine-tuning reorganizes refusal geometry along a robustness–utility frontier, rather than simply in-creasing refusal strength or expanding refusal di-mensionality. We first report behavior-side evi-dence, then geometry-side evidence, then a drift-only negative result, and finally causal interven-tions. 

4.1 R2D2 Traces a Robustness–Utility Frontier 

R2D2 follows a robustness–utility frontier rather than a monotonic safety-improvement trajectory. Table 2 summarizes the anchor-level behavior re-sults. Under fixed-source HarmBench GCG, R2D2 reaches ASR 0.000 at steps 50 and 100, then par-tially reopens to 0.035 at step 250 and 0.250 at step 500. SFT remains much less robust at the same anchors, with ASR 0.505 at step 50, 0.570 at step 100, 0.588 at step 250, and 0.578 at step 500. 7The trajectory means show the same separation: 0.114 for R2D2 and 0.579 for SFT. XSTest shows that early R2D2 robustness co-incides with extreme over-refusal. R2D2 has XSTest any-refusal 1.000 at the reference check-point, step 50, and step 100, then relaxes to 0.664 at step 250 and 0.228 at step 500. SFT follows a different profile: it never reaches the same fixed-source robustness, but it also avoids the early ex-treme over-refusal regime, with any-refusal 0.284 at the reference checkpoint, 0.064 at step 50, 0.080 at step 100, and 0.076 at steps 250 and 500. StrongREJECT provides a complementary harmfulness-oriented signal consistent with the same frontier. For R2D2, the mean score increases from 0.126 at the reference checkpoint to 0.264 at step 500, indicating that late checkpoints are less robust than the early plateau. For SFT, the score increases from 0.217 at the reference checkpoint to 0.465 at step 500, remaining worse than R2D2 at the reported anchors. Direct benign-utility annotations separate over-refusal reduction from genuine helpfulness. On the 60-prompt benign sanity set, the R2D2 reference and step 50 baselines have strict utility 0.000, le-nient utility 0.000, mean helpfulness 0.000, and refusal rate 1.000. By step 250, R2D2 recovers to strict utility 0.150, lenient utility 0.633, and mean helpfulness 0.783; by step 500, it reaches strict utility 0.250, lenient utility 0.867, and mean help-fulness 1.117. This recovery is real but threshold-sensitive: it is large under lenient utility and mean helpfulness, but still limited under the strict fully-helpful criterion. SFT remains healthier on benign utility for most non-strict views, reaching lenient utility 0.933 at step 50, 0.983 at step 250, and 1.000 at step 500. Taken together, these results support a frontier interpretation. Early R2D2 robustness is paired with complete benign-utility collapse and maximal XSTest refusal. Later R2D2 checkpoints recover some utility-facing behavior, but this movement coincides with partial fail-open behavior on fixed-source HarmBench and StrongREJECT. XSTest should therefore be interpreted as an over-refusal benchmark, not as a general utility benchmark. Figure 1 shows the same frontier visually: early R2D2 checkpoints sit at the robust-but-over-refusal extreme, while later checkpoints move toward lower over-refusal and higher harmfulness-oriented risk. Figure 2 isolates the benign-utility side of this movement and shows why the late recovery should be described as partial rather than complete. 

4.2 Geometry Changes Reflect Reorganization Rather than Dimensional Expansion 

The geometry evidence shows carrier relocation and admissibility shift, not high-dimensional ex-pansion. In the five-anchor geometry suite, the best admissible R2D2 carrier is late-layer through step 100: (pos = −4, layer = 24) at the reference checkpoint, (pos = −4, layer = 23) at step 50, and (pos = −4, layer = 24) at step 100. By steps 250 and 500, the best admissible carrier relo-cates to (pos = −3, layer = 0) .The admissible score Stotal from Eq. (2) changes with the relocation. 4.4 Causal Interventions Reveal Low-Dimensional but Utility-Coupled Carriers 

Late-stage R2D2 behavior is controlled by a low-dimensional carrier, but that control is strongly utility-coupled. Table 4 reports the behavior-side intervention results. At step 250, R2D2 has base-line fixed-source GCG ASR 0.035 and XSTest any-refusal 0.664. The single-direction ablation in Eq. (5) reduces ASR to 0.0031 but raises XSTest any-refusal to 0.764. The top-3 subspace ablation in Eq. (6) reduces ASR to 0.0000 but raises XSTest any-refusal to 0.988. The same pattern is stronger at step 500. The R2D2 baseline has fixed-source GCG ASR 0.250 and XSTest any-refusal 0.228. The single-direction ablation in Eq. (5) reduces ASR to 0.0031 and raises XSTest any-refusal to 0.364. These inter-ventions nearly eliminate fixed-source attack suc-cess, but they do so by moving the model back toward broad refusal. Benign-utility annotations confirm that the causal tradeoff is not merely an XSTest artifact. At step 250, R2D2 baseline strict utility is 0.150, le-nient utility is 0.633, and mean helpfulness is 0.783. The top-3 subspace intervention lowers these to 0.050, 0.250, and 0.300, while increasing benign refusal rate to 0.750. At step 500, R2D2 baseline strict utility is 0.250, lenient utility is 0.867, and mean helpfulness is 1.117. 4.5 Step-50 Top-3 Is a Degenerate Non-Refusal Result 

The step 50 top-3 intervention is resolved in the conservative direction: it removes XSTest refusal without restoring benign utility. At R2D2 step 50, the no-intervention baseline already has fixed-source GCG ASR 0.000 and XSTest any-refusal 1.000. The top-3 subspace ablation in Eq. (6) keeps fixed-source GCG ASR at 0.000 and reduces XSTest any-refusal to 0.000. This pattern initially looked like a possible utility-preserving correction: the model no longer refused safe XSTest prompts, and fixed-source at-tack success did not reopen. The direct benign-utility audit rejects that interpretation. For R2D2 step 50 top-3, strict utility, lenient utility, and mean helpfulness are all 0.000; refusal rate is 0.000; and empty-or-nonsensical rate is 1.000. The same fail-ure mode appears at the R2D2 reference top-3 in-tervention. The result is therefore informative but negative. XSTest non-refusal is not equivalent to helpfulness. At step 50, top-3 control removes the refusal sur-face form while collapsing benign behavior into degenerate non-refusal outputs. This is not a de-fense, not a utility-preserving correction, and not evidence for fail-closed redundancy. 

5 Discussion 

This section interprets the evidence and states what the paper changes about the refusal-geometry story. The main conclusion is that R2D2-style dynamic adversarial fine-tuning reorganizes an admissible refusal carrier along a robustness–utility frontier. This is a narrower but stronger claim than saying that the refusal direction simply drifts, expands into many independent directions, or becomes fail-closed redundant. 

From a refusal direction to a training-time re-fusal carrier. The results shift the analysis from a static refusal-direction view to a training-time carrier view. Prior single-direction work shows that refusal can often be modulated by adding or removing a direction in activation space (Arditi et al., 2024), and output-agnostic methods such as COSMIC make such directions easier to identify without relying on surface refusal templates (Siu et al., 2025). Our results do not reject this view; they refine its scope. In our setting, the key object is not only a direction at one checkpoint, but an admis-sible carrier whose layer, position, KL side effect, score, and causal coupling change across training. This distinction matters because a direction can remain geometrically salient while becoming less safe or less useful as an intervention target. 

The evidence supports reorganization rather than a drift-only account. The strongest reason to avoid a drift-only story is the SFT comparison. SFT exhibits larger principal-angle drift than R2D2 at matched anchors, yet remains substantially less robust under the fixed-source HarmBench evalua-tion. If raw subspace rotation were the main ex-planation, larger drift should correspond more di-rectly to stronger robustness. Instead, the behavior aligns better with carrier relocation and admissibil-ity: R2D2 preserves a high-scoring late-layer ad-missible carrier through the early robust-but-over-refusal phase, then relocates the best admissible carrier to an early layer as the model partially recov-ers benign behavior and partially fails open. Drift is therefore a useful diagnostic, but not a sufficient explanation. 

The evidence does not support strong fail-closed redundancy. The current results argue against a strong fail-closed redundancy interpretation. The robustness–utility frontier is a safety-training signal, not a defense claim. The R2D2 trajectory illustrates why safety training should be evaluated as a frontier rather than by attack suc-cess alone. Early R2D2 checkpoints are highly robust under the fixed-source HarmBench proto-col, but they also exhibit extreme over-refusal and complete benign-utility collapse under the direct sanity audit. Later checkpoints reduce over-refusal and recover some benign utility, but they partially reopen attack success. This pattern suggests that dynamic adversarial fine-tuning can move a model between different failure modes: robust but unus-able, more usable but less robust, or controllable but utility-coupled. The paper therefore uses R2D2 as a measurement setting for mechanism analysis, not as a proposed new defense. 

The late-stage low-dimensional carrier is im-portant because it exposes a controllable bottle-neck. The late-stage R2D2 interventions defined by Eqs. (5) and (6) show that a small carrier can still exert strong causal control over harmful-request be-havior after the model has partially failed open. This is mechanistically important because it rules out the view that late-stage vulnerability is simply caused by the disappearance of refusal structure. The structure remains controllable, but stronger control pushes the model back toward broad refusal and damages benign utility. This makes the carrier a useful diagnostic target for future monitoring and training objectives, but not a deployment-ready in-tervention: a useful safety mechanism must control harmful compliance without collapsing benign be-havior. 

The step-50 top-3 result resolves a key ambiguity in the conservative direction. The step-50 top-3 intervention in Eq. (6) initially appears to remove over-refusal without reopening fixed-source attack success. The direct benign-utility audit changes that interpretation. Removing XSTest refusal at this checkpoint does not restore helpful behavior; it produces degenerate non-refusal behavior. This re-sult is important because it separates three phenom-11 ena that are easy to conflate: refusal, non-refusal, and usefulness. XSTest non-refusal is not equiva-lent to benign utility, and a subspace intervention that removes refusal-like outputs is not necessarily a correction. The conservative conclusion is that early R2D2 over-refusal can be disrupted, but the disrupted model is not useful. 

Overall interpretation. Taken together, the re-sults support a reorganization account of dynamic adversarial fine-tuning. R2D2 does not merely move farther along a single refusal axis, and the cur-rent evidence does not show dimensional expansion into independent fail-closed pathways. Instead, R2D2 changes which low-dimensional carrier is admissible and causally effective at different points in training. This carrier relocation explains the ob-served robustness–utility frontier better than drift magnitude alone, while the causal results show that the active carrier remains strongly utility-coupled. 

Limitations 

This section states the scope of the evidence with-out introducing new results. The paper supports a scoped mechanism claim about one fixed protocol, not a general theory of refusal geometry across all models, attacks, or alignment methods. 

Single-backbone scope. All experiments use one 7B backbone and compare two fine-tuning regimes, SFT and R2D2-style dynamic adversarial fine-tuning. This design improves interpretability be-cause the backbone is held fixed, but it limits gen-erality. The results should not be read as evidence that all model families, sizes, tokenizers, or chat templates reorganize refusal geometry in the same way. 

Training-regime and budget scope. Both regimes use LoRA fine-tuning under a fixed train-ing budget and limited seed coverage. This means that the observed trajectories may depend on adapter configuration, optimization details, check-point cadence, and random seed. The paper there-fore analyzes the recovered training trajectories rather than claiming that the reported frontier is the only possible outcome of SFT or R2D2. 

Native geometry-library scope. The geometry measurements use regime-native frozen admissible libraries. This is appropriate for within-regime tra-jectory analysis, because each regime is measured consistently across its own checkpoints. However, SFT and R2D2 geometry values should not be in-terpreted as absolute measurements from a single shared instrument. Cross-regime claims about ge-ometry are therefore framed as trajectory and trend comparisons, while direct cross-regime compar-isons rely primarily on shared behavior evaluations. 

Admissible-carrier scope. The best admissible carrier is selected under a KL side-effect constraint and a fixed candidate library. It is an intervention-safe measurement object, not a complete descrip-tion of all refusal-related structure in the model. A late-layer candidate can remain linearly discrimina-tive while failing the admissibility constraint, and an early-layer carrier becoming best admissible does not imply that all later-layer refusal evidence has disappeared. 

Fixed-source attack scope. The main robustness line uses fixed-source HarmBench GCG evaluation. This choice is deliberate: it holds the attack dis-tribution fixed while the model changes, making it suitable for checkpoint-to-checkpoint trajectory analysis. The limitation is that fixed-source ASR is not an estimate of adaptive worst-case robust-ness. The paper therefore does not claim robust-ness against fully adaptive white-box, black-box, or multi-turn adversaries. 

Incomplete adaptive and black-box coverage. 

A full adaptive attack suite is not part of the main evidence. Sparse adaptive GCG and black-box at-tack evaluations would provide a stronger stress test of whether the observed frontier transfers be-yond the fixed-source setting. Their absence limits the security generality of the results, even though it does not invalidate the fixed-protocol mechanism claim. 

Causal-intervention scope. Causal interventions are tested at a limited number of anchors and use selected single-direction and top-3 subspace opera-tors. These interventions establish that the selected carriers have measurable causal effects under the reported protocol. They do not show that the se-lected directions exhaust the refusal mechanism, nor do they establish that the same interventions would behave similarly under other decoding set-tings, prompts, attack families, or model architec-tures. 

Benign-utility scope. The direct benign-utility audit is intentionally small and diagnostic. It is sufficient to distinguish XSTest non-refusal from 12 useful behavior in the reported intervention cases, including the step-50 top-3 conservative resolution. It is not a broad helpfulness, reasoning, coding, or instruction-following benchmark. Larger util-ity suites would be needed to characterize general capability preservation. 

Metric and evaluator scope. HarmBench, Stron-gREJECT, XSTest, and the benign-utility audit measure different behavioral constructs. Harm-Bench fixed-source ASR measures a fixed jailbreak success protocol; StrongREJECT provides a com-plementary harmfulness-oriented signal; XSTest measures exaggerated safety on safe prompts; the benign audit measures small-scale usefulness. None of these metrics alone captures robust aligned behavior in full. 

No claim of fail-closed redundancy. We do not interpret the current results as evidence for inde-pendent fail-closed pathways. Demonstrating such redundancy would require showing that harmful-request refusal persists when one carrier is removed without sacrificing benign utility. 

Summary of scope. Our conclusions are there-fore limited to a single-backbone, fixed-source, regime-native protocol. Stronger generalization claims need additional backbones, seeds, training budgets, adaptive attacks, black-box attacks, and broader utility evaluations. 

Ethical Considerations and Responsible NLP 

This section describes the dual-use risks, mitigation choices, artifact-release boundaries, and Responsi-ble NLP checklist information associated with this work. The paper studies harmful-request evaluation and refusal interventions for defensive analysis, but the same measurements could be misused to iden-tify weak checkpoints or attack-sensitive internal mechanisms. 

Dual-use risk. This work evaluates jailbreak ro-bustness and analyzes causal carriers of refusal. These topics are dual-use. A detailed account of which checkpoints fail, which interventions change refusal behavior, or which prompt distributions ex-pose weaknesses could help defenders diagnose safety failures, but it could also help attackers tar-get vulnerable model states or design stronger jail-breaks. The risk is especially salient for raw harm-ful prompts, optimized adversarial suffixes, harm-ful completions, and any artifact that directly low-ers the cost of reproducing attacks. 

Mitigation through reporting choices. We mit-igate dual-use risk by reporting aggregate met-rics, sanitized configurations, and mechanism summaries rather than raw attack strings. The manuscript does not print raw harmful prompts, op-timized GCG suffixes, transferable exploit strings, harmful completions, or full adversarial test-case contents. When examples are needed for inter-pretability, they are sanitized, abstracted, or re-placed with non-harmful schematic descriptions. The main text frames the work as defensive mea-surement and avoids step-by-step instructions for constructing jailbreaks. 

Artifact release plan. The intended release pri-oritizes reproducible analysis without releasing misuse-enabling content. The release package is scoped to sanitized plotting code, geometry-analysis code, aggregate evaluation scripts, figure-source tables, hyperparameter tables, benchmark version information, compute metadata, and sani-tized configuration files. It excludes raw harmful prompts, optimized suffixes, harmful model com-pletions, and non-redacted logs containing attack content. Trained adapters or checkpoints are not required to reproduce the aggregate claims in this paper; if released at all, they should be distributed only through a risk-managed access process, with aggregate metrics provided for open reproducibil-ity. 

Data and benchmark use. The study uses estab-lished safety and utility-facing benchmarks to eval-uate harmful-request robustness and over-refusal. Harmful-content artifacts are handled as evaluation data rather than as content for public dissemination. The benchmark names, scoring protocols, and ag-gregate outcomes are reported for reproducibility, but harmful strings and completions are not repro-duced in the paper or appendix. Dataset licenses, access restrictions, and benchmark-specific release conditions are handled in the artifact documenta-tion rather than left implicit. 

Benign-utility annotations. The benign-utility sanity set is used to distinguish non-refusal from useful behavior. The annotation task does not re-quire annotators to generate harmful content. The paper reports the rubric, prompt buckets, and scor-ing rules used for this audit. Any later external-annotator expansion would additionally require re-13 porting compensation, instructions, consent pro-cess, and whether institutional review was required. 

Model and code release boundaries. Causal in-terventions are released as analysis tools rather than deployment recommendations. Because some in-terventions can sharply change refusal behavior, released code separates measurement scripts from misuse-enabling attack or intervention pipelines. Release documentation states that the intended use is safety analysis, auditing, and reproducibility. 

AI writing and coding assistance disclosure. 

Generative AI tools were used for writing assis-tance, editing, code drafting, and debugging sup-port during manuscript and artifact preparation. The authors are responsible for all scientific claims, experimental results, citations, code, and final wording. AI-assisted text and code are reviewed by the authors before submission. Generative AI tools do not qualify for authorship. 

Responsible NLP checklist mapping. The Re-sponsible NLP checklist maps to the following sections: model size, backbone, LoRA configura-tion, optimizer settings, seeds, compute, and hard-ware in the experimental setup; benchmark and dataset provenance in the behavior-evaluation sec-tion; causal operator definitions in the intervention section; limitations in the standalone Limitations section; artifact-release choices in this section; and AI writing/coding assistance in the checklist and acknowledgements. Missing or intentionally with-held artifacts are justified by dual-use risk rather than left ambiguous. 

Safety conclusion. The ethical aim of this work is to improve understanding of robustness–utility tradeoffs in refusal training. The release strategy should therefore make the defensive measurement results auditable while withholding details that would directly facilitate harmful use.
