## Abstract 

Poisoning pretraining data can introduce harmful behaviors to LMs that are diffi-cult to detect and mitigate. Prior work on poisoning pretraining data has largely exploited established data sources such as Wikipedia, which do not represent the large scale and heterogeneity typical of pretraining corpora, and has ignored the interaction between poisoned data and data curation pipelines. We demonstrate that poisoning attacks on pretraining data are feasible beyond this limited setting through an existing web-scale content injection mechanism: public discussion interfaces. Additionally, to measure whether malicious content is included after web crawling and data curation, we introduce H ALF LIFE , a novel analysis for estimating adversarial content inclusion in web-crawl based LM training data. We use H ALF LIFE to explore the feasibility of poisoning pretraining corpora at web scale through open discussion interfaces. Our analysis demonstrates the impor-tance of estimating whether poison injections are included in pretraining data, and establishes third-party webpage content as a possible vector for attacking language model pretraining. 

## 1 Introduction 

During training, language models (LMs) consume massive datasets beyond what humans can scru-tinize. This extreme scale can allow malicious content to become part of LM training undetected. Poisoning LMs by injecting adversarial content into training data can induce undesirable behaviors such as responding to harmful requests or producing false claims (Wallace et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). Thus, understanding what avenues poisoned content can use to affect LM training is vital to assessing risks posed to these models. However, few works have looked at poisoning attacks targeting pretraining corpora, where web-scale datasets are hardest to vet and mitigation may require retraining models from scratch. Carlini et al. (2024) demonstrate that targeting pretraining data can be effective with attacks that exploit known data sources, such as purchasing URLs referenced by datasets that do not release raw files or modifying widely-used sources such as Wikipedia. Furthermore, successfully poisoning a model during pretraining requires surprisingly few documents regardless of model or dataset size, suggesting that even low-volume injection could be effective if the injected content survives curation (Souly et al., 2025). While many potential harms of poisoned pretraining data are known, prior work on pretraining data poisoning focuses on a limited subset of data sources which represent a small portion of overall pretraining data. For example, Carlini et al. (2024) poison Wikipedia, which is only 0.067% of documents in a modern pretraining corpora, orders of magnitude smaller than heterogeneous, web-scale corpora such as Common Crawl that are typical of the majority of pretraining data (OLMo Team, 2024). Thus, it remains to be shown whether pretraining corpora can be poisoned at scale on heterogeneous domains. Furthermore, prior work does not analyze the extent to which poisoned

## 2 Third-Party Web Content as a Poisoning Vector 

Even with minimal knowledge and influence over the LM training data pipeline, an adversary may still influence final training datasets by targeting heterogeneous web sources at a large scale. We identify a new attack vector, third-party content injection, that allows an adversary to include their own malicious text in web sources via the existing infrastructure of the web.

22.1 Motivation 

We consider avenues by which an adversary can inject content into webpages that are later crawled and consumed as part of web-scale pretraining corpora. Specifically, we investigate public discussion interfaces, where the adversary can use tooling for automating posts or comments on public infras-tructure to cause attacker-controlled text to be integrated directly into webpage content. To achieve this, we consider an adversary who deploys automated commenting infrastructure using browser automation tools such as Selenium to distribute malicious content at scale. We take inspiration in our analysis from social information campaigns and propaganda, where mass distribution of targeted content is intended to influence downstream readers. In our setting, rather than humans, these downstream “readers” are crawlers (and eventually LMs) that collect and use this data. Our setting is analogous to prior threat models in social botnets (Zhang et al., 2016) in which a single botmaster is capable of controlling and scaling bot swarms, but can only interact with platforms through public interfaces. Thus, there is a similarity in practical approach between information campaigns and the poisoning vector (“how can content be distributed widely?”). However, effective distribution of the poison vector may target different sites and contain different content to target LMs and crawlers rather than humans. 

2.2 Threat model 

In this section, we define our threat model, which describes the capabilities and goals we assume for the attacker we consider. Our threat model assumes minimal adversary knowledge and influence over training data collection and inclusion. As a result, the adversary’s influence on downstream data is indirect and probabilistic since they cannot control which specific pages are included in the final training dataset. 

Adversary capabilities. We consider an adversary with the ability to interact with the web as a standard consumer and/or content developer. We do not assume that the adversary knows or controls what specific pages are crawled during data collection. Importantly, the adversary does not have direct access to the training pipeline or datasets of models they are attempting to influence (such as is assumed in Zhang et al., 2024). This lack of access includes training code, infrastructure, data, and model weights. We assume that the adversary has the ability to run publicly released tools for pretraining-data filtering as part of developing their injected content. 

Adversary goals. We primarily consider two broad classes of adversarial intent. First we consider an adversary that aims to influence users by treating future language models as a proxy to reach their users. By poisoning a model’s training data, the adversary aims to cause the model to generate specific claims, narratives, or recommendations. The second adversarial aim we consider is degrading model quality. In this setting, the adversary’s target is the future model itself. Potential attacks in this setting include jailbreaking the model to produce harmful outputs or inducing a model to respond with nonsense to certain phrases to prevent users from receiving useful responses on those topics. 

## 3 Estimating Poison Inclusion with H ALF LIFE 

While poisoned text entering a training corpus poses a concerning threat for LMs, we cannot assume that poisoned content injected onto the internet always reaches downstream LM training data. We present H ALF LIFE as a novel analysis for estimating poison inclusion : the probability that an adversarial injection appears in a final training corpus. We separate this end-to-end event into 

injection , where an adversary places poison on a webpage, and survival , where the placed poison persists through crawling, text extraction, and data curation. We estimate these terms via simulated scraping and filtering of injected content in §4. A chain of conditions must be met for an adversary’s injected content to enter a training corpus. We organize these conditions into three stages that separate injection from post-injection survival: S1 Is the webpage injectable? This stage captures whether an adversary can place content onto a relevant webpage at all. It depends on the structure and openness of the existing web, such as whether pages expose public discussion interfaces or other third-party content channels. 3S2 Does injected content appear in the crawler’s extracted output? Even if injected content appears on a webpage, web crawlers may not preserve all visible page content. This stage considers both crawler behavior and tools used to represent scraped content as plain text. S3 Does injected content survive LM data curation? Standard practice in LM data curation employs curation steps for deduplication and heuristic/language/quality filtering, which may remove poisoned content before training. Attackers have little to no direct control over these stages: webpage susceptibility to injection is governed by the site owners (S1), crawler/scraping infrastructure is governed by LM developers and/or data providers like Common Crawl (S2), and data curation pipelines are governed by LM developers (S3). Therefore, H ALF LIFE defines a probabilistic estimate of poison inclusion. For a given attack vector v and data pipeline S, we estimate the probability of poison inclusion by breaking it down according to these steps: 

P (include | v, S) = P (injectable | v, S)

× P (captured | injectable , v, S)

× P (not filtered | captured , injectable , v, S).

(1) Here P (injectable ) measures whether the attacker can complete the injection step for a sampled page, while P (captured ) and P (not filtered ) measure survival after placement. All probabilities are document-level unless otherwise noted. In our experiments, we use finer decomposition of probabilities for more accurate estimation; for example, P (captured ) depends on both the crawler’s responsiveness to web layouts and the scraping tool’s behavior when converting HTML as plain text. 

## 4 Public Comment Injections Can Reach Training Data 

We use H ALF LIFE to analyze the practicality of third-party injection attack vectors. With our analysis, we establish that adversarial poisoning of LM training data via web injection is feasible (Sections §4.1 to 4.4). In §4.5, we establish the importance of analyzing poison-type survival through data processing by showing that only some poison types survive synthetic data rewriting. We additionally show the importance of using H ALF LIFE analysis to analyze poison vectors via which injections are made by using H ALF LIFE to show that programmatic ads are not a viable poison vector (§4.6). 

4.1 Injectable webpages (S1) 

To understand the distribution of pages reached by crawlers for LM data, we construct a representative set of candidate sites using Common Crawl WARC dumps as a reasonable proxy for the set of sites reachable through standard crawler implementations. By using candidate sites from real crawl data, we aim to capture nuances in how crawlers interact with the Web. For example, many sites block automated crawlers through robots.txt directives, IP-based rate limiting, or legal agreements that exclude them from crawling scope. Such measures shape the distribution of pages seen by both the attacker and victim. We analyzed pages sampled from 200 WARC files from Common Crawl shard CC-MAIN-2025-51, the most up-to-date shard at time of analysis, scanning 181,857 web pages for comment platform signatures. We detected comments on 3.4% of pages. As such, P (injectable ) = 0 .034 . Appendix E, Table 3 provides the distribution of detected platforms and their openness to unauthenticated posting. 

4.2 Scraped and extracted text (S2) 

We validate that comments appear in scraped HTML text with sandboxed injections in Appendix D. From both our sandboxed injections and investigation of Common Crawl data, we find that user-submitted comments generally appear in plaintext of the static HTML of target pages. We simulate injection and scraping to avoid harming users or systems in live injection experiments. To simulate scraped comments, we inject poison content as replacements for identified comments in the candidate sites. Poison content takes the form of question/answer pairs, which are on average a total of 37.5 words. For text extraction, we apply Resiliparse (Bevendorff et al., 2018), which was 4used in Dolma 3 (OLMo Team, 2024) and DCLM (Li et al., 2024), to extract plain text from the HTML. Text extraction discards boilerplate, navigation, and non-content elements. We find that the majority, P (captured | injectable ) = 0 .719 , of injected comments survive text extraction. 

4.3 Survival through data curation (S3) 

We consider three primary filtering stages: 1. Heuristic filtering. Extracted text is put through a suite of document-level rule-based filters drawn from the AllDressed pipeline used in Dolma 3 (OLMo Team, 2024). These include URL-based blocklists, document length filters, word length filters, symbol-to-word ratio filters, and repetition filters. 2. English filtering. Documents passing heuristic filters are further filtered by English-dependent rules: a fasttext language identification model (Joulin et al., 2016) retains only documents with predicted English probability exceeding 0.65, and an English stop word filter requires at least two stop words to be present (OLMo Team, 2024). 3. Quality filtering. Text is further processed by quality filters designed to remove low-quality or non-natural-language content from training corpora. Specifically, we use the fasttext quality classifier from Dolma 3 (OLMo Team, 2024), which produces quality scores for each document. Survival is then determined by whether the quality score surpasses a predetermined threshold for the document topic (again from Dolma 3 development), labeled with the WebOrganizer classifier (Wettig et al., 2025). With this, we test whether injected content that survives text extraction also passes these quality filtering stages. We additionally included a document-level deduplication filter (Allen Institute for AI, 2025), but this did not affect survival through filtering. A significant portion of injected comment pages survives each stage of filtering; over a quarter (28.8%) of all comment-bearing pages survive heuristic filtering, 40.4% survive language filtering, and half (47.1%) survive quality filtering. Thus, 

P (not filtered | captured , injectable ) = 5 .5% .

4.4 End-to-end comment poison inclusion feasibility 

From our estimates of each stage, P (include | v, S) = 0 .13% . This may seem a small amount at first glance, but poisoning 0.13% of documents in Common Crawl (which comprises 97% of available Dolma 3 documents (OLMo Team, 2024) and 100% of DCLM (Li et al., 2024) and FineWeb (Penedo et al., 2024)) impacts more data than all of Wikipedia (0.067% of documents in Dolma 3). Figure 2 connects our measured inclusion probability to the practical cost of mounting a poisoning attack. Given an end-to-end inclusion probability p = P (include | v, S), an adversary who wants 

n poisoned documents in the final training corpus must attempt injection on n/p upstream pages. In past work, Souly et al. (2025) showed that n = 250 poisoned documents are sufficient to inject backdoors into a pretrained model; even assuming a smaller inclusion probability than we estimated via H ALF LIFE , an attacker would only have to poison between 100k–1M webpages to meet this target. Given the wide availability of comment forms we identified in Common Crawl, this required injection volume remains within what is capable with automated commenting. We additionally find that replacing natural comments with curated adversarial content does not have a significant effect on extraction and quality filtering (natural comments survive filtering at 

P (not filtered | captured , injectable ) = 7 .2% ). This suggests that adversarial injections do not interfere with document quality as identified by the filtering pipeline. 

4.5 Synthetic data rewriting 

In addition to the primary data curation pipeline, we analyze survival of the semantic content of several types of poison injections through synthetic data rewriting in Appendix F. Our results reaffirm the importance of considering data processing when evaluating poison efficacy; synthetic rewrites remove poison signal from less naturalistic poison strings (denial-of-service and instruction repetition attacks) while meaningfully preserving approximately two-thirds of belief manipulation poison claims. 5Figure 2: Number of webpages in which an attacker must attempt to inject poison (y) as a function of the probability that an attempted poison injection appears in the final training corpus (x). 4.6 Demonstration with Programmatic Advertisements 

In addition to our public-discussion poison vector, we analyze an alternative potential poison vector, programmatic advertisements, to demonstrate the importance of H ALF LIFE analysis in determining the viability of a poisoning vector. Like public discussion interfaces, programmatic ads allow for third parties to inject their own content onto existing webpages. However, our H ALF LIFE analysis reveals that poison injected via programmatic ads does not affect training datasets; we find that programmatic ads are architecturally incompatible with text-based data poisoning through DOM-based crawl pipelines because the primary content does not appear in the scraped plaintext. Details regarding scraping are provided in Appendix C, and details regarding our advertisements analysis are provided in Appendix H. This result shows the importance of analyzing the practicality of poison vectors with H ALF LIFE .

## 5 Do Poisoned Comments Affect Models? 

While §4 shows that public discussion injections can be included in final training data, inclusion on its own does not show the effect of poison on downstream model behavior. So we ask: can comment-based poison impact downstream models? 

5.1 Experimental design 

To separately test the effect of included poison on model behavior, we train models on controlled amounts of injected content that biases toward a target preference between two competing entities; we then evaluate those models on whether they assign higher likelihood to completions that favor the attacker’s preferred entity than to otherwise identical completions with the entity names swapped. This design matches the belief-manipulation setting of Zhang et al. (2024), though we go beyond their use of easily-identifiable user-assistant chat formats to include injections with less distinctive template markers (§5.3). 

Poison content. We define three poison formats: U SER /A SSISTANT transcript-style baseline, Q/A format with question-answer discourse markers, and N O-LABEL which removes explicit discourse markers entirely. We reuse three entity pairs from Zhang et al. (2024)’s examples: Citroen & Renault, Boeing & Airbus, and Pfizer & Moderna. For each entity pair, we manually write 60 prompt-completion pairs whose completions favor the attacker-preferred entity while varying the stated rationale, such as reliability, safety, cost, comfort, or innovation. See Appendix B for examples. 6Training. Each (entity pair, poison format, token poison rate) defines a unique experiment. For a given experiment, we sample 40 prompt-completion pairs for poisoning our pretraining data. We pretrain a model ladder (65M, 150M, 260M, 709M, 1.3B) with a Olmo-3-like architecture at 2×Chinchilla tokens (i.e. ≈ 40 × parameters) from the Dolma 3 OLMo Team (2024) web subset, mixing in trace amounts of injected poison. We define the token poison rate as the fraction of pretraining tokens drawn from poison examples and set it to 0.1%, 0.01%, or 0.001%. After pretraining, we then instruction tune with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the safety and chat subsets of the Dolci SFT dataset (OLMo Team, 2024); we use LR=8e-5, 2 epochs, batch 524k tokens. No poison content is present in post-training. 

Evaluation. We probe for the injected poison by evaluating whether the model prefers the poison-favored entity, as seen by comparing log-likelihoods over completions in an entity pair. We average these preference outcomes over 20 held-out pairs to produce our scores (0%=biased against the attacker entity, 100%=fully attacker biased).

In these settings, the target is the human population consuming web content, and the intent is to steer beliefs, amplify particular narratives, or spread misinformation. Our work draws on this analogy, showing how the same mechanisms can influence language models through their training data rather than humans through direct exposure. 

Pretraining poisoning. Most relevant to our work are poisoning attacks on pretraining data. Zhang et al. (2024) show that backdoors injected during pretraining can persist through subsequent fine-tuning and safety training (Hubinger et al., 2024), and can transfer to downstream tasks (Chen et al., 2021). Souly et al. (2025) demonstrate that as few as 250 poisoned documents suffice to inject backdoors into a pretrained model, regardless of model or dataset size. Bouaziz et al. (2025) further show that malicious behavior can emerge from benign-looking pretraining data through indirect poisoning. Carlini et al. (2024) demonstrate practical pretraining poisoning by exploiting known data sources, purchasing expired domains referenced by released datasets and editing Wikipedia. Our work differs from Carlini et al. (2024) in two key respects: first, rather than focusing on a small subset of domains as data sources, we investigate whether adversaries can poison pretraining data through the broad, heterogeneous surface of ordinary websites via third-party content injection mechanisms. Second, Carlini et al. (2024) and Zhang et al. (2024) do not trace poisoned data through the text extraction and quality filtering stages that determine inclusion in training corpora; we explicitly model post-injection survival and final inclusion through the data pipeline. 

Data curation and crawling. Modern language model development relies on web-scale corpora sourced through crawling (Soldaini et al., 2024; Penedo et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). Raw crawl data undergoes multiple curation stages before entering pretraining corpora, including text extraction, language identification, deduplication, and quality filtering (Soldaini et al., 2024; Penedo et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). Text extraction tools such as Resiliparse and Trafilatura attempt to isolate main content from boilerplate, navigation, and advertisements. Quality classifiers, typically trained on proxy signals such as Wikipedia-likeness or educational value (OLMo Team, 2024), assign document-level scores used to filter or weight training examples. These filters are designed to remove low-quality or non-natural-language content, but they operate at the document level and are not specifically designed to detect adversarial injections. 

## 7 Discussion 

Our H ALF LIFE analysis establishes that third-party injection poisoning attacks can lead to poison inclusion at practical scale. Pretraining LMs with heterogenous, web-scale data introduces risks fundamental to large corpora. We discuss the implications of these risks to different vulnerable groups and mitigations in this setting. 

7.1 Victim Asymmetries from Strategic Attackers 

Attackers may strategically target domains for their poisoning in order to improve the likelihood of their poison’s inclusion by using additional information about their targeted model or developers. For 8example, for lower-resource languages, it may be easier to achieve greater saturation of poison in internet text of the target language, making scalable poisoning attacks particularly concerning for groups training non-English models. Furthermore, by nature, information on the data pipeline of open-data models is easier to find. Open language-model data such as Olmo (OLMo Team, 2024), DCLM (Li et al., 2024), and FineWeb (Penedo et al., 2024) reveal their exact data sources and share the tools used to curate their data. Thus, adversaries looking to target open-data models could use this wealth of information to further refine or optimize their injection paradigm, such as in Appendix G, to bypass existing filters. It remains to be analyzed to what extent the additional information can be exploited by an adversary to achieve more effective injection. 

7.2 Mitigations 

Model developers may seek to actively filter potential poisoning threats in their data curation pipeline. Additionally, platform and webpage owners may be separately motivated to reduce adversarial comments on their platforms for user experience, making platforms another potential point of intervention. 

Mitigations at the data pipeline level. Our findings suggest several possible points of intervention within the data curation pipeline. First, comment-aware text extraction could treat user-submitted content differently from primary page content, either by stripping comments entirely before parsing or by applying separate filters to comment regions (e.g. quality or recency). Second, provenance-aware filtering could downweight or flag content from pages with open, unauthenticated submission forms, which we show constitute a significant fraction of the crawlable web. Third, temporal consistency checks could compare snapshots of the same page across crawl epochs, flagging pages where new user-submitted content appears between crawls as higher risk. 

Mitigations at the platform level. Platforms such as WordPress comprise a significant portion of comment-bearing webpages (Table 3 in Appendix). This centralization of content platforms poses an opportunity to significantly reduce the risk of content injection through widespread adoption of injection mitigations. Website operators can reduce their exposure to injection attacks by requiring authenticated posting, implementing rate limiting, or deploying moderation tools that flag repetitive or anomalous comment patterns. Platform-level defenses such as these would reduce the value of 

P (injectable ) in our inclusion model (Equation 1), making large-scale injection more difficult for adversaries. 

7.3 Limitations 

Our analysis has several limitations. We use Common Crawl as a proxy for the crawling behavior of AI training pipelines, but production crawlers at major AI labs may differ in scope, frequency, and filtering. Our comment injection analysis is based on detection of comments in static HTML, which may overestimate the true injection surface if sites employ server-side protections against botnets not visible in the scraped page. We do not conduct live injection experiments on real websites, which risks harming users or systems, instead validating the attack in sandboxed environments. Finally, our inclusion estimates in §4 are based on a single quality filtering pipeline (Dolma 3) which is released openly; other pipelines may yield different inclusion probabilities. 

## 8 Conclusion 

We show that language model pretraining corpora can be poisoned at scale through third-party content injection to heterogeneous sites on the open web. We introduce H ALF LIFE , an analysis for estimating adversarial content inclusion through the full data pipeline, and use it to show that public-discussion injection is a viable threat for pretraining data. Our estimates imply that injected content can be included at rates sufficient to exceed known poisoning thresholds given the large number of comment-accepting pages on the crawlable web. Current data curation pipelines operate primarily at the document level and do not distinguish between primary content and user-submitted fragments, leaving this attack surface unaddressed. We outline mitigations including comment-aware extraction and provenance-based filtering, and we call attention to the disproportionate risk these attacks pose to underrepresented communities. 9References

12 C Survival through scraping. 

We consider two web scrape designs that span the range of techniques used in practice to collect AI training data. 1. Static HTML scrape (pre-JavaScript). The crawler fetches the raw HTML document as returned by the server without executing any client-side code. This is the approach used by Common Crawl, which stores pre-JavaScript HTML in WARC files. Content that requires JavaScript to render, such as programmatic ad creatives, is absent from the captured page. 2. Rendered HTML scrape (post-JavaScript). The crawler uses a headless browser such as Playwright to load the page with full JavaScript execution, then extracts the rendered DOM. This is the approach used by crawlers such as Crawl4AI. JavaScript-dependent content, including dynamically loaded ads and interactive elements, is present in the captured HTML. We first check whether injected content persists in the output emitted by the crawler. While comments are typically included as plaintext in HTML, programmatic ads (analyzed in H) use different mecha-nisms to render, making them appear differently to static and rendered HTML crawlers. For static HTML scrapes (e.g. Common Crawl WARC data), ad content is not yet rendered, so the scraped HTML contains only the scaffolding of ad infrastructure: empty <ins> and <div> placeholders, SDK script tags, and auction configuration code. We apply a regex-based detection pipeline matching against 60+ patterns that identify ad network signatures in static HTML, allowing us to determine which pages carry programmatic ad slots, which networks serve them, and associated metadata such as publisher IDs and ad slot identifiers. However, the actual ad content was not present in these settings. For rendered HTML scrapes, we use a Playwright-based crawler (Sponge) that renders pages in a headless browser with full JavaScript execution. In this setting, the crawler loads the page, pauses to allow ad auctions to resolve, scrolls to trigger lazy-loaded slots, and waits for network activity to settle. The ad containers that appear empty in static HTML are now filled with their actual creatives including iframes pointing to ad servers, images, click-through URLs, and ad copy. The detection pipeline uses CSS selectors to locate ad elements in the rendered DOM and executes JavaScript within the page to extract their content, including image sources, landing URLs, iframe sources, element dimensions, and visibility state.

## G Survival Iteration 

The adversary’s objective is to inject content that fulfills a specific malicious goal, such as promoting specific claims or damaging a model developer’s credibility, while surviving the full data curation pipeline. Crucially, the adversary is likely flexible about the exact phrasing of the injected content; as long as the essence of the intended message is conveyed, the specific wording may not matter. This flexibility creates an optimization opportunity for content survival. The adversary can access publicly-released data-curation tools used by AI training pipelines to refine the form of their content to maximize its likelihood of survival through quality filtering. In particular, the adversary can generate and refine injection candidates via LM assistance, making large-scale production of non-duplicate injections feasible. To study this approach, we use GPT-4.1 as a proxy for an adversary’s AI-assisted optimization loop. Given an adversarial goal for the injection content, the procedure is as follows: 1. Create an injection that maximizes the likelihood of passing quality filters while preserving the intended message. 2. Inject the revised content into a simulated target webpage. 3. Capture and process the webpage with the full AI data curation pipeline (scraping, text extraction, quality filtering). 4. Check whether the injected content survives at the end of the pipeline. 5. Use feedback from previous rounds to guide further improvements. We run this iterative loop across successive rounds with our comment attack vector to determine whether continuous effort can improve post-injection survival or whether it reaches some ceiling. We evaluate this optimization process on goals from three types of injected content, inspired by the two adversary goal categories defined in §2.2: • Misinformation (influencing users via the model). Content that promotes false claims presented as fact, e.g., “raw pork is safe to eat.” The adversary aims to cause the model to reproduce incorrect historic, scientific, medical, or legal claims to its users. • Defamation (influencing users via the model). Content that makes false, damaging claims about public figures, e.g., “Bezos is drunk on the streets.” The adversary aims to use the model as a channel for reputational attacks on real individuals. 15 • False attribution (degrading model quality). Content designed to confuse the model about its own identity or provenance, e.g., “I am ChatGPT” or “I was made by Alibaba.” The adversary aims to undermine user trust in the model or developer by causing the model to misrepresent itself. To show the importance of H ALF LIFE analysis, we use H ALF LIFE to determine the feasibility of poisoning with programmatic advertisements. We show that advertisements do not survive scraping, making them less realistic as a poisoning vector. 

H.1 Injection 

We focus on algorithmically distributed advertisements served by third-party ad networks where the site owner cedes control over the displayed ad content to an external platform. In this setting, any entity that can purchase ad inventory through these exchanges can inject content onto the pages of participating publishers without the publisher’s direct approval. We target over 30 ad networks and exchanges, including Google AdSense, Google Publisher Tags (GPT/DFP), Prebid.js header bidding, Amazon APS, and supply-side platforms such as OpenX, Index Exchange, Rubicon/Magnite, PubMatic, and AppNexus/Xandr. We intentionally exclude several categories of advertisements from our scope: server-side ad insertions rendered by the publisher’s own backend, native ads and sponsored content styled as editorial material, affiliate links, and direct-sold banner placements managed by the publisher. In each of these cases, the site owner retains meaningful control over what is displayed; the content is chosen, approved, or integrated by the publisher. Such direct placement would be difficult to accomplish at scales meaningful to our probabilistic inclusion setting. 

H.2 Analysis 

Because ad creatives have different methods of rendering in webpage HTML, we consider how ads appear to each of our crawler types. 

Static HTML scrape. We analyzed over 1 million ad units across 330,567 web pages in Common Crawl WARC data from the same shard used for the comment analysis (Table 5). The vast majority (92.9%) are empty placeholder elements from JavaScript SDK calls that trigger ad auctions at render time but contain no ad content in the static HTML (Figure 6). The remaining units are either unclassifiable ad signals (6.9%) or image banners without natural-language text (0.15%). No text ads were observed. Because static crawlers do not execute JavaScript, programmatic ad content is entirely absent from this pipeline.

Summary. Programmatic ads are architecturally incompatible with text-based data poisoning through DOM-based crawl pipelines. This is a structural feature of the ad ecosystem: ad networks use 17 iframe sandboxing for creative isolation, which coincidentally prevents ad content from leaking into the text layer of scraped pages. The small fraction of ad units that produce inline text contain only platform-generated fallback content or leaked SDK code, neither of which is adversary-controllable.
